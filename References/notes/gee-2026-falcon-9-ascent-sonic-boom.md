# Falcon 9 ascent sonic boom measurements in Ventura County, California

- **Authors:** K. L. Gee, M. S. Kellison, M. C. Anderson, L. T. Moats, M. T. Perkins, N. L. Pulsipher, N. F. Carlston, G. W. Hart (BYU Physics and Astronomy); L. K. Hall, O. Cantrell, H. J. ## Summary
Southeasterly Falcon 9 launches from Vandenberg Space Force Base (VSFB) since about 2021 have put ascent sonic booms on the Ventura County coast, which drew complaints and media coverage (p. 2–3). BYU, CSU Bakersfield and the Space Force fielded 16–25 ground-plate microphones per launch over six launches in summer 2024, for 132 recordings in all (p. 2–3). The booms were not N-waves. Near a presumed focal line they looked like a Friedlander (explosion-like) single shock lasting several seconds, with a maximum of 1.90 psf (133 dB) in Oxnard. Further downrange they were multi-arrival rumbles or thumps, mostly below 0.5 psf (p. 4–5). Five Starlink launches flew at a 144° azimuth; four of them put the focus around Ventura/Oxnard (≈34°12′–34°15′N). Starlink 9-3 (southerly low-level wind) and NROL-186 (155° azimuth) left almost no boom on land, so both weather and trajectory matter (p. 5–6). The measured map agreed only "marginally" with the PCBoom-based 2024 environmental assessment (EA) predictions. The EA placed the strongest focus near Santa Barbara/Carpinteria, where no booms were measured (p. 6–7).

## Key findings
- **Dataset** (p. 2–3, Table 1): 6 launches, 132 recordings (16–25 per launch). 94 recordings caught a boom *(own sum of Table 1)*.
- **Peak overpressure statistics** (p. 5):
  - Maximum Δp = 1.90 psf (133 dB re 20 µPa peak), at Oxnard during Starlink 9-4.
  - At stations that detected a boom, mean Δp = 0.37 psf and standard deviation 0.29 psf.
  - Of the 132 measurements, 69 (>50%) had Δp < 0.5 psf and 37 (>25%) had Δp < 0.25 psf.
  - Those low-amplitude booms "did not generate significant complaints" that summer (p. 5).
- **Waveform types** (p. 4–5, Fig. 2):
  - The booms are neither aircraft N-waves nor the M-wave (triple boom) of Falcon 9 or Starship booster flyback (Anderson & Gee 2025; Gee et al. 2024).
  - Near the focus the boom is a Friedlander-like single shock, as for Saturn V undertrack focus booms on Apollo. The authors attribute the single shock to the expanding plume, which decays gradually and has no fixed "tail", unlike an aircraft (citing Maglieri et al. 2014; p. 4).
  - Durations are several seconds (Apollo 15: ~8 s). With the impulse and initial rarefaction lasting >2 s, the spectral peak is below 0.5 Hz (≈0.4 Hz on average). Even so, the Fig. 2(a) boom is above ambient up to 2 kHz although its source is tens of km away (p. 4–5).
  - Downrange recordings are complicated multi-arrival waveforms of 0.1–0.4 psf. They are heard as a series of thumps or a rumble with a sudden onset (unlike the gradual rise of distant rocket exhaust noise), with bandwidth ≤ 100 Hz, like aircraft booms near the carpet edge (p. 5).
- **Comparisons with other low-frequency events** (p. 5):
  - Overpressures are similar to Concorde's ~0.2 psf secondary booms (5–10 s long). Long-range thunder can reach 0.13 psf but peaks at ~50–200 Hz, at least ten times higher in frequency.
  - The boom spectral peak lies below regional earthquake frequencies (2–10 Hz). The lowest resonances of 1–3 storey buildings (1–12 Hz) still mean booms can shake houses, which residents have mistaken for earthquakes.
- **Carpet structure for the 144° launches** (p. 5–6, Fig. 3):
  - For 4 of 5 launches (not 9-3), a high-Δp region sits between ≈34°12′N and 34°15′N latitude. The authors read it as the acceleration focus.
  - Well uprange of the focus: no boom. Just uprange: low-frequency transients whose arrival times and amplitudes fit prefocus evanescent waves. At the focus: Friedlander-like waves, often with signs of multiple arrivals. Downrange: more arrivals, with Δp mostly 0.25–0.5 psf.
  - Even 25 stations were "only marginally adequate" to resolve spatial trends (p. 6).
- **Weather vs trajectory** (p. 6):
  - Starlink 9-2 and 9-3 flew the same nominal trajectory and were measured at nearly the same sites. 9-2 gave booms at 21/23 stations; 9-3 at 12/23, nine of them < 0.1 psf and two more < 0.2 psf, and the boom was heard directly at one station only (≈0.57 psf).
  - VSFB weather balloons show wind from the south below ~15 km for 9-3, but from the southwest for 9-2 and the other 144° launches. Without an eastward component to push the carpet landward, the 9-3 carpet likely moved south and west, out of the array.
  - NROL-186 (155°, 5 days after 9-2): booms at only 3 stations, ≤ 0.09 psf. Its weather profile resembled 9-2, so the authors attribute the absence to the more southerly trajectory, pending PCBoom ray tracing with as-flown weather.
- **Comparison with EA predictions** (p. 6–7, Fig. 4):
  - Amplitude ranges are similar: measured 0.01–1.90 psf; predicted maxima 2.13 psf (eastern Santa Barbara County) and 2.03 psf (Ventura County). Predicted Δp in Ventura County is < 0.5 psf for 65% of cases and > 1 psf for 17%.
  - The focus locations disagree. The EA's strongest focus is near Santa Barbara, with >1 psf mostly between Carpinteria and Ventura and a low probability of focus in Ventura. The measurements put the focus at Ventura/Oxnard for 4 of 6 launches, and no booms were measured near Carpinteria.
  - Possible reasons: the EA "easterly" trajectory may not be the 144° one, and the EA's 125 two-dimensional weather profiles may not represent summer conditions. The EA itself says PCBoom suits carpet extent better than overpressure (p. 7).
- **Conclusion** (p. 7): these are the first ascent-boom measurements near the lateral cutoff region; earlier ones were near-undertrack, on ships or islands. They form a low-amplitude baseline from a period when complaints stopped. Measured overpressures in Ventura/Oxnard exceed the EA predictions.

## Setup / method
- **Launches** (p. 3, Table 1; launch site SLC-4 at VSFB, Fig. 1). The minimum distance from vehicle to measurement area is ≈80–95 km for the 144° azimuth at a representative altitude of 30 km (p. 3).
- **Sensors** (p. 3):
  - 12.7 mm (½") type 1 microphones: GRAS 46AE (~50 mV/Pa, 2.5 Hz–20 kHz) or GRAS 47AC (~8 mV/Pa, 0.1 Hz–20 kHz).
  - Sampling 51.2 kHz on 24-bit NI 9250 (−3 dB at 0.43 Hz) or NI 9234 (−3 dB at 0.5 Hz) modules, ±5 V range. At a few stations, NI 9232 (±30 V, −3 dB at 0.13 Hz) with 47AC microphones, to extend the low-frequency response and verify the cutoffs used in the filtering.
  - **Digital pole-shift filtering** (Rasband et al. 2023; Marston 2008) corrects the high-pass roll-off of the chain. This restores waveform shape for rumble-like booms with spectral peaks < 1 Hz and gives a more accurate Δp.
  - Microphones are inverted over a 30 cm resin ground plate under a weather-resistant dome windscreen (Gee et al. 2020; Anderson et al. 2022). Sites: parks, schools, municipal and military properties, private residences (Fig. 1).
- **Array evolution** (p. 6): the carpet location was unknown beforehand. The layout changed after 9-1 (fielded at 1 day's notice) and after 9-3, when near-coast stations were added; 9-2, NROL-186 and 9-3 share nearly the same sites.
- **Analysis:** time-domain peak overpressure Δp per station, mapped per launch (Fig. 3; white < 0.1 psf, black > 2.0 psf, 1 dB colour steps). Spectra are mentioned (peak frequency, bandwidth, level above ambient) but not shown, and no spectral method parameters are given. No time–frequency, intermittency or array processing is used.
- **Modelling side** (p. 6–7): the only model is the EA's PCBoom prediction (PCBoom 7; Lonzaga et al. 2025), a ray-tracing sonic boom code with focus handling, run by the Space Force for one "easterly" trajectory over 125 two-dimensional weather profiles. The authors digitised the EA map by matching pixel colours to the legend (6 dB colour steps over most of the range) and replotted it on OpenStreetMap. They warn that image compression makes it inexact (Fig. 4, p. 7). The authors ran no simulations of their own; PCBoom runs with as-flown trajectories and weather are planned future work (p. 6–7).

## Notation
- Δp: peak overpressure (psf; 1 psf ≈ 48 Pa). EA: environmental assessment (US Space Force 2024b). PCBoom: NASA/Wyle sonic boom prediction code. VSFB: Vandenberg Space Force Base. SLC-4: Space Launch Complex 4. Carpet: ground area reached by the boom; lateral cutoff: its sideline edge. Friedlander wave: blast-like waveform, a sharp rise then an exponential decay into a rarefaction.

## Relevance to this project
This is a field-measurement letter on rocket ascent booms. It is about far-field propagation, focusing and meteorology, not jet noise sources, so it is peripheral to the user's screech and impinging-jet work. The points that carry over:
- **Plume as part of the "body" that makes the boom:** the single-shock Friedlander signature is attributed to the expanding exhaust plume acting as an open-ended body (p. 4). This links the plume's shape, and so its shock-cell and expansion structure, to the boom, but the paper gives no model of it.
- **Low-frequency acoustic measurement practice:** booms peaking below 1 Hz need DAQ and microphone chains with known high-pass cutoffs and pole-shift correction to recover peak overpressure (p. 3). This matters for any rocket or large-jet acoustic data where the waveform shape and peak, not just the spectrum, are the quantity of interest. It is the same BYU hardware (ground plate, dome windscreen, 47AC/NI 9232) used in the sibling far-field notes [gee-2024-starship-super-heavy-far-field-noise.md](gee-2024-starship-super-heavy-far-field-noise.md), [mathews-2021-falcon-9-far-field-noise.md](mathews-2021-falcon-9-far-field-noise.md), [kellison-2026-falcon-9-noise-two-campaigns.md](kellison-2026-falcon-9-noise-two-campaigns.md) and [gee-2023-sls-artemis-i-far-field-noise.md](gee-2023-sls-artemis-i-far-field-noise.md).
- **Variability and intermittency:** launch-to-launch variability (9-2 vs 9-3) is set by wind below ~15 km, not by the source. Separating source and propagation variability is the same problem as in far-field launch-noise statistics (cf. [kellison-2026-falcon-9-noise-two-campaigns.md](kellison-2026-falcon-9-noise-two-campaigns.md)). The analysis here is peak-only and per event. No time–frequency, wavelet or Hilbert analysis is done, so nothing feeds the SPOD → reconstruction → HPOD pipeline directly. Each boom is a single transient, and only the time-domain waveform matters.
- **Methods vs data type:** everything here needs time-resolved pressure records (waveform, peak, spectrum). No modal decomposition is relevant: the 16–25 stations spread over tens of km are too sparse even for the spatial maps (p. 6).
- **Model–experiment comparison:** the PCBoom comparison is qualitative (digitised map, different trajectory and weather ensemble). It shows how far a ray-tracing propagation model on a statistical weather ensemble can miss focus location, even when it gets the amplitude range right (p. 6–7). This is a reminder to match model inputs (trajectory, atmosphere) to the as-run case before judging a model, as with the LES and stability comparisons elsewhere in the library (e.g. [bres-2021-les-twin-rectangular-jets-plasma.md](bres-2021-les-twin-rectangular-jets-plasma.md)).
- **Related works cited:** Anderson & Gee 2025a,b (Falcon 9 booster triple boom), Rasband et al. 2023 (low-frequency digital filtering), Maglieri et al. 2014 (sonic boom review), Lonzaga et al. 2025 (PCBoom 7), Hilton et al. 1972 / Henderson & Hilton 1974 (Apollo ascent booms). These are candidates for `/add-paper` only if propagation becomes a focus.
