## Summary
The authors map the discrete tones of a cold, choked 8 mm round jet impinging on circular plates of 0.5–38 nozzle diameters, over NPR 2.02–4.75 and nozzle-to-plate spacings of about 0.5–8 diameters (p. 309–312). Below NPR 2.70 only "large plate" tones occur, for every plate size. They fall on three parallel lines, L1, L2 and L3, on log–log plots of λ/d against h/d, and stage as spacing grows, merging into choked-jet screech at the largest spacings (p. 311–316). From NPR 3.04 up a second class, "small plate" tones (S4, S5) with steeper slopes, appears for small plates (p. 316–318). Multiple tones are the rule, and at one condition shortening the spectral average shows the jet switching at random between two lines (p. 312, 318). A reflector placed a chosen fraction of a wavelength behind the nozzle exit suppresses or promotes tones of both classes, so both loops close at the nozzle, contrary to Powell's earlier view of the small-plate tones (p. 318–319). Two-microphone cross-spectra at the nozzle exit identify L1 as symmetrical and L2 and L3 as helical, and single-spark schlieren shows asymmetric disturbances and moving cell shocks for large plates, but only symmetric disturbances and large stand-off-shock oscillations for small plates (p. 319–323). The only model is Powell's (1988) feedback equation, which counts 2–8 cycles in the loop and fits most data near integer N (p. 313–315).

## Key findings
- **Two classes of tone** (p. 307, 311, 316):
  - Large-plate tones exist at all NPR tested. Small-plate tones appear only from NPR 3.04 upward (p. 316). Nothing was tested between 2.70 and 3.04, so the onset lies somewhere in that interval.
  - "Large" plates are those larger than about 4d (Powell 1988, cited on p. 308).
  - For NPR ≤ 2.70, only large-plate tones occur for every plate size, although tones are much weaker for the smallest plates (p. 325).
- **Four spacing regions at low NPR** (Fig. 4, p. 312; boundaries read from figure at NPR 2.36):
  - *Very small* spacings (h/d ≲ 1.1): lines with gentler slopes that steepen towards the small-spacing region; tones quite weak (p. 312).
  - *Small* spacings (≈1.1–3.0): very active, usually three parallel lines L1, L2, L3, with multiple tones "the rule rather than the exception" (p. 312). The dominant tone could not always be predicted from run to run at nominally identical conditions (p. 312).
  - *Intermediate* spacings (≈3.0–5.0): the lines continue with staging. Each stage starts with a sudden jump in frequency and then falls continuously as h/d grows. Some stages sit around the choked-jet screech frequency, as Norum (1989) saw for a rectangular impinging jet (p. 312).
  - *Large* spacings (≳ 5.0): the stages merge into choked-jet screech (p. 312).
- **Jump sizes depend on NPR** (Figs 4–5, p. 312–313): at NPR 2.70 the L1 line jumps from 4 to 6 cycles in the loop, at NPR 2.36 from 4 to 5. The authors suggest a change in the preferred screech mode near these NPR (p. 313). Fig. 5 (NPR 2.70) labels the jump ratios 4:6, 6:7 and 7:8 on one branch and 5:6 and 6:7 on another (read from figure). The actual jump positions varied "somewhat randomly" (p. 312). Which line dominates also depends on NPR (p. 313).
- **Plate size** (p. 314):
  - It changes the dominant tone and the jump positions.
  - The smallest plate (D/d = 0.5) gives only choked-jet screech at the lowest NPR (2.02 and 2.36).
  - Intermediate plates (D/d = 1 and 2) often greatly reduce tonal activity.
- **Powell's equation counts the cycles** (Figs 7–8, p. 314–315). With Powell's constants (p = 0, M_con,u = 0.73, h″/d = 0.8), most data fall near integer N:
  - NPR 2.36: L2 first stage N ≈ 3; L1 stages ≈ 4, 5, then ≈ 5.5–6 (the L1 third stage is the clearest non-integer case); L3 stages ≈ 5, 6, 7, 8.
  - NPR 2.70: L2 ≈ 3; L1 stages ≈ 4, 6, 7, 8; L3 first stage ≈ 5 (values read from figures).
  - Residual trends: N falls slightly with h/d below h/d ≈ 4, is flat near 4 and rises above ≈ 4.5. The authors attribute this to convection speeds that change with where the plate sits in the cell structure, or to the influence of choked-jet screech (p. 314).
- **Powell's single mode was several modes** (Fig. 9, p. 314–315): Powell (1988) had read the data on the L1, L2 and L3 lines as stages III, IV and V of one mode, with N = 2–8. He used a frequency counter, which sees only tones about 10 dB above any other. With an FFT analyser, the stages turn out to belong partly to different modes, perhaps like the symmetrical, asymmetrical and helical screech modes of Umeda et al. (p. 315).
- **Intensity and receptivity** (Fig. 10, p. 315–316; NPR 2.70, ≈ 45° from the upstream axis, ≈ 42d from the plate centre):
  - At small spacings all three lines first grow and then decay, at comparable levels, so all tones are likely to appear.
  - With increasing spacing one mode (L1 here) grows to a maximum and then decays while another (L3) grows. Once the first dominant mode has fallen substantially, a wavelength is added to the loop, giving a sudden jump in both frequency and intensity, near h/d ≈ 3.5 for L1. The cycle repeats (second L1 jump near h/d ≈ 4.6, read from figure). This resembles classical edgetone staging, which has only one mode (p. 316).
  - Near jump positions the dying stage and the next one are both weak (e.g. L3 at h/d = 4.5). The authors call this an apparent "receptivity" to other modes and suggest the jet may oscillate between modes there (p. 316).
- **Random switching seen directly** (p. 318): at NPR 3.04, D/d = 4 and h/d = 3.8 the tone sounded very unsteady.
  - With 32 spectral averages there were two peaks.
  - With 2 averages, some spectra showed a 20 kHz tone and others a 14.25 kHz tone, within one run. The time between switches appeared random.
  - The switch is between the L1 and L2 lines, near an L1 jump position, which is where the NPR 2.70 intensity study found the jet most receptive.
  - The authors leave open whether multiple tones in long averages are simultaneous modes or switching; this "was not investigated" (p. 318).
- **High-NPR behaviour** (p. 316):
  - Above NPR 3.04 the L3 line stops for several large plates, the intermediate-spacing tones stop, and choked-jet screech falls sharply.
  - This fits preliminary free-jet experiments in the same apparatus, which found **no screech above NPR 3.79** (p. 316).
  - The conclusions say that "only the L2 and L3 lines persisted" at the highest NPR (p. 325), which contradicts §3.1.2 and Fig. 11, where L1 and L2 remain at NPR 4.40 (see verify item 1).
- **Small-plate tones** (Figs 11–12, p. 316–318):
  - They first appear at NPR 3.04 and grow more common with NPR. There are two types, S4 and S5, with different and steeper slopes than the L lines.
  - At the lowest NPR of this range the smallest plates show both classes. At NPR 4.40 and 4.74 the D/d = 1 and 0.5 plates show only small-plate tones. Intermediate plates show both, over a range of plate sizes that depends on NPR (p. 316–317).
  - Comparison with Powell (1988): his S4 tones first appear near NPR 3.38, and he reported none at 3.04, perhaps because he did not test small plates there (p. 317). His small-plate tones occurred only in the pressure-recovery part of each cell (the downstream half), which is why they were linked to high harmonics of the Hartmann whistle (p. 308, 318). Here one or another small-plate tone occurs almost continuously from h/d ≈ 1.6 to 3.5 (NPR 4.40), and only S5 stays confined to the pressure-recovery regions (p. 318).
- **Reflector tests: both loops close at the nozzle** (p. 318–319):
  - Large plate (D/d = 38, NPR 4.40, h/d = 1.72): the L1 tone at 28.51 kHz is present without the reflector and extension.
    - With the reflector at 2.5λ behind the extension exit, the same tone appears.
    - At 2.25λ the tone is **completely suppressed**, and a 19.96 kHz tone (weak without the reflector at h/d = 1.70) takes its place. The authors say the reflector position corresponds to 2λ for this tone, so the pressure should be reinforced at the exit.
    - The authors read this as strong support for feedback to the nozzle (p. 319).
  - Small plate (D/d = 2, NPR 4.47, h/d = 2.92): a reflector at 0.75λ **completely suppresses** the 11.25 kHz S4 tone. S5 tones were only reduced, not cancelled (p. 319).
  - So, contrary to Powell's (1988) suggestion that small-plate feedback is confined to the stand-off region, the small-plate loop also involves the nozzle (p. 319).
- **Azimuthal modes from cross-spectra** (p. 322–323):
  - L1 is symmetrical, and L2 and L3 are helical.
  - The phases never matched the expected values exactly, unlike Umeda et al., and changed when h/d changed by as little as "0·6" (as printed) on the same line.
  - Example (L2, D/d = 38, NPR 2.70, 60° separation): measured −48.7° at h/d = 0.74 (16.203 kHz), corrected to −57.0°; measured −41.7° at h/d = 0.76 (16.039 kHz), corrected to −50.0°. The 7° spread for one mode is called "a significant discrepancy", yet the indication that the mode is helical is "strong" (p. 323).
- **Schlieren, large plates** (Figs 14–17, p. 319–322; NPR 2.70, D/d 38):
  - Most photographs show a prominent asymmetric disturbance pattern (Fig. 14, h/d 2.15). Assuming a convection speed of 0.6 of the fully expanded value, the authors match its scale to the L3 tone. A single plane cannot tell asymmetric from helical (p. 319).
  - Where L1 could be isolated (Fig. 15, h/d 2.47), the disturbance is "quite symmetrical" with some asymmetric parts, perhaps from L3, which is present at neighbouring h/d (p. 319).
  - The shocks of the cell structure move: dark expanding regions behind a shock displace streamwise (Fig. 16, h/d 2.34) or cross-stream, distorting the shock ahead (Fig. 17, h/d 2.15). In both cases the shock motion seems tied to the passage of flow disturbances (p. 320–321).
  - Even when the dominant tone is the symmetrical L1, the disturbances are never purely symmetrical, and photographs at one condition differ irregularly (p. 324).
  - At very small spacing (Fig. 18, NPR 4.06, h/d 1.46) no jet disturbances are evident. The wavefronts are centred further out on the plate in the wall jet, not on the impingement region, and several large disturbances sit on the plate. At larger spacings the fronts appear to originate in the impingement region (p. 321–322).
- **Schlieren, small plates** (Fig. 19, p. 322–323; NPR 4.06, D/d 1, h/d 1.65, an S4 tone): disturbances, when visible, are always symmetrical, implying toroidal instabilities, as Powell (1988) found. The **stand-off shock oscillates streamwise by up to d/3**. The oscillation is confined to the stand-off shock (the cell shocks do not distort as for large plates) and does not seem tied to the passage of disturbances (p. 322).
- **Inconsistencies discussed by the authors** (§4.1, p. 323–324):
  - Dominance changed unpredictably; no position gave one predictable dominant tone, which may explain the scatter in the schlieren and cross-spectra.
  - Multiple modes with large, comparable amplitudes might interact nonlinearly to give sum and difference tones. These would not be true modes, so their cross-spectral phase could mislead.
  - In the initial free-jet screech experiments the **spin direction of the helical mode changed randomly** during continuous running, whereas Umeda et al. found it could change between runs but was stable once started. A spin change within the averaging time would give a phase that does not match the microphone separation, although "the coherence function could remain large" (p. 324).
- **Proposed small-plate loop** (§4.2, p. 324): disturbances convect down the jet and displace the stand-off shock. The displaced shock emits a pressure pulse that reflects from the plate, meets the shock again, distorts it and possibly produces sound outside the jet. In Powell's large-plate loop the disturbances instead strike the plate directly. The new loop resembles Glaznev et al.'s (1977) model for very highly expanded jets. The slopes differ for the two classes, so the loop length must change with h/d differently in each case (p. 324).
- **Open at the end** (p. 325): whether the shock oscillations are a necessary part of either loop. Future work was to test this for large plates, and to use more schlieren to find the origin of the small-plate sound.

## Setup / method
- **Flow supply** (§2, p. 309): compressed air at about p₀/p_a = 6.44 through a regulator. Nozzle-exit pressure ratio 2.02–4.75, with p₀ the stagnation and p_a the ambient pressure.
  - Muffler 1.8 m long: a 7.6 cm perforated pipe inside a 20.3 cm pipe, with absorbent in the annulus, to attenuate regulator noise.
  - An elbow (lab space was limited); swirl reduced by a honeycomb of 2–3 mm straws 6.4 cm long; two 50-mesh screens; a 1.5 m settling chamber of 7.6 cm pipe.
  - Stagnation temperature from a thermocouple in the settling-chamber wall: 22–23 °C. Anechoic-chamber temperature measured after each run, usually about 22 °C on average. So the jet is cold (TR ≈ 1).
- **Nozzle** (p. 309): axisymmetric convergent, exit diameter d = 0.8 cm, **lip thickness 2.25 mm** (t/d ≈ 0.28), interior contour of cubic arcs after Morel (1975). The outer lip shape is not described.
- **Plates and chamber** (p. 310): circular plates of D/d = 0.5–38 (0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4 and 38 in the Fig. 4 legend), on a computer-controlled traverse along the jet axis with steps as small as 0.0254 mm. Nozzle and plate sit in an anechoic chamber of 2.4 m × 3.0 m × 3.0 m.
- **Acoustics** (p. 310–311):
  - 3.175 mm B&K type 4138 microphones; Ono Sokki CF 920 FFT analyser (0–100 kHz span in Fig. 3). Frequency resolution, frame length and window are not stated; 32 averages are mentioned (p. 318).
  - Microphones at about 1/2 m from the plate centre, at jet-axis height, 30–45° from the upstream jet direction measured from the nozzle exit (Fig. 2). The intensity study was at ≈ 45° and ≈ 42d (≈ 0.34 m) (p. 315), which does not quite match "1/2 m" (verify item 5).
  - Levels come from the power spectrum (r.m.s. per frequency). Only fundamentals were plotted unless a harmonic relation was uncertain. Tones less than 7 dB above the local broadband were dropped; most were 15 dB or more above it (p. 310–311).
  - Frequencies were converted to wavelengths with the measured ambient sound speed and plotted as λ/d against h/d on log–log axes with the λ/d axis **inverted** (frequency increases upwards) (p. 311).
- **Cross-spectral phase** (§3.4, p. 322–323): two microphones at the nozzle exit, 60°, 90° or 180° apart in azimuth.
  - Calibration: the microphones could not be placed accurately without adding reflecting surfaces, so a low-speed edgetone, assumed asymmetric, was used. Its measured phases were corrected to 180° (180° separation) or 0° (60° separation). The correction was converted to a path-length offset with the edgetone wavelength and then applied, as a phase, at each jet tone's wavelength.
  - Expected for 60° separation: 60° for a helical mode, 0° for symmetrical or asymmetrical modes.
  - Both legs are averaged spectral estimates from time-resolved signals. The number of averages for the phase data is not stated.
- **Reflector test** (§3.2, p. 318–319): a thin nozzle extension with a very gradual outer slope, to minimise reflecting surfaces in the exit plane, plus a movable circular reflector 0.30 m in diameter mounted on the extension. Reflector positions were chosen so that the plate's sound and its reflection cancel at the exit, ideally at nλ/4 behind the extension exit with n odd; diffraction at the plate edges means cancellation need not be exact there.
- **Schlieren** (p. 310): single-pass conventional system outside the chamber, looking through replaceable windows. A remotely triggered stroboscope gives a single spark; spherical mirrors of 1.524 m focal length; 35 mm film in a Nikon N2000 body (printed "Nikkon"). Dark and light regions are positive and negative axial density gradients (p. 321), so the knife edge was presumably normal to the jet axis (my inference). Each photograph is a single, unconditioned snapshot: not time-resolved, not phase-locked, path-integrated.
- **Modelling side:** there is no simulation, stability analysis or new model. The model content is:
  - Powell's (1988) feedback equation for large-plate tones (eqs. 1–2), used with his fitted constants to count cycles (p. 313–315).
  - The Smith & Powell (1964) Hartmann-whistle model, in which stand-off-shock oscillations are triggered by periodic filling and emptying of a cavity when the shock sits in the pressure-recovery region. The recirculation bubble on the plate was speculated to act like the Hartmann whistle's dividing streamline (p. 308).
  - Glaznev et al.'s (1977) loop for very highly expanded jets, which the new small-plate loop resembles (p. 308–309, 324).
  - The authors give no test of how well the counts fit, beyond "reasonably well" (p. 314).
- **Literature reviewed** (p. 307–309):
  - Powell's edgetone feedback and staging, and his screech model of fixed, regularly spaced shock sources adding at the nozzle exit.
  - Umeda et al. (symmetrical, asymmetrical and helical screech modes; multiple tones, one usually dominant); Davies & Oldfield (1962; jet oscillating between modes).
  - Marsh (1961), first plate tones; Ho & Nosseir (1981), feedback; Didden & Ho (1985), counter-rotating wall-jet vortices, with symmetric oscillations.
  - Norum (1989) and Krothapalli (1985) on rectangular impinging jets: staging around the screech frequency, and screech coexisting with impinging tones.
  - The Soviet "very highly expanded" jet work (Golubkov et al. 1974; Semiletenko et al. 1974; Ginzburg et al. 1975): Mach-disk oscillation periodic at the plate-pressure frequency, ceasing when a second compression shock forms ahead of the baffle and the first cell is undisturbed; and a weaker secondary instability.

## Key equations
Retyped from a 300 dpi crop of p. 314.

**Powell's feedback equation for large plates** (eq. 1, p. 314), as printed:
$$\frac{h-s}{(u_{con,u})_{av}}+\frac{s}{(u_{con,d})_{av}}+\frac{s}{c_0}+\frac{h-s}{c_0}=N+p$$
- h: nozzle-to-plate spacing; s: stand-off distance (shock to plate, Fig. 6); (u_con,u)_av and (u_con,d)_av: mean disturbance convection speeds upstream of the stand-off shock and in the subsonic stand-off region; c₀: ambient sound speed; N: integer; p < 1.
- As printed, the left side is a time and the right side a number. The frequency f, listed among the inputs in the text, must multiply the left side: $f\,[\dots]=N+p$ (my correction).

**Wavelength form** (eq. 2, p. 314):
$$\frac{h+h''}{\lambda}\left(1+\frac{1}{M_{con,u}}\right)=N+p,\qquad M_{con,u}=\frac{(u_{con,u})_{av}}{c_0}$$
- h″ is the extra distance needed in an equivalent loop if the disturbance wavelength in the stand-off region equals that upstream of it.
- Check (mine): with f = c₀/λ, eq. 2 equals f × eq. 1 exactly when $h''=s\,(1/M_{con,d}-1/M_{con,u})/(1+1/M_{con,u})$. So h″ absorbs the slow transit through the stand-off region, and also its share of the acoustic return.
- Powell's fit: p = 0, M_con,u = 0.73, h″/d = 0.8 for 2 ≤ N ≤ 8 (p. 314). Check (mine): N = 4 at h/d = 2 gives λ/d = (2 + 0.8)(1 + 1/0.73)/4 = 1.66, against ≈ 1.6 for the L1 line at h/d = 2 in Fig. 5 (read from figure).
- Slopes (mine): at fixed N, eq. 2 gives $\mathrm d\ln\lambda/\mathrm d\ln h = h/(h+h'')<1$. Read from Fig. 11, the L lines have slopes ≈ 0.8, S4 ≈ 1.2–1.3 and S5 ≈ 2. With h″ > 0 and constant convection speed, eq. 2 cannot give a slope above 1, which supports the authors' remark that the loop length must vary differently for small-plate tones (p. 324).

**Reflector condition** (p. 319): cancellation at the exit for a reflector distance $L=n\lambda/4$, n odd; reinforcement for $L$ a multiple of λ/2. As used: 2.25λ (n = 9) cancels, 2.5λ reinforces, 0.75λ (n = 3) cancels.
- Check (mine): 2.25λ at 28.51 kHz is 27.2 mm (c₀ ≈ 344 m/s), which is 1.58λ at 19.96 kHz, not the "2λ" printed. The reflected path 2L ≈ 3.15λ is still close to reinforcing (verify item 3).

**Cross-spectral phase expectation** (p. 323; my notation): for an azimuthal mode m and microphones Δθ apart, Δφ = mΔθ. Helical (m = 1) at Δθ = 60° gives 60°; symmetrical (m = 0) gives 0°.

## Notation
- d: nozzle exit diameter (8 mm). D: plate diameter. h: nozzle-to-plate spacing. s: stand-off distance. **In Fig. 9 (Powell's data) the letters swap: D is the nozzle and d the plate.**
- p₀/p_a: nozzle pressure ratio (stagnation over ambient). λ = c₀/f: acoustic wavelength from the measured ambient sound speed.
- L1, L2, L3: large-plate tone lines (L1 symmetrical, L2 and L3 helical by cross-spectrum). S4, S5: small-plate tone groups.
- N + p: number of cycles in the loop, N integer, p < 1. u_con,u, u_con,d: mean convection speeds upstream and downstream of the stand-off shock. M_con,u = u_con,u/c₀. h″: equivalent extra loop length (eq. 2).
- "Symmetrical" = toroidal (m = 0); "asymmetrical" = planar flapping; "helical" = spinning m = 1. The paper uses "stand-off shock" for the normal shock in front of the plate; the Soviet papers call it the Mach disk in front of the plate (p. 308–309). It is tagged `Mach-disk` here.
- M_j, U_j, D_j and St = f d/U_j appear only in this note's own estimates; the paper uses λ/d and never quotes Strouhal numbers.

## Relevance to this project
Impinging supersonic jets are one of the lab's core resonance problems (CLAUDE.md), and this paper is the classical map of their tones: two classes, several azimuthal modes, staging and random switching. [Henderson 2002](henderson-2002-flow-structure-supersonic-impinging-jets.md) takes its first-leg convection Mach number (0.7–0.75) from Powell (1988) and this paper (p. 5), and its "L1, previously shown to be axisymmetric" label (p. 3) almost certainly refers to the cross-spectra here (its ref. 11 is not identified in that note). It is also the first evidence in the library that a supersonic impinging jet switches between loops at random.

**Reading it critically**
- **The reflector test shows the loop needs the external field at the lip, not that the upstream wave travels outside the jet.** A reflector behind the exit only changes the sound field outside the jet at the lip. Suppressing a tone (p. 319) therefore shows that external sound at the lip matters to the loop's gain. It cannot by itself rule out a guided-jet-mode return in parallel, since the lip responds to both. This is the same limit as the thesis foam tests ([Weightman 2019](weightman-2019-sound-production-impinging-supersonic-jets.md), p. 74–82), and [Nogueira 2024](nogueira-2024-guided-jet-waves.md) (p. 31–33) explains when each path can exist. Note too that the reflector **switched** the tone (28.51 → 19.96 kHz) rather than merely silencing the jet: the reflector selects between loops, as reflectors and lip thickness do for screech ([Raman 1998](raman-1998-supersonic-jet-screech-review.md), p. 75–78; [Ponton 1992](ponton-1992-nozzle-exit-lip-thickness-plume-resonance.md)).
- **Mode identification is weak.** The cross-spectral phases never reached the expected values, varied by 7° between h/d 0.74 and 0.76 on one line, and depend on an edgetone calibration of microphone position (p. 323). The authors' spin-reversal explanation has a testable consequence they did not use (my calculation): averaging equal amounts of +60° and −60° gives a 0° phase with γ² ≈ 0.25, and an 80:20 mix gives ≈ 46° with γ² ≈ 0.5. So "the coherence function could remain large" (p. 324) holds only if one spin dominates, and the measured |Δφ| of 42–49° (before correction) would fit roughly 80:20 mixing. Coherence values were not reported, which leaves this untested. An azimuthal ring of three or more microphones would separate m = +1 from m = −1 block by block.
- **The L2 example may be screech.** The helical "L2" tones at h/d 0.74–0.76 sit on the NPR 2.70 screech wavelength (see the Data tables note). If so, the strongest "helical L2" evidence is evidence about free-jet screech near the plate.
- **Powell's equation is under-determined.** Three fitted constants (p, M_con,u, h″) and a free integer per stage fit "most" data, with residual trends attributed to unmeasured convection-speed changes (p. 314). This is the same looseness found in the thesis's Powell checks (topic page, "The feedback equation and the phase lag"). The slope argument above (my analysis) is a sharper test: no constant-parameter version of eq. 2 gives the steep S-tone slopes.
- **Internal inconsistencies to keep in mind:** the conclusions' "only the L2 and L3 lines persisted" against Fig. 11's L1 and L2 (p. 316–317, 325); the reflector "2λ" (p. 319); h/d 1.72 in the text against ≈ 1.9–2.0 for the reflector marker and for 28.51 kHz on the L1 line in Fig. 11 (my reading); and microphone distances of 1/2 m against 42d (p. 310, 315).

**Agreements and contradictions with other notes**
- *Ho & Nosseir* ([Ho 1981](ho-1981-dynamics-impinging-jet-feedback-phenomenon.md), p. 134–135): the same diagnosis of switching. A long average shows two tones, short windows show one at a time, at a stage boundary. Here it is 32 against 2 averages at an L1 jump (p. 318); there 2.4 s against 0.061 s. Both find switching near jumps, and neither measures dwell times. Here it is also supersonic, multi-modal (different azimuthal modes, not just neighbouring N) and plate-size dependent.
- *Tam & Ahuja* ([Tam 1990](tam-1990-theoretical-model-discrete-tone-impinging-jets.md), p. 70–71, 82): at supersonic M_j both the (0, 1) axisymmetric and (1, 1) helical upstream neutral waves fall in the allowed band, so both axisymmetric and helical resonance should occur. That matches L1 (symmetrical) coexisting with L2/L3 (helical) here at M_j ≈ 1.2–1.6 (my link; the paper does not discuss guided waves). Whether the L lines fall inside the respective neutral-wave bands has not been checked.
- *Henderson 2002* ([henderson-2002](henderson-2002-flow-structure-supersonic-impinging-jets.md)): it takes over the L1 nomenclature, the "axisymmetric" L1 label (p. 3, probably from this paper's cross-spectra) and M_con ≈ 0.7–0.75 for the first leg (p. 5). Its large-plate source in the wall jet at r ≈ 1.3d (NPR 4.03, h/d 2.02) sits uneasily with this paper's statement that, beyond very small spacings, wavefronts "appear to originate in the impingement region" (p. 322; NPR 2.70 photographs). Both are visual judgements from path-integrated single images, at different NPR. Its small/large definition (≤ 2d against ≥ 4d) matches the plate sizes here.
- *The user's thesis* ([Weightman 2019](weightman-2019-sound-production-impinging-supersonic-jets.md)):
  - The lip here is t/d ≈ 0.28, close to the thesis's intermediate lip (t/D 0.33), which behaves like the infinite lip and gives both toroidal and helical tones (p. 73–79). Here, too, symmetrical (L1) and helical (L2, L3) tones coexist. A thick-lipped 0.5d nozzle gave only axisymmetric L1 tones in Henderson 2002. So the three nozzles form a lip-thickness sequence, consistent with the thesis's acoustic-path explanation (my synthesis; the outer nozzle shape here is not described).
  - The reflector tests are the 1993 analogue of the thesis foam tests: both show that the external acoustic path to the nozzle exterior controls the loop.
  - The large-plate schlieren shows cell shocks displaced as disturbances pass (p. 320–321), the picture behind stand-off-shock and cell-shock leakage in the thesis (Ch. 6) and in [Edgington-Mitchell 2021](edgington-mitchell-2021-generation-screech-tones-shock-leakage.md).
- *Edgington-Mitchell 2025* ([edgington-mitchell-2025](edgington-mitchell-2025-vortices-shocks-nonlinear-waves-impinging-jet-resonance.md)): its finding that several sources act within one cycle, and that some measured tones are by-products (p. 24–25), is a mechanism-level version of this paper's worry that some tones are nonlinear sum and difference products, not modes (p. 324). Its shocklet regime (NPR ≥ 4.5, z/D ≤ 4) overlaps the small-plate NPR range only for large plates; small-plate jets have no wall jet to carry a shocklet.
- *Screech staging* (topic page; [Mancinelli 2019](mancinelli-2019-screech-prediction-upstream-modes.md), p. 6–7): mutual exclusion with dwell times of seconds near A1/A2 is the free-jet counterpart of the random L1/L2 switching here. Two of this paper's side results bear on free-jet staging: the random reversal of helical spin direction in free-jet screech on this rig (p. 324), and the absence of screech above NPR 3.79 (p. 316). The latter is lower than many rigs, which supports the topic page's claim that staging limits belong to the rig.
- *Kuo & Dowling 1996* (cited in the Henderson 2002 note, p. 2–3): a one-dimensional shock–plate model with entropy waves matches small-plate tones. That fits this paper's proposed small-plate loop through the stand-off shock (p. 324). But the reflector result here shows that such a model also needs coupling to the nozzle.

**Methods and data for the lab workflow (CLAUDE.md)**
- **Time-resolved point acoustics:** everything here is block-averaged FFT spectra and cross-spectra. That is enough to map tone lines, but cannot tell coexistence from switching, which the authors flag as untested (p. 318). The fixes are cheap with modern records:
  - spectrograms or short-time spectra over long records, as Ho 1981 did;
  - Hilbert envelopes of band-passed tones and their joint PDFs (anti-correlated envelopes indicate switching);
  - bicoherence to test the sum/difference-tone hypothesis (p. 324);
  - an azimuthal microphone ring decomposed into m = 0, ±1 per block, which resolves the spin direction and its reversals, and gives a coherence-based mode identification without edgetone calibration.
- **Non-time-resolved imaging:** the single-spark, unconditioned schlieren gives qualitative symmetry only and is ambiguous between flapping and helical (p. 319). For the group's PIV the analogue is a POD pair with a phase portrait ([Weightman 2019](weightman-2019-sound-production-impinging-supersonic-jets.md)). When two modes coexist or alternate, as here, POD pairs mix them, and a ring in the phase portrait cannot tell which state each snapshot came from. Conditioning snapshots on a simultaneous microphone's short-time spectrum would.
- **Time-resolved schlieren and the SPOD → reconstruction → HPOD pipeline:** this paper's conditions (several tones, random switching near jumps, spin reversal) are the use case for that pipeline:
  1. SPOD of long high-speed schlieren records at each tone, with the k_x sign separating upstream and downstream waves.
  2. Reconstruction of each tone band.
  3. HPOD in time for instantaneous amplitude and frequency of each loop.

  Anti-correlated amplitudes would show switching, and simultaneous non-zero amplitudes with a phase-locked sum or difference tone would show nonlinear coupling. Block-averaged SPOD alone would reproduce this paper's ambiguity.
- **Plate-size sweeps** need no new diagnostics and give the most discriminating data. The small-plate class appears only for D/d ≲ 2–4 and NPR ≥ 3.04, and has its own slopes. The lab's cylinder and hemisphere surfaces (thesis; Edgington-Mitchell 2025) sit between "small" and "large" in this sense.

**Modelling side and the PyFR staging LES**
- The paper offers no simulation. Its proposed small-plate loop (disturbance → stand-off-shock displacement → pressure pulse → plate reflection → shock distortion → external sound; p. 324) and the question of whether shock oscillation is needed in either loop (p. 325) are natural LES targets. An impinging LES with a small plate could attribute sources directly.
- For the PyFR free-jet staging LES (`Simulations/PyFR_Staging_Continuation/`), two side results matter. Helical spin direction can reverse at random in a screeching jet (p. 324), so an LES helical state need not be one steady chirality, and azimuthal Fourier analysis should keep m = +1 and m = −1 separate. And rig-specific screech limits (none above NPR 3.79 here) caution against comparing LES staging NPR with any single experiment.
- Linear models: the L1 (m = 0) and L2/L3 (m = 1) families invite a check against the axisymmetric and helical guided-jet-mode bands of [Tam 1990](tam-1990-theoretical-model-discrete-tone-impinging-jets.md) and [Nogueira 2024](nogueira-2024-guided-jet-waves.md) at each NPR, which would say whether a guided return is possible for each line. The cycle counts N of Figs 7–8 could be recomputed with a vortex-sheet phase criterion in place of Powell's constant speeds.

**Rocket noise**
- Launch pads and deflectors are "large plates" in this sense, and the small-plate stand-off-shock oscillation is the moderate-NPR relative of the Soviet very-highly-expanded baffle instabilities (p. 308–309), which are rocket-relevant. Full-scale pads show no tones ([Lubert 2022](lubert-2022-supersonic-jet-noise-launch-vehicles-review.md), p. 16–20). This paper's finding that tones need feedback to the nozzle, and that a reflector at the right distance kills them, points at the nozzle-region acoustic environment (vehicle base, launch mount) as part of the explanation, alongside the heated, overexpanded plume and inclined deflectors (my link).
