## Summary

This is an experimental study of every link in the aeroacoustic feedback loop of underexpanded jets impinging on flat plates and a convex half-cylinder (NPR 3.4–3.6, z/D = 3.5–5) (p. i).

- **Methods:** acoustics, ultra-high-speed (UHS) schlieren and non-time-resolved planar PIV. Snapshot POD of the PIV picks out the resonant mode pair, and its phase portrait gives quasi-time-resolved phase cycles.
- **The "shear thickness mode" (Ch. 3):** a low-energy, unpaired POD mode that tracks how the pair's amplitude modulates the shear-layer thickness.
- **Nozzle geometry selects the mode (Ch. 4):** changing only the nozzle's external geometry switches the dominant mode from helical (thin lip) to toroidal (thick lip).
  - Foam covers show the cause is the **acoustic feedback path**, not entrainment.
  - The thin-lip helical loop closes **only** by reflection off the nozzle's outer base.
- **Two tone-production mechanisms:**
  1. **Shock leakage at the standoff shock** (Ch. 6). Ray tracing on POD-reconstructed PIV agrees with schlieren and has no phase delay.
  2. **The "shocklet"** (Ch. 7). A transient wall-jet shock whose delay between inception and emission explains Powell's phase lag *p*. Including it brings a fully measured feedback equation to within 0.8% of the tone frequency.

**How the chapters cover the four loop components** (p. 11–21):

## Key findings

### Ch. 1: literature framework (pp. 1–21)

**Impinging-jet flow**
- Adds a standoff shock and a wall jet to the free-jet shock cells (p. 1).
- **Zones of silence** occur when the shocks sit at their free-jet positions (Henderson 2002, 2005; p. 4).
- **Standoff-shock oscillation as a source:**
  - Powell (1988): the shock moves by up to 0.35D and changes shape from disc-like to conical.
  - Kuo & Dowling (1996): linear stability shows the oscillation also makes entropy waves.
  - Hirata et al. (1971): the shock is unstable when the plate sits in a decelerating region (p. 3–4).
- **Convex cylinder:** fixes the axis of a flapping mode (Mason-Smith et al. 2015). A concave surface often gives no tone (p. 2–3).

**Feedback model**
- Powell's timing equation (eq. 1.1) and gain criterion (eq. 1.2). The loop with the largest gain–efficiency product wins, whatever its path (p. 5–6).
- Mitchell et al. (2012) found no measurable delay at the lip, so the phase lag *p* is placed at the source. Ch. 7 targets this term.
- Ho & Nosseir (1981) is Powell's equation with p = 0 (p. 6).
- Several loops can coexist and produce combination tones (Tam & Norum 1992) (p. 6).

**Component 1, propagation (p. 7–10)**
- Raman (1997a): above some NPR the expanded jet boundary blocks acoustic waves from a thin lip, and a thicker lip restores screech.
- Duct walls and reflectors alter staging (p. 8).
- Nonlinear steepening of loud tones may shift frequency (p. 8–9).
- Gojon & Bogey (2016–17) LES: upstream **neutral acoustic (guided) modes inside the jet**, with a modified Powell model, predict tone and azimuthal mode. This contrasts with the external propagation reported by Henderson 2005, Mitchell 2012 and Raman 1997a (p. 9–10).

**Component 2, receptivity (p. 11–12)**
- The shear layer is most receptive where it is thinnest.
- Tam (1986): frequency and wavenumber must match.
- Barone & Lele (2005): receptivity depends on incidence angle.
- Oberleithner et al. (2011): critical forcing grows about linearly with frequency detuning.
- Lip thickness changes the dominant mode in free jets (Ponton & Seiner 1992), and **Beneddine et al. (2015) reproduce this in a global stability analysis** (p. 12).
- Microjets, tabs and chevrons suppress tones (p. 12).

**Component 3, coherent structures (p. 14–17)**
- Azimuthal Fourier modes (Cohen & Wygnanski 1987): m = 0 is toroidal, m = 1 helical, and flapping is m = ±1 with a fixed phase.
- Growth falls as convective Mach number rises (Papamoschou & Roshko 1988).
- Literature u_c values for impinging jets are 0.52–0.6 U_clm (Krothapalli 1999) and 0.60 U_j (Gojon 2016) (p. 15).
- **Why POD:** snapshot POD is preferred over λ_ci or Galilean decomposition for quasi-periodic structures. A travelling structure appears as a sine/cosine pair, and its phase portrait is a ring (p. 16–17).
- Thesis data (Fig. 1.14) show coherent axial variance dipping at the shock reflection points x/D = 1.5, 2.8, 4.1. Whether the regrowth is shock-driven is left open (p. 17).

**Component 4, tone production (p. 19–21)**
- The generation efficiency η_g is "perhaps the least well understood" component (p. 19).
- **Shock leakage** (Manning & Lele 2000; Suzuki & Lele 2003): a vortex tilts the sonic line and the shock escapes into subsonic flow.
  - In free jets it occurs at shocks 2–6.
  - In impinging jets with z/D < 5 it may occur at earlier shocks or at the standoff shock (p. 19–20).
- **Henderson et al. (2005):** the wall-jet boundary near r ≈ 1.3D oscillates like a membrane (p. 21).
- There is no consensus on the impinging-tone mechanism (p. 21).

### Ch. 2: dataset (pp. 23–38)

The same D = 15 mm base nozzle is used throughout; only the external lip and the impingement surface change (p. 29). The conditions table is under Setup.

- **Thin lip** (t/D 0.1, NPR 3.4, z/D 5):
  - One tone, St = 0.255, with harmonics.
  - POD modes 1–2 hold 33.8%; every other mode is below 4% (p. 31).
  - The pair is helical (Ch. 3–4), and mode 3 is the STM.
- **Infinite lip** (t/D 7):
  - Tones at St = 0.385 and 0.241, plus 0.140 ≈ 0.385 − 0.241, attributed to nonlinear interaction between coexisting modes (p. 33).
  - POD: modes 1–2 toroidal and modes 3–4 helical, together 35.6% (p. 33).
- **Intermediate lip** (t/D 0.33):
  - Tones at St = 0.383 and 0.253.
  - Modes 1–2 hold 28.4% (toroidal) and modes 3–4 hold 6.2% (helical) (p. 34).
- **Infinite-foam/thin-lip:**
  - No tone; only a bump under 5 dB at the thin-lip frequency.
  - Sharper shock cells and less spreading.
  - Smooth POD spectrum with mode 1 ≈ 4.5%, so the loop is suppressed (p. 35–36).
- **Cylinder** (z/D 3.5, lip 0.33D, convex half-cylinder):
  - A fixed-axis **flapping** mode, so repeated PIV captures the same instability.
  - Single tone at St = 0.290; modes 1–2 hold 42.3% (p. 29–30).

### Ch. 3: shear thickness mode and POD practice (EiF 2018; pp. 39–58)

**What the STM is**
- In many resonating-jet PIV datasets (free and impinging, flat and cylindrical) a structured but **unpaired** low-energy POD mode appears, elongated along the whole shear layer.
- It is mode 2 of a free jet at NPR 3.4 and mode 10 of the NPR 3.6 cylinder case (p. 39, 43).
- Its shape varies between flows, so it is hard to identify by eye (p. 43).

**Thin-lip case** (= EiF Case 1)
- Modes 1–2 hold ≈ 33.7% and are a helical pair (antisymmetric u_x). Mode 3 holds 3.9% (the STM) and mode 4 1.7% (p. 45–46).
- **The STM coefficient tracks the pair amplitude.**
  - Pearson correlation of r₁₂ = √(a₁²+a₂²) with each of the first 20 coefficients: **0.74 for the STM and < 0.15 for all others** (p. 47).
  - Negative STM coefficients sit on the outer ring of the phase portrait (large amplitude).
  - The authors recommend this correlation, rather than spatial shape, as the way to find the STM (p. 47).
- **The STM tracks shear-layer thickness.**
  - In quintiles of the STM coefficient, the conditional δ_ω curves are the same up to x/D ≈ 1.4, then diverge after the shock reflections at x/D ≈ 1.4 and 2.8.
  - At x/D = 3.6 the spread is ≈ 0.12D, which is **27%** of δ_ω. The most positive quintile has the thinnest shear layer (p. 48–49).
  - At the 5th percentile the vorticity sheets meander; at the 95th they are straight.
  - Interpretation: amplitude drops of the **single** helical mode, not switching between modes (p. 49).

**Synthetic test** (Stuart vortex row, 10,000 fields)
- Translation only: modes 1–2 hold ≈ 99%, and modes 3–4 are the harmonic pair. **No STM** appears (p. 50).
- Snapshot-to-snapshot variation in δ_y (N(0.4, 0.05²)): an STM appears with ≈ 6% of the energy, the same shape as in the experiment and the same correlation with amplitude (p. 51).
- STM energy rises with **both** the size of the δ_y variation and the fraction of snapshots that vary (p. 54). So it measures amplitude unsteadiness and intermittency.

**POD basis variable** (u+v, u, v, ω_z; p. 52–53)
- Whether the STM appears does not depend on the basis, but how much energy it gets does.
- u-only gives it about twice the energy of v-only. ω_z lowers the energy of all modes and moves the STM to mode 4.
- Excluding the wall jet (x/D > 3.6) with v-only POD drops the STM to mode 6.
- In the synthetic data v-only gives almost none.
- Advice: look beyond the leading pair and try several bases (p. 53, 55, 58).

**POD convergence** (jackknife, B = 200, N = 100–5000, energies matched by spatial correlation; §3.7, p. 56–57)

| Case | Modes 1–2 converged by | Modes 3–4 converged by |
|---|---|---|
| Resonant flows (thin, infinite, cylinder) | N ≈ 500 | N ≈ 1000 |
| Foam case (no dominant mode) | N ≈ 1000 | N ≈ 2500, with erratic matching |

> The convergence threshold is inconsistent; see verify item 10.

### Ch. 4: nozzle external geometry selects the azimuthal mode (JFM 2019; pp. 59–90)

**Thin vs infinite lip** (fixed NPR 3.4, z/D 5)
- **Mean flow is almost the same** for both lips (peak ≈ 1.8 U_E; wall jet ≈ ±1.1 U_E). Only the infinite lip has an intermittent low-speed region behind the first shock (p. 68).
- **Variance maps differ:**
  - Infinite lip: large **axial** variance at the standoff shock, the axisymmetric signature (Powell 1988).
  - Thin lip: **transverse** variance at every shock (p. 68–70).
- **Tones** (p. 70; St = f D_j/U_j with D_j = 0.016 m and U_j = 413 m/s):
  - Thin lip: 0.255.
  - Infinite lip: 0.385 (dominant), 0.241, and 0.144, which is the difference tone.
- **POD:**
  - Thin lip: a helical pair, 17.7% and 16.1%. It is classed as helical rather than precessing flapping because the energy sits in one pair. Modes 3–4 form a cardioid, so they are not a pair.
  - Infinite lip: an m = 0 pair (12.1%, 11.3%) and a helical pair (6.6%, 5.6%) (p. 71–72).
- **u_c from POD wavelength × tone frequency:** λ = 2.0D and 1.94D give U_c ≈ 0.48 U_j (thin) and 0.44 U_j (infinite helical) (p. 72).
- **Contrast with free jets:** this lip-driven switch differs from Ponton & Seiner (1992), where a free jet staged from B to C as the lip thickened (p. 73).

**Two hypotheses tested**
- (1) A different acoustic path: the expansion blocks waves from reaching a thin lip, and a thick lip adds reflecting surface.
- (2) A different entrainment angle (p. 73).

**Intermediate lip** (t/D 0.33, conical outer surface, so thin-lip-like entrainment)
- Tones at 0.383 and 0.253. POD has an m = 0 pair (14.9%, 13.5%) and a weak helical pair (3.3%, 2.8%).
- It behaves like the infinite lip, so **entrainment is rejected** (p. 74, 77–79).
- Caveat: the entrainment that matters may lie closer to the exit than the PIV resolves.

**Foam variants**

| Variant | Result | Pages |
|---|---|---|
| Inf-foam/thin-lip | Tone suppressed; broadband-to-peak falls from 25 to 4 dB; no POD pair | 74–75, 78 |
| Rec-foam/thin-lip | Weak St 0.409 tone, probably toroidal | 74–75 |
| Inner-foam/thin-lip | No change | 80 |
| Outer-foam/thin-lip | St 0.255 tone gone; weak 0.472 appears | 80–82 |

- **The thin-lip helical loop closes only by reflection off the outer base surface.** The authors know of no earlier case of a loop closed exclusively by an upstream reflection (p. 75, 81).
- **Intermediate lip with foam:** the toroidal tone shifts to 0.398 and the helical tone survives, so that loop uses a different path (p. 79–80).

**Recurring tones and the Powell check**
- **Recurring tones** (Fig. 18): almost every configuration gives St ≈ 0.25 (helical) and/or ≈ 0.39 (toroidal). These are *assumed* to be the most unstable frequencies of the base flow (p. 81, 86), which is untested.
- **Powell check** (Table 5, p. 85):
  - The source is at the standoff shock, from schlieren, and looks like periodic shock leakage. p = 0 and U_s = 340 m/s.
  - Direct, inner-base and outer-base paths each reproduce both tone families for suitable integer N. The feedback equation therefore cannot choose the path, and the gain criterion must decide (p. 82–85).
  - The reflection point is uncertain. Moving it across the outer base shifts St from 0.34 to 0.42 (p. 84).
- **Thesis conclusion** (p. 89):
  - Closure here is by **freely propagating external acoustic waves**, contrasting with the guided-mode closure in Gojon/Bogey's LES.
  - The high-contraction nozzle's very thin initial shear layer makes η_r ≈ 1, so even a weak path can sustain feedback.

### Ch. 5: growth and convection of the coherent structures (pp. 91–108; unpublished)

**Triple decomposition** (cylinder case, M = 2 POD modes; p. 92–93)
- The coherent part holds the vortices plus the shock motion they cause.
- **The impingement-tone acoustic fronts end up in the *random* part.** Steep fronts are poorly represented by smooth modes, and cycle-to-cycle position jitter decorrelates them.
- The coherent variance shows a **standing-wave pattern outside the shear layer**, attributed to acoustic–hydrodynamic interaction (p. 93).

**Growth (CSTKE, eq. 5.4; p. 94–101)**
- **Thin lip:** coherent CSTKE grows sharply after the first shock reflection point and is flat after the second. The thesis concludes that **shock reflection points "appear to be significant instigators of growth"** (p. 95). See the tension with the Edgington-Mitchell 2020 note under Relevance.
- **Infinite lip:** the helical mode is ≈ 0 before the first reflection and overtakes the toroidal mode at x/D ≈ 3.1 (p. 95–98).
- **Intermediate lip:** total CSTKE is about half the infinite-lip value. Why the helical mode grows so late is "not clear" (p. 99).
- **Foam case:** random part only, with low CSTKE. It is proposed as a benchmark (p. 100).
- **Across cases:** a ~25% CSTKE spread before the second shock. The toroidal decay before x/D 3 may be early m = 0 saturation or competition from the helical mode (p. 100–101).

**Phase cycles from non-time-resolved PIV** (p. 101–107)
- **Phase average:** bin the *total* velocity by POD phase θ (δθ = 5°, ≈ 130 snapshots per bin).
  - It keeps shocks and acoustic fronts.
  - Waves travel from plate to lip at **330 m/s**, which is the speed of sound minus the entrainment flow (p. 102).
- **Phase reconstruction:** rebuild the field from the scaled pair (eq. 5.6). Shocks are smudged and acoustic waves are largely lost, so it suits ray tracing (p. 105).
- **Convective velocity** (cylinder):
  - u_c = 0.42–0.52 U_j, mean **0.48 U_j**, valid only over 0.75 < x/D < 2.6 (p. 107).
  - The minimum, ≈ 0.42 U_j, is at x/D ≈ 1.55 where the shock meets the shear layer; the maximum, ≈ 0.51 U_j, is at x/D ≈ 2.1 (Fig. 5.22, read from figure).

### Ch. 6: shock leakage at the standoff shock (pp. 109–122; unpublished)

- **Schlieren upstream component:** a 2D Fourier split in (x, t) of the UHS schlieren isolates upstream waves (k⁻). The thin-lip schlieren shows an acoustic wave emitted **at the standoff shock**, alternating sides (helical), and travelling to the lip (p. 109–111).
- **Ray tracing on measured fields** (geometrical acoustics; Manning & Lele 2000; Shariff & Manning 2013) is applied **for the first time to measured fields**: POD phase-reconstructed PIV (p. 117).
  - Thin lip, rays injected at the first shock: the rays mimic the shock-cell reflection pattern. The tone-relevant leakage is at the **standoff shock**, one strong event per side per cycle, travelling upstream to the lip (p. 117–118).
  - This differs from free jets, where the source lies between shock cells 3 and 5 (p. 118).
- **Validation against schlieren:** ray tracing and schlieren agree in **location and timing** (Fig. 6.8), within the limits of path-integrated vs planar data. Hence shock leakage is a tone-production mechanism in impinging jets, shown here for the first time (p. 118, 122).
- **Infinite lip:** each mode pair is reconstructed separately. The toroidal mode leaks on both sides and the helical mode antisymmetrically. Hand-picked schlieren sequences show matching symmetric and one-sided events (p. 119–121).
- **No phase delay:** shock leakage shows no delay between vortex arrival and emission (p ≈ 0), consistent with Gojon et al. (2016). Their source may also be standoff-shock leakage (p. 122).

### Ch. 7: the shocklet and the phase lag (JFM 2017; pp. 123–138)

**What the shocklet is**
- UHS schlieren (hemisphere, NPR 5.0, 12 µs between frames) shows a transient shock forming in the **wall jet** as a large vortex passes along the wall.
- It rotates towards perpendicular to the wall, moves slightly upstream, and finishes by emitting the acoustic wave that returns to the lip (p. 129).
- It is also seen at other NPRs, standoffs and surfaces, including flat plates (p. 129).

**Where it is**
- At (x/D, y/D) = (3.78, 1.43) from high-magnification phase-averaged PIV (p. 132).
- This is about the r ≈ 1.3D source of Henderson et al. (2005), and the thesis gives the first mechanistic explanation of it (p. 136).

**The phase lag**
- *p* is reinterpreted as the delay Δt between shocklet inception and acoustic emission (eq. 3.1).
- Lip receptivity delay is at most 2 µs, so negligible (p. 126).
- Earlier fitted values ranged from p = 0 (Gojon 2016) to −0.4 (Krothapalli 1999) (p. 126).

**Every term measured** (cylinder, NPR 3.6):
- Δt = **29.4 µs** (88° of the 120.5 µs cycle), equivalent to p ≈ −0.24.
- Prediction **8230 Hz vs 8300 Hz measured (−0.8%)**; with Δt = 0 it is 8750 Hz (+5.4%).
- Matching with Δt = 0 would need mean u_c ≈ 0.43 U_j, judged implausible.
- Hemisphere: Δt = 40 µs at 12,250 Hz, so p = −0.49 (p. 129–134).

### Ch. 8: conclusions (pp. 139–140)

**Stated conclusions**
- The POD pair describes the periodic vortices, and the STM shows that modes beyond the leading pair matter.
- External nozzle geometry alone switches the dominant azimuthal mode, through the acoustic feedback path. The jet "constructs" the loop that amplifies a mode.
- There are two new tone-production mechanisms: standoff-shock leakage and the shocklet.

**Ch. 8 has no future-work section.** Open questions stated elsewhere:
- why the secondary helical mode grows from near zero (p. 99);
- how near-wall vorticity allows leakage for both infinite-lip modes (p. 119);
- the cycle-to-cycle variation of the shocklet delay, which a phase average cannot give (p. 133);
- geometrical acoustics' failure on directivity and amplitude (p. 115).

### Appendices (pp. 145–190)

**App. A: six extra conditions for LES comparison** (thin vs infinite lip at three NPR/z/D pairs; statistics and POD only, no acoustics; p. 145–157)
- **NPR 3.0, z/D 3.5:** **both lips give a toroidal pair**, so lip thickness does not always switch the mode (p. 147, 157).
- **NPR 3.4, z/D 2, thin lip:** a toroidal pair plus an unexplained mode 3.

**App. C: ray-tracing validation**

### Ray tracing (Ch. 6, App. C)

**Lineage** (Manning & Lele 2000; p. 111–114)
- DNS of a forced shear layer passing an oblique shock.
- Simplified step by step to linearised Euler, then a Gaussian "G-wave" in place of the shock, then a Stuart vortex sheet, then geometrical acoustics (the shock treated as a standing acoustic wave). Each step kept the sound generation.

**Assumptions, both violated here** (p. 112):
- base-flow scales much larger than the acoustic wavelength;
- slow flow time scales. The convective Mach number is in fact about 1.

The method still reproduces the location and timing of leakage, but **not its directivity or amplitude**. It is qualitative only (p. 112–115).

**Physics** (p. 114–115)
- In a steady shear layer, rays turn back at the sonic line.
- Vortices tilt the sonic line (by α at the saddle point), so rays can escape. In the Stuart model nothing escapes for A ≤ 0.4.

**Implementation** (p. 116–117)

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Input fields | POD phase reconstruction of each mode pair |
| Time steps | 2000 per tone period |
| Step size | About half a vector spacing |
| Interpolation | Cubic |
| Velocity gradients | Second-order |
| Injection (thin lip) | At the first shock, (x/D, y/D) = (0.95, 0); one ray per step over the first period, then tracked for 9 more periods |
| Initial wavevector angle | arccos(1/M), with M from c = 340 m/s |

**Why the reconstruction is used:** PIV contains real shocks, which cause spurious ray reflections. The reconstruction smudges the shocks, which is an advantage here (p. 116–117).

### Modelling and simulation content
The thesis is experimental. Its models are:
- Powell's phenomenological feedback equation and gain criterion;
- geometrical-acoustics ray tracing on measured fields;
- a kinematic Stuart-vortex synthetic dataset (Ch. 3, App. C).

There is **no stability analysis, LES or DNS of its own**. Cited modelling that bears directly on the conclusions:
- Beneddine et al. 2015: global stability reproduces lip-driven mode switching.
- Gojon & Bogey 2016–17: LES with guided-mode closure, m = 0 + helical superposition, p = 0.
- Kuo & Dowling 1996: linear stability for standoff-shock entropy waves.
- Barone & Lele 2005 and Tam 1986: receptivity.
- Manning & Lele 2000, Suzuki & Lele 2003, Shariff & Manning 2013, Berland et al. 2007: DNS, LES and ray tracing of shock leakage.
- Morris 2010: q_h depends on frequency.

## Key equations

**Powell feedback timing** (eq. 1.1, p. 5; JFM eq. 1.1, p. 62, 126):
$$\frac{N+p}{f}=\int_0^{h_c}\frac{\mathrm dh}{u_c}+\frac{h_s}{U_s}$$
- N: vortices formed at one azimuth per loop.
- p: phase lag, at the source.
- h_c, u_c: convective path and local convective velocity.
- h_s, U_s: straight acoustic path (source to lip) and mean lab-frame wave speed.
- Ch. 4 uses constant U_c and p = 0, so $f=N/(h_c/U_c+h_s/U_s)$.
- The 2016 paper writes it with x_c, x_s and u_con.

**Powell gain criterion** (eq. 1.2, p. 6): $q_h\,\eta_r\,\eta_p\,\eta_g\ge 1$.
- q_h: shear-layer amplification, which depends on frequency but not on the path.
- η_r, η_p, η_g: efficiencies of receptivity, propagation and generation.
- The loop with the largest product dominates.

**Modified feedback equation** (JFM 815 eq. 3.1, p. 129):
$$\frac{N}{f}=\int_0^{h_c}\frac{\mathrm dh}{u_c}+\frac{h_s}{U_s}+\Delta t,\qquad \Delta t=-p/f,\quad 0\le\Delta t\le 1/f\ (-1\le p\le 0)$$

**Ho & Nosseir** (eq. 1.3, p. 6): $x/D=N/[St_{min}(U/C_1+U/C_2\cos\theta)]$. This is eq. 1.1 with p = 0, a mean u_c and a fixed path.

**Azimuthal decomposition** (Cohen & Wygnanski; eqs. 1.4–1.5, p. 14): Fourier series in time and azimuth.
- m = 0 is toroidal, m = 1 helical, and flapping is m = ±1 with a fixed phase.
- > Eq. 1.5 is printed with sums from m = 0 plus separate c₀(φ) and e₀(φ) terms, which looks redundant.

**Convective Mach number** (eq. 1.6): $M_{c,1}=(U_1-U_c)/a_1$.

**2D swirling strength** (eq. 1.7): λ_ci is the complex eigenvalue of the 2×2 velocity-gradient tensor.

**Gladstone–Dale relation and ray deflection** (eqs. 2.1–2.2, p. 24):
- $n=K\rho+1$;
- $\partial^2x/\partial z^2=(1/n)\,\partial n/\partial x$.

**Snapshot POD** (eqs. 2.3–2.7, p. 27; EiF eqs. 1–5):
$$\mathbf R\boldsymbol\nu=\lambda\boldsymbol\nu,\quad \mathbf R=\mathbf V^{T}\mathbf V,\quad \mathbf V=[\mathbf u'^1\cdots\mathbf u'^N],\quad \boldsymbol\phi_n=\frac{\mathbf V\boldsymbol\nu_n}{\lVert\mathbf V\boldsymbol\nu_n\rVert},\quad a_n=\boldsymbol\nu_n\lVert\mathbf V\boldsymbol\nu_n\rVert$$
- Each column stacks u′ over v′.
- No 1/N factor, which only scales λ.

**Pair phase and amplitude** (p. 28; EiF eq. 9):
- $\hat a_k e^{i\theta_k}=a_1(t_k)+i\,a_2(t_k)$;
- $r_{12}=\sqrt{a_1^2+a_2^2}$.

**Vorticity thickness** (EiF eq. 10, p. 48): $\delta_\omega=(U_2-U_1)/(\mathrm dU/\mathrm dy)_{max}$, with U₂ = U_E and U₁ = 0.

**Stuart vortex row** (EiF eqs. 6–8, p. 45; App. C eqs. C.1–C.4, p. 161):
$$u=U_c\Big(1-\frac{\sinh\tilde y}{\cosh\tilde y+\tilde A\cos\tilde x}\Big),\quad v=-U_c\frac{\tilde A\sin\tilde x}{\cosh\tilde y+\tilde A\cos\tilde x},\quad \tilde x=\frac{x-U_ct}{\delta_x},\ \tilde y=\frac{y}{\delta_y},\ \tilde A=\frac{A}{\sqrt{A^2+1}}$$
- App. C builds the 2D "jet" as $V_i=\tilde V_i(y_0=D/2)-\tilde V_i(y_0=-D/2)$.
- > Both EiF eq. 7 and eq. C.2 are **printed with sinh x̃**. sin x̃ is the standard form, and sinh would be unbounded in x̃. EiF also lacks a δ_y/δ_x factor on v.

**Triple decomposition** (eqs. 5.1–5.3, p. 92):
- $u_i=U_i+u_i^c+u_i''$;
- $\mathbf u^c=\sum_{n=1}^{M}\boldsymbol\phi_n a_n^T$;
- $\mathbf u''=\mathbf u'-\mathbf u^c$.

**CSTKE** (eq. 5.4, p. 94): $C(x)=2\pi\int_0^{y}\frac{\langle VV\rangle}{U_E^2}\,y\,\mathrm dy$, with V = u′, u^c or u″.
- > Not stated: the upper limit, whether C sums both components, and which half-plane is used.

**Phase reconstruction** (eqs. 5.5–5.6, p. 105):
- $\phi_{i,scaled}=\sqrt2\,\mathrm{rms}(a_i)\,\phi_i$;
- $\mathbf u(x,y,\theta)=\phi_{1,s}\cos\theta+\phi_{2,s}\sin\theta$;
- θ = 2π f_tone t.
- This is Re[(S₁φ₁ − iS₂φ₂)e^{iθ}], the same complex-mode form as Φ = φ₁ + iφ₂ in Edgington-Mitchell et al. 2020 (my observation, not the thesis's).

**(x, t) Fourier split** (eqs. 6.1–6.3, p. 110):
- $\hat{\hat I}(k,y,\omega)=\iint I\,e^{-i\omega t}e^{-ikx}\,\mathrm dt\,\mathrm dx$;
- $u_p=\omega/k$, whose sign separates k⁻ (upstream) from k⁺ (downstream).

**Eikonal and Hamiltonian rays** (eqs. 6.4–6.8, p. 114), with q = ∂Θ/∂t and **b** = ∇Θ:
$$\Theta_t+\mathbf v\cdot\nabla\Theta=-c|\nabla\Theta|,\quad \frac{\mathrm d\mathbf R}{\mathrm dt}=\mathbf v-\frac{c^2\mathbf b}{q+\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf b},\quad \frac{\mathrm d\mathbf b}{\mathrm dt}=\frac{(\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf b)\nabla c^2}{2(q+\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf b)}-\nabla(\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf v),\quad \frac{\mathrm dq}{\mathrm dt}=\frac{(\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf b)\,\partial_t c^2}{2(q+\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf b)}-\mathbf b\cdot\partial_t\mathbf v$$
- Also dΘ/dt = 0.
- Using q + v·b = −c|b|, these reduce to $\dot{\mathbf R}=\mathbf v+c\,\mathbf b/|\mathbf b|$ and $\dot{\mathbf b}=-|\mathbf b|\nabla c-\nabla(\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf v)$.

**Shariff & Manning form** (eqs. 6.9–6.10, p. 116):
- $\dot x_i=V_i+k_ic/|\mathbf k|$;
- printed as $\dot k_i=k_j\,\partial V_i/\partial x_j+\partial c/\partial x_i$.
- > The printed eq. 6.10 conflicts with eq. 6.7. The standard form is $\dot k_i=-k_j\,\partial V_j/\partial x_i-|\mathbf k|\,\partial c/\partial x_i$. App. C's agreement with Shariff & Manning suggests the code is right and the print is a typo.

**Mach angle** (p. 114, 117): $\theta_M=\arcsin(1/M)$ for the wavefront, while the initial wavevector angle is arccos(1/M) = 90° − θ_M.

**Convective velocity from a POD wavelength** (p. 72): $U_c=f\lambda$, i.e. $U_c/U_j=St\,\lambda/D_j$.
- Check: 0.255 × 2.0 × 15/16 = 0.478, and 0.241 × 1.94 × 15/16 = 0.438.

## Relevance to this project

**This is the user's own PhD work and the foundation of the group's impinging-jet analysis practice.** It predates the SPOD, BMD and Hilbert-POD stages of the current workflow in CLAUDE.md, so it shows clearly what the non-time-resolved route can and cannot give.

### Non-time-resolved PIV: the POD route, fully specified
- **Workflow:** statistics, then snapshot POD (u′ and v′ by default), then pair identification by phase portrait and spatial symmetry, then θ = arg(a₁ + i a₂).
- **Then one of two phase-cycle methods:**
  - *phase average*: δθ = 5°, ≈ 100–130 snapshots per bin, total velocity;
  - *phase reconstruction*: S_i = √2 rms(a_i).
  - Phase is mapped to time with the microphone tone frequency (p. 27–28, 101–107).
- **Which method to use:**
  - The average keeps shocks and acoustic fronts.
  - The reconstruction is smooth but contains only the fundamental (p. 105).
  - The same loss of harmonics and fronts applies to an SPOD reconstruction that keeps only the tone frequency.
- **Snapshot budget:** ≈ 500 snapshots for the leading pair and ≈ 1000 for modes 3–4 in resonant flows; ≈ 2500 with no dominant mode (§3.7).
  - Report mean uncertainty in px against the 0.1 px limit (App. B).
- **Easy improvements to test (my suggestions):**
  - a radius threshold or r-weighting in the phase binning, since low-amplitude snapshots have an ill-defined phase;
  - a wavenumber-filtered (KH-only) CSTKE.

### The STM as the non-time-resolved signature of amplitude modulation
- **r₁₂ is a per-snapshot envelope.** It is the POD-pair analogue of a Hilbert amplitude, and the STM is the mean-flow distortion (shear-layer thickening) that goes with drops in amplitude (Ch. 3).
- **The STM measures unsteadiness, not its timescale.** Its energy grows with both the size and the intermittency of the variation (p. 54), but PIV cannot give how fast the modulation happens.
  - **HPOD on time-resolved schlieren** (or SPOD → reconstruction → HPOD) can measure it directly. The STM result says what to correlate the HPOD amplitude with: shear-layer thickness or mean-flow deformation.
- **(My inference) The STM behaves like a mean-field "shift mode."** In SPOD its content sits at f ≈ 0 or in low-frequency sidebands. A reconstruction that keeps only the tone-frequency modes will drop it, so keep a low-frequency band if amplitude–mean-flow coupling matters.
- **The basis variable matters.** v-only POD hides the STM, which is why Edgington-Mitchell et al. 2020 use it to isolate the resonance pair. Run (u,v) or u-only POD too before calling a flow a clean single-mode oscillation (p. 52–58).

### Open questions the time-resolved pipeline (SPOD/BMD → reconstruction → HPOD) could answer
- **Coexistence or switching** of the m = 0 and helical modes in the infinite- and intermediate-lip jets.
  - PIV POD and averaged spectra cannot tell them apart (p. 33, 70, 119). The literature is split: Kumar et al. 2013 found switching, while the 2017 AIAA paper found the modes simultaneous.
- **Triadic coupling** of the infinite-lip tones (0.144 = 0.385 − 0.241, plus sums and harmonics): a direct **BMD** target. The thesis only infers nonlinear interaction (p. 33, 70).
- **Distribution of the shocklet delay Δt**, which the thesis says the phase average cannot give (p. 133). Because Δt sets f through eq. 3.1, cycle-to-cycle Δt from HPOD instantaneous phase would test the mechanism directly.
- **Intermittency** of standoff-shock leakage events, and of the possible intermittent toroidal mode in App. A Case 4 (NPR 3.4, z/D 2, thick lip; p. 151).
- **u_c variability:** u_c(x) is a single phase-averaged curve. SPOD or HPOD phase gradients would give its distribution in time.

**Data limits for that pipeline**
- The thesis schlieren gives at most 102 frames (~204 µs at 500 kfps), which is ≈ 1.4 thin-lip tone periods. SPOD blocks would have Δf ≈ 5 kHz (ΔSt ≈ 0.18), too coarse.
- So the existing schlieren suits **mechanism visualisation**, not SPOD or HPOD. New long-record time-resolved schlieren of these configurations would be needed.
- The 2.5 s acoustic records *are* long enough for Hilbert or short-time tracking of tone amplitude and frequency, and for bispectra.

### Modelling side
- **Powell's equation is under-determined.** Many (path, N) pairs fit each tone, and moving the reflection point shifts St by ±0.04 (p. 84–85). It shows a path is *possible*. Choosing between paths needs the gain criterion (receptivity, amplification, propagation efficiency), which is never quantified.
- **Ch. 7 is the template for a quantitative test:** measure every term, including U_s corrected for entrainment, and run a sensitivity check on Δt or u_c.
- **The two mechanisms imply different phase lags.** Shock leakage gives p ≈ 0 and the shocklet p ≈ −0.24 to −0.49. A fitted p may therefore show which source is active.
- **The "most unstable frequency" claim is untested.** The recurring St ≈ 0.25 and 0.39 tones (p. 81, 86) are assumed to be the base flow's most unstable frequencies.
  - A local or global stability analysis of the measured PIV mean flows, like the global analysis in [Edgington-Mitchell et al. 2020](edgington-mitchell-2020-waves-in-screeching-jets.md), would test it.
  - Beneddine et al. (2015) already show lip-driven mode switching in a global analysis.
  - Resolvent analysis on the mean flow could quantify q_h and the receptivity to lip forcing at different incidence angles (Barone & Lele 2005).
- **Ray tracing on measured fields:**
  - It is qualitative: location and timing only, with its scale-separation assumptions violated.
  - It needs a sound-speed field that PIV does not provide; c = 340 m/s is used at injection.
  - Leakage is very sensitive to vortex amplitude (App. C). A phase reconstruction smooths that amplitude, while SPOD-reconstructed or HPOD-resolved fields would keep it.
- **Synthetic Stuart-vortex datasets** are a cheap way to test what POD, SPOD or HPOD will show for a known kinematic change (amplitude modulation, intermittency) before applying them to experiments.
- **Receptivity matters when comparing nozzles.** The high-contraction nozzle gives a thin initial shear layer, so η_r ≈ 1 (p. 89). This matters when comparing with other nozzles or with LES inflow conditions.
- **The LES-comparison matrix** (eight conditions, App. A) has no LES results in the thesis. The non-resonant cases (App. A Cases 4–6) are useful negative cases for any LES or stability comparison.

### Cross-links: [Edgington-Mitchell et al. 2020](edgington-mitchell-2020-waves-in-screeching-jets.md)
**Agreements**
- Both use the same group, family of D = 15 mm converging nozzles, and PIV-POD practice.
- EM2020's transverse-only POD rests on Ch. 3, which adds the caveat that it hides the STM.
- EM2020 cite Ch. 4 (Weightman et al. 2019) as an impinging case closed by **free-stream acoustic waves**, consistent with this thesis.
- The free-jet sound source lies between shock cells 3 and 5 (p. 118), consistent with EM2020's guided mode peaking near the fourth shock cell.

**Tension: shocks and coherent growth**
- Ch. 5 concludes that shock reflection points trigger coherent growth (p. 95), and Fig. 1.14 shows variance dipping at the reflections.
- EM2020 find no direct shock modulation of KH growth, and explain the spatial modulation as interference between k_kh⁺, k_t⁻ and k_t⁺ waves.
- The thesis's own coherent variance shows a standing wave outside the shear layer (p. 93). So the CSTKE steps may be partly wave interference. A KH-only (k_x > 0) filtered CSTKE would decide this.

**Not contradictory: the closure path**
- Ch. 4 shows the external reflecting path is *necessary* for the thin-lip helical tone, but never tests for guided-mode content, which would need a wavenumber decomposition.
- Re-analysing the thin- and infinite-lip PIV with EM2020's complex-mode wavenumber split would show whether a k_t⁻ component is present.

**Hardware differences**
- EM2020's nozzle lip is 5 mm = 0.33D, the same t/D as the intermediate lip and cylinder cases.
- EM2020 give a contraction of ≈ 100:1. This thesis gives 81 (Ch. 2) and the 2016 paper 240, so they may not be the same nozzle.

### Cross-links: other notes in the library
- **Hilbert POD on non-time-resolved PIV:** [Raiola & Kriegseis 2025](raiola-2025-hilbert-pod.md) and [Davey et al. 2025](davey-2025-hpod-sphere-wake.md). Space-only HPOD takes the Hilbert transform along x instead of t, so it runs on snapshot PIV like the thesis datasets.
  - It would give each snapshot a local amplitude and wavenumber along the shear layer. That is a spatially resolved version of r₁₂, and a direct way to locate where the STM's amplitude modulation happens.
  - Davey et al. also use HPOD to confirm POD mode pairs, which could cross-check the phase-portrait and symmetry pairing used here.
  - Neither version gives temporal information from snapshot PIV. The timescale still needs time-resolved data.
- **Guided-mode closure:** [Edgington-Mitchell et al. 2018](edgington-mitchell-2018-upstream-acoustic-modes-screech-closure.md) and [Mancinelli et al. 2019](mancinelli-2019-screech-prediction-upstream-modes.md). This is the alternative to the thesis's external-acoustic-ray closure.
  - Mancinelli et al. note the same resonance model applies to impinging jets, with the plate as the downstream end condition.
  - Testing the thin- and infinite-lip PIV for a k⁻ guided-mode component would show whether the two closure paths compete in these jets.
- **Shocks modulating the wavepacket:** [Nogueira et al. 2021, wavepacket modulation](nogueira-2021-wavepacket-modulation-shock-containing-jets.md) gives a linear (parabolised Floquet) model of how shock cells modulate KH wavepackets. - **Screech as a global mode:** [Nogueira et al. 2021, absolute instability](nogueira-2021-absolute-instability-shock-containing-jets.md) treats screech as a global oscillator. It is a possible framework for testing the thesis's untested "most unstable frequency" explanation of the recurring St ≈ 0.25 and 0.39 tones.
