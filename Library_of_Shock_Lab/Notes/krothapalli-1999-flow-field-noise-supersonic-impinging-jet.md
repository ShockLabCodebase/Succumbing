# Flow field and noise characteristics of a supersonic impinging jet

- **Authors:** A. Krothapalli, E. Rajkuperan, F. Alvi and L. Lourenco, Department of Mechanical Engineering, Florida A&M University and Florida State University, Tallahassee (p. 155). ## Summary
A cold, nominally ideally expanded Mach 1.5 jet (C-D nozzle, NPR 3.7), with a sonic nozzle for comparison, issues through a 254 mm lift plate and impinges on a large ground plane at h/d = 2.5–60, to study STOVL hover lift loss and near-field noise (p. 155, 159–161). Mean surface pressures give suckdown forces of up to about 60 % of thrust near h/d_j ≈ 2, falling as a power law in h/(D − d); shock cells matter only for h/d ≲ 3 (p. 172–174). Forty PIV snapshots per case, processed with a mesh-free particle-based algorithm, show large vortical structures appearing with impingement and driving entrainment speeds of up to 50 m s⁻¹ (8 m s⁻¹ for the free jet) (p. 165–167). The structures' convection speed, taken from vorticity peaks in single snapshots, is 0.6U_j in the free jet but falls to 0.52U_clm at h/d = 4 and rises linearly with h/d. Shock cells (NPR 2.5 and 5.0) raise it (p. 167). The impinging tones stage with h/d. Powell's feedback formula with these measured speeds and zero phase lag misses them; an empirical lag p = −0.4 brings them into agreement at NPR 3.7 and 5.0 (p. 176–178). Since this jet has no stand-off shock, the authors put the lag down to more complex receptivity at a lip that is also a reflecting plate, though they say p is a fit that cannot be split into its causes (p. 177–178). No stability analysis or simulation is done; the modelling is Powell's equation and Tam's screech-frequency formula.

## Key findings
- **Mode of oscillation against plate height, M 1.5 nozzle near design** (shadowgraphs, Fig. 3, p. 162–163).
  - The free jet is helical-dominated (images not shown) and stays helical with the plate down to h/d ≈ 8, where the acoustic field also looks asymmetric. From h/d ≈ 6 to 4 the axisymmetric mode dominates, with symmetric large structures. At h/d ≈ 3 the helical mode returns.
  - Incident (upstream, concave-upwards) and lift-plate-reflected (downstream, opposite curvature) waves are visible. Fronts traced back to their centre place the source in the stagnation region on the ground plane (Fig. 4a, h/d ≈ 4; p. 163–164).
  - The weak shock cells of the near-design jet come from the straight conical divergence and finite lip. The authors say they "preclude" screech, a statement that holds for the free jet without the plate (p. 163; compare Fig. 17).
  - At h/d ≈ 5.5 a standing-wave pattern of horizontal lines fills the gap between lift plate and ground plane: a "duct" resonance set by that gap (Fig. 4b, p. 164; Krothapalli & Hsia 1996). The spacing of the upstream fronts roughly matches the feedback-loop frequency (p. 164).
- **Underexpanded jet, NPR 5.0** (mean of 17 shadowgraphs, Fig. 5, p. 164–165). A stand-off shock forms at h/d ≈ 6, 4 and 3.4, with a stagnation bubble at h/d ≈ 4. It is never seen for h/d > 8 and not always for h/d < 8; its size, shape and presence depend strongly on NPR and h/d. Axisymmetric and helical oscillations both occur, as in the near-design jet.
- **PIV structure** (Figs 6–8, p. 165–168).
  - The free near-design jet shows no discernible large organized structures. At h/d = 4 large, nearly symmetric structures appear and pass into the radial wall jet "without losing much of their coherence" (p. 165–166).
  - At NPR 5.0 the structures are smaller and less organized, and the stand-off shock is visible in the images (p. 166).
  - Vorticity peaks exceed the ±76 000 s⁻¹ colour scale (Figs 7–8). The structure in Fig. 8 sits at r ≈ 13 mm, x ≈ 70 mm, on the lip line (lip radius 13.75 mm).
  - The wall jet is "primarily characterized by" large vortical structures that originate in the jet shear layer (p. 170).
- **Convection velocity of the large structures** (Figs 9–10, p. 166–167).
  - Free jet: C_i ≈ 0.6U_j (p. 167; Fig. 10 shows ≈ 0.60 for h/d = 60).
  - Impinging, NPR 3.7, h/d = 4: individual structures give C_i/U_clm ≈ 0.47–0.64 between x ≈ 8 and 74 mm, with a mean of 0.52 and no clear trend with x (Fig. 9, p. 167).
  - The mean C_i/U_clm rises linearly with h/d towards the free-jet value. At NPR 3.7 it goes from 0.52 at h/d = 4 to 0.56 at h/d = 10. For a given h/d, shock-containing jets have faster structures: NPR 2.5 (overexpanded for this nozzle) gives 0.557–0.583 and NPR 5.0 gives 0.560–0.592 (Fig. 10, read; table below). The authors note that the structures are much smaller with shock cells and sit towards the high-speed side of the shear layer (p. 167).
  - No structures could be followed within the last ≈ 20–25 mm before the wall (x ≲ 75–80 mm at h/d = 4), so C_i may be biased **high**, since structures should slow near the plate. The authors expect the bias to be small (p. 167).
  - Convective Mach number at NPR 5.0, M_c = (U_clm − C_i)/a_j, is quoted as falling from 0.73 at h/d = 10 to 0.66 at h/d = 4, consistent with Powell's (1988) values from flow visualisation (p. 167). My check from the Fig. 10 values (U_clm ≈ 453 m s⁻¹, a_j ≈ 273 m s⁻¹) gives 0.73 at h/d = 4 and 0.68 at h/d = 10, so the two h/d labels look swapped.
- **Entrainment** (Figs 11–12, p. 167). At r/d = 1.5 the instantaneous near-field speed q = (u² + v²)^½ is about 8 m s⁻¹ for the free jet and up to about 50 m s⁻¹ for the impinging jet, with peaks at passing structures. The radial wall jet reaches q ≈ 75–95 m s⁻¹ beyond x ≈ 75 mm (read), and its thickness changes from snapshot to snapshot. The high entrainment speeds lower the pressure on the lift plate, which is the suckdown mechanism (p. 167, 179).
- **Mean centreline velocity** (40-snapshot means, Fig. 13, p. 170–172).
  - The authors say 40 fields are "not sufficient to obtain a true mean", only trends (p. 170).
  - U_clm, the mean free-jet centreline velocity over x/d = 0.2–5, is about 423 m s⁻¹ at NPR 3.7 and 453 m s⁻¹ at NPR 5.0 (read from Fig. 13). The isentropic U_j values are 429 and 466 m s⁻¹ (my calculation).
  - NPR 3.7, h/d = 4 (plate at x = 101.6 mm): the centreline is "nominally constant" to x/d ≈ 3, then decelerates smoothly through compression waves and is sonic at x = 86 mm (x/d ≈ 3.4), with **no stand-off shock** (p. 171–172).
    - My reading: the impinging trace has a dip to ≈ 360 m s⁻¹ near x ≈ 45 mm where the free jet is near its peak, so the weak cells shift with the plate.
  - NPR 5.0, h/d = 4: a sharp deceleration downstream of x ≈ 84 mm (x/d ≈ 3.3) marks the stand-off shock, smeared by particle lag (p. 172).
  - The critical flow changes are confined to about one jet diameter above the ground plane (p. 172).
- **Lift loss** (Figs 14–16, p. 172–174).
  - Suckdown, normalised by the isentropic thrust, reaches ≈ 0.56 (M 1.5) and ≈ 0.63 (M 1.0) at h/d_j ≈ 1.8, falls to ≈ 0.13 by h/d_j ≈ 3.7 and to ≈ 0.02 by 9.2 (Fig. 14, read). The text quotes "about 60 %" at h/d ≈ 2 (printed as "x/d = 2", p. 172).
  - It agrees with the thrust-balance data of Levin & Wardwell (1997) (sonic nozzle, NPR 2.75), which supports integrating the surface pressures (p. 173).
  - Correlation: (L − L_f)/T = 0.02 [h/(D − d)]^−2.24, similar to Levin & Wardwell's (Fig. 15, p. 173).
  - Ideally expanded and underexpanded jets give nearly the same lift loss except very close to the ground (p. 172–173). For the sonic nozzle, the variation with NPR is measurable only for h/d ≲ 3 (Fig. 16, p. 174). At h/d = 2.5 and 3 the loss drops by ≈ 0.05–0.06 of thrust between NPR 3.7 and 4.0 (read). This is speculatively tied to the appearance of the stand-off shock and bubble and to the entrainment of a transonic-to-supersonic wall jet (p. 174).
- **Screech and the lift plate** (Figs 17–18, p. 174–175).
  - Free jet, NPR 3.7, no lift plate: no screech, only a broad BBSAN hump from the weak cells (≈ 5–9 kHz, ≈ 112–115 dB, read). With the lift plate a distinct tone appears (≈ 4.7 kHz, ≈ 121 dB, read; St ≈ 0.30 on D_j, my conversion), so the plate raises screech amplitude. At NPR 5.0 with the plate the tone is strong, with harmonics (≈ 3.8 kHz at ≈ 139 dB, then 7.6, 11.4 and 15.1 kHz, read) (p. 174–175).
  - The authors expected this. A reflecting surface near the jet alters screech (Poldervaart et al. 1973), and a thick lip adds about 10 dB ([Norum 1983](norum-1983-screech-suppression-supersonic-jets.md)) (p. 174).
  - Against Tam's (1991) formula, both nozzles, with and without the plate, agree for NPR ≳ 3.3. The lift plate therefore enhances the screech magnitude without measurably shifting its Strouhal number (p. 175). The authors attribute the departures below NPR 3 to symmetric (toroidal) modes, which the helical-mode formula does not cover (p. 175).
    - My reading of Fig. 18: the convergent-nozzle points fall from St ≈ 0.70 and 0.63 (NPR 2.2, 2.3; within 5–9 % of the curve, which I reproduce as 0.77 and 0.66 with T_r/T_∞ = 1) to ≈ 0.41 at NPR 2.4, about 30 % below the curve. That jump looks like the A-to-B stage change of round convergent jets, in which case the low points at NPR 2.4–3.0 would be the sinuous B family rather than toroidal modes.
    - Also my reading: at NPR 2.9–3.3 the convergent nozzle *with* the plate sits ≈ 15 % above the no-plate points (e.g. 0.37 against 0.32 at NPR 3.0). That suggests the plate changes stage selection there, which the paper does not discuss.
- **Impinging tones, NPR 3.7** (Figs 19–20, p. 175–176).
  - Near-field spectra hold several tones, and a small change in h/d changes both frequency and level sharply. At h/d = 3.75, 4 and 4.25 the strongest peaks are (read; Fig. 19 levels offset by 0, 10 and 20 dB) about 6.2, 4.6 and 5.65 kHz. The last reaches ≈ 150 dB at the microphone, with harmonics at 11.3 and 17.1 kHz.
  - The tone map (Fig. 20, h/d_j = 1.8–9.2) falls "roughly along parallel lines", the staging pattern of a feedback loop. Most amplitude-dominant tones do **not** lie on the free-jet screech line (≈ 4.7 kHz), so most tones are made by impingement (p. 176).
  - There are tones at every plate height down to h/d_j ≈ 1.8, with no tone-free gaps (Fig. 20). This fits the remark of [Edgington-Mitchell 2019](edgington-mitchell-2019-aeroacoustic-resonance-review.md) (p. 144 there) that zones of silence do not occur in these ideally expanded jets.
- **The phase-lag argument** (p. 176–178). This is the paper's main modelling claim, step by step:
  1. Eq. (1) with C_i from the PIV (Fig. 10) and **p = 0** gives the lines in Fig. 20, which "clearly do not agree" with the tones. In subsonic jets the zero-lag formula works reasonably well (p. 177).
  2. Henderson & Powell (1993) and Powell (1988) add stand-off-shock oscillation as the source for underexpanded jets. That is rejected here: flow visualisation and PIV show sound coming from the impingement region with no stand-off shock, because the weak-celled flow decelerates through compression waves (smooth centreline, Fig. 13a) (p. 177).
  3. Receptivity: the formula assumes a simple conversion of sound to instability waves at the lip, whereas coupling actually spreads over a few instability wavelengths (Tam 1978; Ahuja & Tam 1982). Here the lift plate at the exit also reflects waves (Fig. 4), and these, together with the incident upstream waves, meet the shear layer near the exit. Powell had already argued that the phases of sound and disturbance need not match at the lip and at the source; the reflecting plate "may" accentuate this. So p = 0 is "probably incorrect" (p. 177).
  4. **p = −0.4** gives "good agreement" (Fig. 21, p. 177–178). A limited NPR 5.0 data set (not shown) also needed p ≠ 0 and fitted the same −0.4. The authors conclude that either stand-off-shock oscillation plays a minor role or its effect is absorbed in p, and add that under-prediction of C_i could also contribute (p. 177–178).
  5. Caveats from the paper: p "was not obtained from a rigorous analysis" but is simply the best fit "over a wide range of conditions". The shares of stand-off-shock oscillation, convection-speed error and true phase lag "cannot be determined". For highly underexpanded jets the stand-off shock probably matters more (p. 178).
  - The conclusions call the prediction "accurate" without repeating that p was fitted (p. 180).
  - **My check of Fig. 20.** I read 33 amplitude-dominant and 30 other tones against the plotted p = 0 lines (all values in the results file). The fractional part of N + p has a circular mean of −0.42 for the dominant tones (resultant length 0.56) and −0.46 for the others (0.75), which supports p ≈ −0.4 on average. Individual dominant tones scatter from about −0.1 (h/d_j 6.2–6.4, N + p ≈ 6.9–7.0) to −0.6 (h/d_j 5.5–6.0, N + p ≈ 3.4–3.5).
    - Within a stage N + p creeps upwards with h, e.g. 6.52 → 6.81 from h/d_j 8.0 to 8.9, so the frequency falls more slowly than the model's ≈ h^−0.96. This is the "frequency squeezing" the topic page records for Ho & Nosseir's subsonic stages.
    - My reading resolution is ±2–3 % in f, i.e. ±0.1–0.2 in N + p.
  - **Wording conflict.** On p. 167 the authors say missing near-wall data may bias C_i *high*. On p. 177–178 they list "under-prediction" of C_i as a possible contributor to p. A negative p is what an over-estimated C_i would mimic (see Key equations), so the p. 167 direction is the consistent one.
- **Duct modes** (p. 178). When the screech or impinging-tone frequency matches a "duct" mode of the lift-plate–ground gap, standing waves form (Fig. 4b), as Messersmith (1995) also saw.
- **Overall noise** (Fig. 22, p. 178–179).
  - The lift plate alone has a negligible effect on free-jet OASPL (within ≈ 0.5 dB, read).
  - The ground plane raises OASPL by about 8 dB at NPR 3.7 (≈ 155.6–156.1 against 147.4 dB, read). This is consistent with Soderman's (1990) full-scale data (p. 178), and the increase is "relatively insensitive" to NPR above 3 (p. 180).
  - h/d = 3 and 5 differ by ≲ 1.5 dB above NPR 2.5 (read).
  - The introduction gives the impingement increase as "approximately 10 dB" (p. 158). The image-source model of Sutherland & Brown (1972) predicts up to 14 dB, more than Soderman (1990) measured (p. 158).
- **Control and lift loss** (p. 179). The conclusions claim that eliminating the self-sustained oscillation would significantly reduce lift loss, for example by suppressing the tone with a small plate normal to the jet centreline outside the jet (Karamcheti et al. 1969; Elavarasan et al. 1998). This paper does not demonstrate it.

## Setup / method
- **Facility** (p. 159–160). A hover test rig for jet-induced forces on STOVL models (details in Wardwell et al. 1993).
  - The ground plane is 2.44 × 2.44 m on a hydraulic lift, centred under the model, carrying a 1 × 1 m, 2.54 cm thick instrumented Plexiglas plate. The introduction calls it "4000d" across (p. 157), which neither the 96d width nor the area ratio (≈ 11 700) matches.
  - Air comes from a blow-down system: a reciprocating compressor, storage up to 160 bar and 10 m³ of tanks, enough to run the M 1.5 jet continuously for up to 40 min.
  - Shadowgraph: conventional single-pass system, 30 cm field, stroboscopic flash of 1.3–7 µs at five settings, up to 1 kHz repetition (p. 160). Images were recorded on SVHS video (p. 162). Not time-resolved at the tone frequencies (2–10 kHz).
  - Nearby exposed metal was covered with 10 cm acoustic foam (p. 160). The room is not described as anechoic.
- **Nozzles** (p. 159).
  - Convergent: exit d = 25.4 mm.
  - C-D: throat 25.4 mm, exit 27.5 mm, designed for M 1.5, with a straight conical divergence of 3° to mimic practical nozzles. Other divergence angles were built, but only the 3° nozzle is reported. The contraction is a third-order polynomial with a contraction ratio of ≈ 5.
  - The lip thickness is not given ("finite", p. 163). The nozzle is flush with the lift plate, so the exit is in effect a hole in a 254 mm baffle reaching ≈ 4 d_j beyond the lip (my estimate).
- **Lift plate** (p. 160). Diameter D = 254 mm (≈ 10 exit diameters), 17 static taps along one radius, clustered near the nozzle, read by a Validyne transducer through a Scanivalve with several seconds per port. Lift force comes from integrating the mean pressures.
- **Conditions** (p. 161). NPR 1.5–5.5; h = 2.5d–60d (d = throat diameter); stagnation temperature ≈ 20 °C; top-hat exit profile with laminar boundary layers; nominal exit Re = 7 × 10⁵. My isentropic values (T0 = 293 K):

| Nozzle | NPR | M_j | U_j (m s⁻¹) | D_j (mm) | State |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| C-D, M_d 1.5 | 2.5 | 1.22 | 368 | 25.9 | overexpanded |
| C-D, M_d 1.5 | 3.7 | 1.50 | 429 | 27.6 | ≈ ideally expanded (design NPR 3.67) |
| C-D, M_d 1.5 | 5.0 | 1.71 | 466 | 29.5 | underexpanded |
| Convergent | 3.7 | 1.50 | 429 | 27.6 | underexpanded |

- **Acoustics** (p. 160). One 0.635 cm (1/4") B&K microphone at 90° to the jet axis, about 25 cm (≈ 9 D_j) from the nozzle exit, recorded with a National Instruments/LabView system. Narrowband spectra. Sampling rate, record length, averaging and window are not stated.
- **PIV** (§2.1, p. 161–162; §3.2, p. 165–166).
  - Hardware: Kodak ES1.0 camera (KAI-1001 interline CCD, 1008 × 1018 px, 9 µm pixels, 8 bit, 30 frame/s, so 15 image pairs/s); dual Spectra-Physics Nd:YAG lasers at 15 Hz; pulse separation **2 µs** (≈ 0.86 mm or ≈ 8.6 px of travel at U_j, my estimate). Single plane through the jet axis only.
  - Seeding: ≈ 1 µm oil droplets in the jet from a modified Laskin nozzle, and ≈ 5 µm smoke in the ambient from a Rosco fog generator (p. 160).
    - My Stokes estimates, with assumed densities of 900 and 1000 kg m⁻³: τ_p ≈ 3 µs for the oil and ≈ 80 µs for the smoke. The smoke value is comparable to the transit time of an entrained structure, so the entrainment speeds may be lagged. The paper gives no response estimate.
  - Processing: the mesh-free algorithm of Lourenco & Krothapalli (1998).
    - Particle images (3–4 px) are themselves the interrogation regions. Each particle pair gives a displacement at its midpoint on an unstructured set of points.
    - A second-order polynomial is least-squares fitted to the scattered vectors (eq. 2) and differentiated analytically for the gradients (eq. 3), with built-in vector validation. Results are then put on a regular 120 × 80 (x, r) grid "with interrogation regions of 8 × 8 pixels" = 0.8 × 0.8 mm (p. 166), which gives 0.1 mm px⁻¹ and ≈ 0.03D_j.
    - The two statements of the effective window (3–4 px on p. 161, 8 × 8 px on p. 166) are not reconciled in the text.
  - **Snapshots:** 40 instantaneous fields per condition (p. 166). Not time-resolved.
  - **Particle lag:** strong shocks are smeared; Ross, Lourenco & Krothapalli (1994) found relaxation strongly dependent on particle size and shock strength (p. 162). Without shock cells the mean exit velocity agrees with the isentropic value to ±1 % (p. 162). The authors regard the lag in vortices and the near-wall region as not assessed but of "ancillary importance" (p. 162).
- **Analysis chain and data requirements.**
  - *Mean and RMS fields:* 40-snapshot means (authors: trends only), centreline profiles, and U_clm as a spatial average.
  - *Convection velocity from single snapshots* (p. 166–167). Structures are identified as regions of concentrated out-of-plane vorticity in one field. "Once a structure is identified, its convection velocity can be obtained easily from the velocity data": the paper does not say whether this is the velocity at the vorticity peak or a vorticity-weighted mean. The same group's later [Alkislar 2003](alkislar-2003-screeching-rectangular-jet-stereo-piv.md) uses a vorticity-weighted average (eq. 14 there), probably the same idea (my inference).
    - This needs no time resolution, but assumes that a vortex moves with the local fluid velocity at its centre. Each identified structure gives one point (Fig. 9, ≈ 23 points from 40 fields at NPR 3.7, h/d = 4, my count), and the average is taken over x.
  - *Entrainment:* instantaneous q along r/d = 1.5 (Figs 11–12).
  - *Acoustic spectra and OASPL* need the time-resolved microphone; no processing details are given.
  - *Tone map:* peaks picked from narrowband spectra over many h/d, with the "amplitude-dominant" tone marked (Fig. 20).
  - *Lift loss:* integration of mean surface pressures.
- **Modelling side.**
  - Powell's feedback formula, eq. (1), with C_i from PIV, C_a the ambient sound speed (my reading; not stated numerically), N an integer and p a free phase lag. The integral form is evaluated as h/C_i with an h-dependent mean C_i (Fig. 10).
    - My check: with C_i/U_clm from the Fig. 10 fit (≈ 0.50 at h/d_j = 2 rising to ≈ 0.56 at 10), U_clm ≈ 423 m s⁻¹ and a_∞ ≈ 343 m s⁻¹, the N = 2 line at h/d_j = 2 is 4.8 kHz and at 10 is 1.0 kHz. Fig. 20's p = 0 lines read 4.7 and 1.0 kHz. They also confirm that the abscissa is h over the 27.6 mm fully expanded diameter.
  - Tam's (1991) screech-frequency formula is used as the reference for the free-jet tones (Fig. 18). It is not printed in the paper; the curve matches the cold-jet form written out in the [Tam 1995](tam-1995-supersonic-jet-noise.md) note (my check: 0.77 at NPR 2.2, 0.32 at 3.7, 0.25 at 5.0).
  - Cited but not used: the linear-stability models of [Tam & Ahuja 1990](tam-1990-theoretical-model-discrete-tone-impinging-jets.md) and Kuo & Dowling (1996), which the authors call better models for frequency (p. 157). Also cited: How & Tam's (1998) computations of screech amplitude, and the image-source ground-reflection model of Sutherland & Brown (1972) (p. 157–158).
  - No stability analysis, LES, DNS or receptivity model. The phase lag is a fitted constant.

## Key equations
- **Powell's feedback formula** (eq. 1, p. 157; checked on the rendered page):
  $$\frac{N+p}{f_N}=\int_0^{h}\frac{\mathrm dh}{C_i}+\frac{h}{C_a}\qquad (N=1,2,3,\dots)$$
  Here h is the nozzle-to-wall distance, C_i the convection speed of the downstream-travelling large structures, C_a the speed of the upstream acoustic waves, N an integer, and p the phase lag "due to the fact that the phases of the acoustic wave and of the convected disturbance are not always exactly equal at both the nozzle exit and the source of the sound" (p. 157). The fit uses p = −0.4 (p. 177).
- **Equivalent delay** (my rearrangement, in the convention of [Weightman 2017](weightman-2017-phase-lag-supersonic-jet-impingement.md), eq. 3.1 there): $N/f = h/C_i + h/C_a + \Delta t$ with $\Delta t = -p/f$. For p = −0.4 that is 0.4 of a period: ≈ 71 µs at 5.65 kHz, ≈ 100 µs at 4 kHz and ≈ 175 µs at 2.3 kHz. That is 2–6 times the 29–40 µs shocklet delays of Weightman 2017, in a jet without a stand-off shock.
- **Trade-off between p and C_i** (my derivation). With $T = h/C_i + h/C_a$ and $f = (N+p)/T$, a fractional change δ in C_i changes T by $-s\,\delta$, where $s = (h/C_i)/T \approx 0.6$ here. Matching a shift Δp at stage N needs $\delta \approx \Delta p/[s\,(N+p)]$. For Δp = 0.4 at N ≈ 5, a C_i about 14 % lower (≈ 0.45–0.48U_clm instead of 0.52–0.56) closes the gap with p = 0.
  - The stage spacing (≈ 1/T) constrains C_i only weakly, to about 10 %. So p = −0.4 cannot be separated from a C_i error of this size using these data, as the authors concede (p. 178).
- **PIV local fit** (eq. 2, p. 161): $u = ax^2 + bx + cy^2 + dy + exy + f$, least-squares fitted to the scattered particle-pair displacements (the same form for v).
- **Analytic gradients** (eq. 3, p. 162): $\partial u/\partial x = 2ax + b + ey$, $\partial u/\partial y = 2cy + d + ex$. The fit is second-order accurate everywhere, including the derivatives used for vorticity.
- **Convective Mach number** (p. 167): $M_c = (U_{clm} - C_i)/a_j$, with a_j the jet sound speed.
- **Entrainment speed** (p. 167): $q = (u^2 + v^2)^{1/2}$.
- **Spatial mean centreline velocity** (p. 167, 171): U_clm is the mean of the free-jet centreline velocity over x/d = 0.2–5, used to normalise C_i because the shock cells make the centreline uneven.
- **Lift-loss correlation** (p. 173; Fig. 15): $(L - L_f)/T = 0.02\,[h/(D-d)]^{-2.24}$. L is the downward force with the ground plane, L_f the same for the free jet, T the isentropic thrust, D = 254 mm the lift-plate diameter and d = 25.4 mm the throat diameter. The fit covers h/(D − d) ≈ 0.35–1.1 at NPR 3.7.

## Notation
- d: nozzle throat diameter, 25.4 mm (also the convergent exit); d_j: fully expanded jet diameter (p. 172), ≈ 27.6 mm at NPR 3.7 (my calculation); D: lift-plate diameter, 254 mm (p. 160, 173). The introduction also writes h/D = 4 (p. 157), meaning h/d.
- h: distance from the nozzle exit (and lift plate) to the ground plane. The text and Figs 3–16 and 19 use h/d; Figs 14, 20 and 21 use h/d_j.
- x: axial distance from the exit; r: radial distance. In Figs 7–8 x is negative downstream.
- U_j: "fully expanded jet velocity" (p. 157) but "mean jet exit velocity" (p. 166, 180); U_clm: spatial mean free-jet centreline velocity over x/d = 0.2–5; U_cl: centreline velocity.
- C_i: convection velocity of the large structures; C_a: speed of the upstream acoustic waves; f_N: tone frequency of stage N; p: phase lag (cycles).
- M_c = (U_clm − C_i)/a_j: convective Mach number; a_j: jet sound speed; q: in-plane speed magnitude at r/d = 1.5; Ω_z: out-of-plane vorticity.
- L, L_f, T: downward force on the lift plate with and without the ground plane, and the isentropic thrust.
- NPR = p0/p∞; M_d: design Mach number; St = f D_j/U_j (Fig. 18 axis).

## Relevance to this project
This paper is the classical reference for an **ideally expanded** supersonic impinging jet and for the negative phase lag in Powell's equation. Bogey & Gojon's LES copied its conditions and use its tones for validation ([Bogey & Gojon 2017](bogey-2017-feedback-loop-upwind-waves-ideally-expanded-impinging-jets.md), p. 564, 572–577), and the user's thesis and its papers quote both its p = −0.4 and its convection speeds ([Weightman 2019](weightman-2019-sound-production-impinging-supersonic-jets.md), p. 15, 126; [Weightman 2017](weightman-2017-phase-lag-supersonic-jet-impingement.md), p. 2; [Weightman et al. 2019](weightman-2019-nozzle-external-geometry-azimuthal-mode-impinging-jet.md), cited on its u_c). It bears directly on the impinging-jet feedback thread in CLAUDE.md.

**The phase lag, and what it can and cannot show**
- **No stand-off shock, yet a lag.** In this jet the stand-off shock and its oscillation (Henderson & Powell 1993), and with it leakage and the wall-jet shocklet that need strong shocks ([Weightman 2017](weightman-2017-phase-lag-supersonic-jet-impingement.md); [Edgington-Mitchell 2025](edgington-mitchell-2025-vortices-shocks-nonlinear-waves-impinging-jet-resonance.md), p. 15–17), are absent or weak, yet p ≈ −0.4 is still needed.
  - So a negative p by itself is not evidence for a shocklet delay. Read as a delay, it is 70–175 µs, longer than the 29–40 µs shocklet life.
  - This supports the topic page's caution that a fitted p cannot identify the source. The candidate source for this jet is vortex–wall impact in the stagnation region, which Edgington-Mitchell 2025 class as an in-core source (p. 14–15). The paper's own front-curvature argument puts the source at the stagnation region too (p. 164).
- **Convection speed, N and p trade off.** A C_i about 14 % lower would make p = 0 work (Key equations). That value, 0.45–0.48U, is close to the 0.44–0.48U_j that [Weightman et al. 2019](weightman-2019-nozzle-external-geometry-azimuthal-mode-impinging-jet.md) obtained from POD wavelength times tone frequency (p. 432–433) and used with p = 0.
  - In the other direction, Bogey & Gojon's LES finds u_c rising from 0.5 to 0.65u_j along the lip line, with means of 0.58–0.62u_j, and p = 0 "approximately" reproducing these tones with different N (Bogey 2017 note, p. 571, 575–577).
  - This paper's C_i comes from the velocity at vorticity peaks in single snapshots, while the LES uses space–time correlation on r = r0. The two measure different things. Their 10–20 % gap, together with the missing near-wall leg, is enough to account for the whole of p.
  - Bogey & Gojon's standing-wave cell count fixes N independently of u_c. Comparing it with the N assignments here, the same tone at the same L/D, is a cheap test of whether p = −0.4 or a slower C_i is right. Not done here; it needs Bogey's Fig. 14(a).
- **A half-cycle reading** (my interpretation). The tones sit on average near N − 0.4 to N − 0.5, roughly half-way between integer-N lines. In the dispersive phase models, an end-condition phase φ = π, a sign change at one reflection, gives exactly a half-integer shift ([Jaunet 2019](jaunet-2019-dynamics-round-jet-impingement.md), p. 5; Jordan 2018 via the topic page). Here the lip is a large flat baffle, the lift plate, which may change the reflection or receptivity phase relative to a free lip, as the authors argue (p. 177). The plate-on and plate-off screech data (Fig. 18) show that it does change screech stage selection for the convergent nozzle at NPR 2.9–3.3 (my reading).
- **Upstream wave.** The paper assumes free-stream sound at a∞. At M_j 1.5 the guided-mode branches travel at 0.93–1.00a₀ (Bogey 2017 note's check, quoted on the topic page), which would account for only about 0.1 of a stage of p.

**Mode selection and the recurring St families**
- The dominant-tone St values cluster at 0.35–0.38 where the shadowgraphs show axisymmetric oscillation (h/d ≈ 4–6), at 0.25–0.30 where they show helical oscillation (h/d ≈ 3 and 8), and at 0.14–0.20 at intermediate heights (my conversion and matching).
  - This is the alternating band ordering of Bogey & Gojon's LES: axisymmetric < 0.225, helical 0.225–0.355, axisymmetric 0.355–0.5 (p. 577 there), and Tam & Ahuja's KH-band selection of guided modes ([Tam 1990](tam-1990-theoretical-model-discrete-tone-impinging-jets.md)).
  - It is also close to the group's recurring helical St ≈ 0.25 and toroidal St ≈ 0.385 at NPR 3.4 ([Weightman et al. 2019](weightman-2019-nozzle-external-geometry-azimuthal-mode-impinging-jet.md), p. 428–433).
- The lift plate is effectively an infinite lip (a baffle reaching ≈ 4 d_j beyond the exit), like the thesis's t/D 7 cap. Here it gives helical or axisymmetric tones depending on h/d, consistent with the thesis finding that the infinite lip carries both families. The band test on the topic page could be applied to these tones directly, now that they are tabulated.
- The low St 0.14–0.20 tones (N = 3–4) at h/d_j 4.6–7.6 lie at or below the S(0,1) limit of the m = 0 guided mode near M_j 1.5 (≈ 0.2; [Jaunet 2019](jaunet-2019-dynamics-round-jet-impingement.md) via the topic page). They would be candidates for guided (0, 1) closure, while the 0.35–0.38 axisymmetric tones would need the (0, 2) band (my reading; to be checked against cold bands at M_j 1.50).

**Methods and data for the lab workflow (CLAUDE.md)**
- **Non-time-resolved PIV.** The convection-velocity method (identify a vortex by its vorticity in a single snapshot and take the local velocity) works on the group's non-TR PIV and is independent of POD.
  - It is a useful cross-check on u_c from POD-pair phase averages ([Weightman 2019](weightman-2019-sound-production-impinging-supersonic-jets.md)) and from x–t diagrams in LES ([Karami 2020](karami-2020-acoustic-hydrodynamic-waves-underexpanded-impinging-jets.md)), which disagree by 30–40 % for the thesis case (topic page).
  - Its weaknesses: it assumes a vortex convects at its centre velocity; it has a choice of centre (peak or vorticity-weighted); it has no data in the last 20–25 mm before the wall; and it rests on 40 snapshots.
  - With the group's 1,000–10,000 snapshots, the same estimate could be made as a PDF of C_i(x) and compared with the k_x of a POD pair (u_c = 2πf/k_x).
- **Mean statistics** from 40 snapshots are trend-only (p. 170); the group's convergence tests suggest at least 500 for a POD pair (topic page, diagnostics).
- **Time-resolved acoustics.** The tone map comes from averaged narrowband spectra. Several tones appear at most heights (Figs 19–20), and small changes in h swap the dominant one, so averaged spectra cannot tell coexistence from switching.
  - Long microphone records with band-pass Hilbert envelopes, bicoherence of the harmonic sets (e.g. 5.65, 11.3 and 17.1 kHz at h/d = 4.25), and TR schlieren run through SPOD → reconstruction → Hilbert POD would show whether neighbouring stages (e.g. N = 4 and 7 near h/d_j 6) alternate or coexist, and whether the effective p wanders from cycle to cycle.
  - The ±0.2-stage scatter of N + p in Fig. 20 (my reading) is what cycle-to-cycle phase wander or C_i variation would look like in averaged data.
- **Shadowgraph** here is single-shot strobe video, enough for mode type and front curvature but not for timing.

**Modelling side and the PyFR LES**
- The paper has no model beyond Powell's equation and Tam's screech formula; it names Tam & Ahuja and Kuo & Dowling as the better linear models (p. 157).
- Its tone map is the standard validation set for an ideally expanded M_j 1.5 round impinging jet. An impinging extension of the PyFR set-up (`Simulations/PyFR_Staging_Continuation/`) could be checked against it as Bogey & Gojon did. Three caveats:
  - the lift-plate baffle at the exit is part of the geometry (exterior geometry matters; Weightman 2019, p. 89);
  - Re here is 7 × 10⁵, against 6 × 10⁴ in the LES;
  - the conical 3° nozzle produces weak shock cells that a shock-free inflow would miss.
- Screech with a reflecting baffle (Figs 17–18) is a reminder for the free-jet staging LES that the nozzle exterior changes amplitude and possibly stage selection.

**Rocket noise.** Impingement raises near-field OASPL by ≈ 8 dB here (p. 178–179), consistent with Harrier full-scale data and well below the 14 dB of an image-source model (p. 158). That bears on launch-pad reflection modelling. [Lubert 2022](lubert-2022-supersonic-jet-noise-launch-vehicles-review.md) cites this paper among the laboratory jets with strong impingement tones when discussing why launch data show none (p. 16–17 there, as cited on the topic page).
- The authors expect impingement tones to persist in hot, full-scale supersonic jets by analogy with hot-jet screech (p. 158). The topic page records no tones at launch-vehicle scale, so that expectation is untested for rockets.
- [Tsutsumi 2015](tsutsumi-2015-epsilon-liftoff-acoustics.md) cites this paper for K = U_c/U_e ≈ 0.5–0.7 in a Mach-wave-angle model. The values here are for a cold M 1.5 jet, normalised by U_clm and reduced by the plate.
