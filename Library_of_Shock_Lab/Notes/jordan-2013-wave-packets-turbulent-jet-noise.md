## Summary
The review argues that the low-frequency, low-angle (aft) noise of high-subsonic and supersonic jets comes from wavepackets: intermittent, advecting disturbances of low azimuthal order that stay correlated over distances far beyond the integral scale (p. 173). It traces their signatures through the far field (directivity, m = 0–2 content, bursts), the near-acoustic field (evanescent wavepackets that leak sound only through their envelope, or radiate Mach waves when supersonic) and the vortical region (conditional averages, forced jets, LSE) (p. 175–181). Section 3 casts them as modal solutions of the Navier–Stokes equations linearised about the turbulent mean, with nonlinearity lumped in a mode-dependent Reynolds stress, and reviews the evidence: LSA and PSE match azimuthally decomposed near-field pressure and time-resolved PIV to the end of the potential core, while laminar or forced simulations need nonlinear terms (p. 181–186). Section 4 covers "acoustic distillation" of the sound-producing motions, intermittency and its link to radiation efficiency, reduced-order models and noise control, arguing that wavepackets give the missing framework for control (p. 186–189). The open problems are the unproved scale-separation hypothesis behind mean-flow models, whether intermittency is deterministic or stochastic, how much of the sideline noise wavepackets carry, and how to build time-domain models for control (p. 181, 185, 188–190).

## Key findings
- **Far-field signatures** (§2.1, p. 175–177):
  - After removing the 1/R decay, the spectrum depends only on the polar angle φ (from the downstream axis). It peaks at 20° < φ < 40°, where the spectrum peaks near St = fD/U_j ≈ 0.2 and falls steeply. With increasing φ the level drops sharply, the spectrum broadens, and the peak moves up to St ≈ 0.4 by 90° (p. 176).
  - For cold jets up to moderately supersonic M_j, OASPL ∝ U_j^n with n = 8 at 90° and n > 8 at shallower angles (p. 176).
  - Below St = 1 the sound decomposes almost entirely into azimuthal modes m = 0, 1 and 2 (Cavalieri et al. 2011a,c; Juvé et al. 1979; Kopiev et al. 2010) (p. 176).
  - The noise is intermittent: it recurs at St ≈ 0.2 in temporally localised bursts, seen with time-local analysis or a wavelet basis (p. 176).
- **Why only m ≤ 2 radiate** (p. 176, Fig. 3): Michalke & Fuchs (1975) showed that the integral solution of the inhomogeneous wave equation carries a Bessel factor J_m(2π St M sin φ). For a source radially compact relative to the acoustic wavelength, only m = 0, 1, 2 survive whatever the axial structure; m = 0 dominates at low φ, and m = 1, 2 grow in importance at higher φ and St. Fig. 3c (Cavalieri et al. 2012a, St = 0.2) shows m = 0 within ≈ 1 dB of the total at φ = 20° (≈ 99 against ≈ 100 dB) and below m = 1 and m = 2 by 90° (read from figure).
- **Wavy line sources** reproduce the directivity and the n > 8 low-angle velocity exponent (Crow 1972; Michalke 1970, 1972; Michel 2009), and simple models show that intermittency of a wavepacket source raises its radiation efficiency (Cavalieri et al. 2011b; Ffowcs Williams & Kempton 1978; Sandham et al. 2006) (p. 176–177).
- **Source localisation is ill posed** from the far field alone; a wavepacket ansatz makes it tractable and returns envelope amplitude, wavelength, position and convection velocity (Koenig et al. 2012; Morris 2009; Papamoschou 2011). Cavalieri et al. (2012a) added LSA radial eigenfunctions and fitted m = 1 and m = 2 envelopes from azimuthally decomposed far-field data (p. 177).
- **Near-acoustic field** (§2.2, p. 177–179):
  - Turbulent pressure advects at U_c < U_j. For U_c < a_∞ the inner solution must be matched to evanescent waves, whose amplitude decays exponentially in r (p. 177).
  - Mollo-Christensen's two-point correlations (1963, 1967) first showed the wavepacket form; he called the jet a "semi-infinite antenna for sound" (p. 177).
  - Mechanism (Fig. 5, after Crighton & Huerre 1990): at fixed frequency a homogeneous subsonic wave is silent; spatial modulation of its amplitude spreads its wavenumber spectrum, and the part with |α| a_∞/ω < 1 radiates to φ = arccos(αa_∞/ω). The envelope sets how much leaks, and some envelopes give superdirectivity (pressure decaying exponentially with φ), as measured for m = 0 (Cavalieri et al. 2012a). A supersonic phase speed puts the spectral peak itself in the radiating range, which gives much stronger Mach waves (wavy-wall analogy, Tam 1995; antenna analogy, Crighton 1975) (p. 177).
  - Near to far is well posed (Kirchhoff surfaces, Lyrintzis 1994). Reba et al. (2010) projected the caged-array data of Suzuki & Colonius (2006) to the far field and recovered the aft-angle, low-frequency directivity reasonably, which they took as proof that the convecting wavepacket sets the aft peak. The missing end caps of the surface, which cut through the jet, are the main error (p. 177–178).
  - The 294-microphone, 10° half-cone array of Viswanathan et al. (2011) started 3D from the centreline, so for all but the lowest frequencies it recorded propagating sound, not the near field defined here. Its strong correlation with the aft far field, read by those authors as sources at 15–30D, is reinterpreted as low-order azimuthal acoustic waves passing from the array to the far field (p. 178).
  - The evanescent field is the footprint of the coherent turbulence, so it can serve as the trigger or reference for conditional averaging and other decompositions (p. 179).
- **Vortical region** (§2.3, p. 179–181):
  - Moore (1977) conditionally averaged schlieren (Re = 5 × 10⁵, M = 0.83) on spikes of m = 0 and m = 1 near-field pressure from six microphones and recovered axially extensive axisymmetric and helical patterns, although the trigger was at one axial station (Fig. 6b, p. 179–180).
  - Pressure correlations extend far beyond the integral scale (Fuchs 1972), and the convection velocity varies only weakly with position (p. 180).
  - Turbulent pressure energy lies in three azimuthal modes (Michalke & Fuchs 1975; Armstrong et al. 1977), but the velocity's azimuthal spectrum is broader. Even POD-filtered velocity fields do not show a wavepacket as the dominant structure (Bonnet et al. 1994; Freund & Colonius 2009; Glauser & George 1987; Jung et al. 2004; Sinha et al. 2010, 2011b; Tinney et al. 2008b) (p. 180).
  - Forced jets raise the latent order above the background: Hussain & Zaman (1981), forcing at St_D = 0.3, found a train of structures over six diameters, with coherent energy (from the triple decomposition) comparable to the background turbulence (p. 180).
  - In unforced jets the trains are intermittent and "puff-like" (Crow & Champagne 1971). The first three azimuthal velocity modes hold only about 15% of the low-frequency fluctuation energy (Michalke & Fuchs 1975), against about 50% in the single axisymmetric forced mode of Hussain & Zaman (1981); PIV of Cavalieri et al. (2012b) supports the estimate (p. 180).
  - LSE (Adrian 1977), extended to space–time sampling, gave the first definitive link in unforced jets between the vortex trains and the near-field wavepackets (Picard & Delville 2000; Fig. 7) (p. 180–181).
  - Conclusion of §2: the coherent velocity field of an unforced jet is a wavepacket with low m, a nearly constant convection velocity and coherence far beyond the integral scales. It holds little of the energy but dominates the aft-angle sound because its space–time coherence makes it far more acoustically efficient than the more energetic, disorganised turbulence (p. 181).
- **Against two separate mixing-noise sources** (p. 181): the authors reject Tam et al.'s (1996) two similarity spectra as evidence of two mechanisms. The measured shape evolves smoothly with φ, and the most energetic parts of aft and sideline spectra span similar bands, so the sideline noise may be the same wavepacket source radiating differently (Papamoschou 2011). They concede that the wavepacket share of sideline noise "remains to be quantified" (p. 189).
- **Mean-flow hypothesis** (§3, p. 181–182): models use a time-invariant base flow, an idealised profile or the measured mean, following Crighton & Gaster's (1976) idea that the turbulence sets up an "equivalent laminar flow profile" for the large scales. A rigorous scale-separation proof is still lacking, so the support is empirical (p. 181).
  - Too much amplification makes nonlinear saturation and pairing dominate. That is so for initially laminar shear layers (Bradshaw 1966) and strongly forced jets (Crow & Champagne 1971). The turbulent mean spreads much faster, so the total amplification at fixed frequency is much smaller, and with nonlinearity already in the mean flow the disturbance evolution may be close to linear (p. 181–182).
  - The review is restricted to convectively unstable jets; absolute instability of hot, low-speed jets (Huerre & Monkewitz 1990; Lesshafft et al. 2006) is left out (p. 182).
- **Hierarchy of linear models** (§3.1, p. 182–183): locally parallel LSA (Orr–Sommerfeld-type, Michalke 1984), for which a subsonic unheated round jet has at most one unstable family per m (Mattingly & Chang 1974); energy-integral (Chan 1974; Liu 1974; Mankbadi & Liu 1981) and multiple-scales (Crighton & Gaster 1976; Tam & Morris 1980) extensions to slowly diverging jets; PSE (Herbert 1997), which gives similar results more cheaply (Chang et al. 1993); and global analysis, as an eigenproblem for presumably damped modes (Nichols & Lele 2011) or a boundary-value problem with specified inlet forcing (Mohseni et al. 2002). Nonlinear PSE can keep a subset of the triads in R_{m,ω}, and an eddy viscosity can model the rest (p. 182–183).
- **Comparisons with experiments** (§3.2, p. 183–184):
  - 1970s comparisons agreed only qualitatively; Crighton & Gaster (1976) warned that apparent wavenumbers and growth rates depend on where and which variable is measured (p. 183).
  - Suzuki & Colonius (2006): a caged, azimuthally resolved array on a cone in the near-acoustic field gave wavelength, phase speed and growth rates in reasonable agreement with quasi-parallel LSA on the measured mean. That location naturally filters out small-scale turbulence (p. 183).
  - Gudmundsson & Colonius (2011): the leading POD mode of the array CSD at M = 0.5 (cold) follows PSE over the whole envelope, "even beyond the close of the potential core", and similar agreement holds at higher speed and with heating (Fig. 8a, p. 183–184). In Fig. 8a the St = 0.2, m = 0 PSE peak sits upstream of and below the data (read from figure), the low-St failure recorded in the [gudmundsson-2011](gudmundsson-2011-instability-wave-models-near-field-jets.md) note (p. 110 there).
  - Cavalieri et al. (2012b): PSE against time-resolved stereo PIV in cross planes, with the m = 0 amplitude calibrated by a centreline hot-wire traverse in the potential core. Agreement with the leading POD mode is "striking" up to the end of the core for a range of low frequencies; beyond it, nonlinearity or a too-weak mode for POD to isolate (or both) (Fig. 8b, M_j 0.4, x/D = 3, St = 0.5; p. 183–184).
- **Comparisons with simulations** (§3.3, p. 183–184): all are laminar or transitional, since LES was only then reaching thin, initially turbulent boundary layers (Bogey et al. 2011).
  - Mohseni et al. (2002), DNS of an M = 1.92 jet at Re ≈ 2,000: linear and nonlinear runs with the same inlet forcing agree at the most amplified frequency and mode, but the linear model under-amplifies the others substantially (p. 183–184).
  - Piot et al. (2006): linear PSE matches LES of an M = 0.7 jet near the most amplified frequencies to the end of the core (p. 184).
  - Cheung & Lele (2009), forced 2-D shear layer: nonlinear PSE is needed for the mean-flow correction and for subharmonic pairing, the dominant sound source there (p. 184).
  - Suponitsky et al. (2010, 2011), on the low-Re jet of Stromberg et al. (1980) with an artificial term to hold the base flow: with excitation at a few frequencies, sound from nonlinear difference modes dominates the sound of the linear mode at the difference frequency (p. 184).
  - Whether these conclusions carry to high-Re turbulent jets is unclear: laminar layers amplify far more, and the coherent wavepacket is a small fraction of the turbulent energy (p. 184).
- **Acoustic prediction from stability models** (§3.4, p. 184–185): three routes, direct, Kirchhoff surface, or equivalent source.
  - Mankbadi & Liu (1984) were first, with an energy-integral model and an equivalent source; with much empirical input they reproduced Lush's (1971) aft directivity, the peak shift with angle and the velocity scaling (p. 184–185).
  - For subsonic PSE neither a Kirchhoff surface nor an equivalent source is straightforward, because both are sensitive to parabolisation error; the near-acoustic evanescent field is roughly right, though (p. 185).
  - Colonius et al. (2010) projected linear PSE from a conical near-field surface with a tailored Green's function (Reba et al. 2010) and matched the axisymmetric, low-frequency, aft-angle sound of a heated M_∞ = 0.9 jet reasonably. Sensitivity to the surface position and other conditions was untested (p. 185).
  - Supersonic phase speeds are easier: PSE then approximates Tam's matched-asymptotic Mach-wave theory (Tam 1995) and, calibrated by an initial disturbance spectrum, gives the low-frequency aft directivity (Balakumar 1998; Tam & Chen 1979; Zaitsev et al. 2009). PSE agreed generally well with linearised Euler for supersonic modes (Piot et al. 2006), and global modes predict both structures and radiation in subsonic and supersonic jets (Nichols & Lele 2011) (p. 185).
- **What linear success means** (§3.5, p. 185–186): linear models of turbulent jets agree well enough to pursue, but that does not make turbulence generation linear; using the turbulent mean as base state is itself an admission that nonlinearity is essential. Future work should test scale separation and model the nonlinear interactions, for instance as a frequency-dependent Reynolds stress. Dynamical (control) models must handle intermittency, not just mean fluctuations, and a linear model may not suffice. Few studies yet process turbulent data properly to extract the coherent motion for comparison (p. 185–186).
- **Acoustic distillation** (§4.1, p. 186): reducing the flow to the minimum that makes the same sound. Two-point flow–acoustic correlations (Lee & Ribner 1972; Seiner & Reethof 1974; Juvé et al. 1980; Panda et al. 2005; Bogey & Bailly 2007; Tam et al. 2008) give limited insight because wavepackets are extensive, and a causal reading needs a source model written in a locally measurable quantity.
  - Hileman et al. (2005) conditioned high-speed condensation images of an M_j = 1.28 jet on the far-field sound: loud windows coincide with rapid breakdown of wavepackets near the end of the potential core, as do correlation and LES/DNS studies (p. 186).
  - Freund & Colonius (2009), POD of an M = 0.9 DNS: pressure POD (L2 norm) gives wavepacket modes but needs many of them for the far field; an inner product weighted to the far field resolves it with fewer modes, which are wavepackets that decay faster near the end of the core. Similar "acoustically optimised" modes: Kerhervé et al. 2012b, Schlegel et al. 2012 (p. 186).
  - Kerhervé et al. (2012b): LSE from multipoint flow–acoustic correlations in an M_j = 0.9 LES (Bogey & Bailly 2006) educes the velocity and pressure tied to low-angle sound. The result is an axially extensive, intermittent wavepacket whose growth rate and radial shape match quasi-parallel LSA on the mean flow for a range of low frequencies and positions (Fig. 9). A line-source model with a fitted wavepacket whose amplitude and extent follow the STFT of the educed field reproduces the LES aft radiation (Cavalieri et al. 2011b). Tinney et al. (2008a) did the same with TR-PIV and near-nozzle near-field pressure (p. 186–187).
- **Intermittency: loud and quiet flows** (§4.2, p. 187–188):
  - Crow & Champagne described three or four puffs forming and moving downstream, a spell of confused flow, then more puffs (p. 187). This behaviour correlates with far-field bursts (Juvé et al. 1980; Guj et al. 2003; Hileman et al. 2005; Cavalieri et al. 2011a; Kerhervé et al. 2012b; Koenig et al. 2012) (p. 187).
  - Adjoint optimal control of a 2-D mixing layer (Wei & Freund 2006) made the wavepackets more regular and the flow quieter; Cavalieri et al. (2010) showed that the order-of-magnitude reduction came from removing one particular vortex merger with a loud emission. Early 3-D LES of jets (Freund 2011; Kim et al. 2011, M 1.3) suggest the same route works (p. 187).
  - Temporal modulation raises acoustic efficiency, especially at high subsonic Mach number (Sandham et al. 2006; Cavalieri et al. 2011b) (p. 187).
  - Open question: is the intermittency a deterministic nonlinear mechanism or a result of stochastic broadband inlet forcing? Even a linear system can produce it when two waves of similar frequency happen to reinforce or cancel (p. 188).
- **From statistics to dynamics** (§4.3, p. 188): linear PSE renders only the time-averaged wavepacket, and does not show that the time-local dynamics are linear; a time-domain operator is needed for real-time sensing and control. A reduced-order model needs a truncated basis (stability eigenfunctions, POD or other empirical modes) and a dynamic ansatz; plain POD–Galerkin models can be fragile or unstable (Holmes et al. 1998; Noack et al. 2011).
  - Sinha et al. (2010, 2011a,b): a stable 35-dimensional ROM from transonic-jet data that includes plasma forcing, validated for Kalman-filter estimation.
  - Kerhervé et al. (2012a): a 20-degree-of-freedom ROM on acoustically optimised POD modes reproduces the intermittent wavepacket evolution and, with the Cavalieri et al. (2011b) line source, aft radiation similar to the LES. Schlegel et al. (2009) added actuation to such a model.
- **Noise control** (§4.4, p. 188–189):
  - Steady devices (tabs, chevrons, non-circular nozzles, fluidic injection) have been limited by the lack of a framework linking near-nozzle perturbations to the far field (p. 188).
  - Unsteady actuation: pulsed plasma actuators up to St ≈ 3, phased around the lip to approximate m up to 3, gave the largest reductions at higher frequency and higher m (Kearney-Fischer et al. 2011) (p. 188–189).
  - Two candidate mechanisms: shifting azimuthal energy away from the efficient low-order modes, or small-scale forcing whose Reynolds stresses spread the mean faster and so reduce wavepacket amplification (p. 189).
  - Evidence: chevron mean flows cut the amplification and phase speed of low-order modes (Gudmundsson 2010). Koenig (2011), with steady injection from a rotating centre body exciting m = 2: forcing at frequencies that parallel LSA calls unstable raised the noise, raised the coherent Reynolds stress and showed quadratic interactions in the bicoherence; forcing at stable frequencies lowered the noise, raised the incoherent Reynolds stress, spread the mean flow faster and reduced m = 0 amplification, confirmed in velocity data (p. 189).
- **Summary points** (p. 189): wavepackets are seen in natural and forced jets; their near field is evanescent (subsonic U_c) or Mach-wave radiating (supersonic); they are a small part of the energy but dominate the low-frequency (at least to St = 1) aft-angle sound through coherence and intermittency; in unforced turbulent jets linearised stability on the mean matches them at least to the end of the potential core, while forced and initially laminar jets have significant nonlinear interactions; and the framework may explain noise reduction self-consistently.
- **Future issues** (p. 190): richer databases (real-time PIV, simultaneous flow–acoustic measurements) and new post-processing; measurement and simulation of the upstream and near-nozzle flow to establish inlet sensitivity; real-time modelling and control guided by theory; and direct prediction from linear and nonlinear disturbance equations plus low-order models built on acoustically efficient modes.
- **Sidebar, upstream sensitivity** (p. 175): the Re above which jets and their noise become Re-independent is disputed. Viswanathan (2004) varied Re and T_j/T_∞ independently, suggested that earlier "heating" effects were Re effects, and proposed Re > 4 × 10⁵. Harper-Bourne (2010) and Zaman (2011) found sensitivity to nozzle contraction ratio even with nominally turbulent boundary layers; lab nozzles, with high contraction ratios and thin layers, make more high-frequency noise than industrial ones. Whether this works through the wavepackets, and can be used for control, is raised as a question (p. 175).

- **Coordinates** (Fig. 2, p. 175): cylindrical (x, r, θ) on the jet axis, spherical (R, φ) for the far field, with φ the polar angle from the downstream axis. Nozzle-exit parameters Re = ρ_jU_jD/μ_j (above about 10⁵ in labs and applications), T_j/T_∞, M_j = U_j/a_j or M_∞ = U_j/a_∞ (p. 174).
- **Modal framework** (§3.1, p. 182): Reynolds decomposition; azimuthal and temporal Fourier modes of the fluctuation; the mean satisfies RANS (approximately) and each mode obeys the mean-flow-linearised operator forced by a generalised mode-dependent Reynolds stress (eqs. 1–3). Ensemble averaging eq. 3 for stochastic inlet forcing leaves the left side unchanged. Neglecting R_{m,ω} for the low-m, low-ω modes only gives the Crighton & Gaster (1976) linear theory.
- **Method dependence on data (as reviewed):**
  - *Far-field azimuthal decomposition and bursts* need synchronous microphone rings; burst detection needs time-resolved signals (STFT, wavelets) (p. 176).
  - *Near-field array plus POD of the cross-spectral matrix* (Gudmundsson & Colonius 2011) is a frequency-domain POD per (m, St), i.e. SPOD in today's terms, and needs time-resolved, azimuthally resolved pressure (p. 183).
  - *TR stereo PIV in cross planes* allows azimuthal and temporal Fourier transforms at once; the PSE amplitude was fixed by a single hot-wire traverse, so one free constant per (m, ω) remains (p. 183).
  - *Conditional averaging* on near-field triggers works on non-time-resolved images (Moore 1977) but depends on a subjective threshold; *forcing* gives an objective phase reference (phase averaging, triple decomposition) but changes the flow; *LSE* replaces the threshold with correlations, and its space–time and spectral forms (Tinney et al. 2006) need time-resolved reference signals (p. 180).
  - *Acoustically weighted POD* needs simultaneous flow and far-field data (DNS/LES) (p. 186).
- **Modelling side** in this review is limited to linear and weakly nonlinear disturbance equations about mean flows, simple line-source models and ROMs; there are no shocks, no screech, no BBSAN and no resonance (trapped acoustic waves came later; see [cavalieri-2019](cavalieri-2019-wave-packet-models-jet-dynamics-sound-radiation.md)).

## Key equations
Checked against the rendered pages 174–182.

- **Nozzle-exit parameters** (p. 174): $\mathrm{Re}=\rho_jU_jD/\mu_j$, $M_j=U_j/a_j$, $M_\infty=U_j/a_\infty$; Strouhal number $\mathrm{St}=fD/U_j$ (p. 176).
- **Azimuthal radiation factor** (p. 176): far-field amplitude of azimuthal mode m of a radially compact source ∝ $J_m(2\pi\,\mathrm{St}\,M\sin\phi)$ (Michalke & Fuchs 1975).
  > Check (mine): for a ring source of radius $r_s$, the argument is $k r_s\sin\phi = 2\pi\,\mathrm{St}\,M\,(r_s/D)\sin\phi$ with $k=\omega/a_\infty$ and $M=U_j/a_\infty$. The text's $2\pi$ corresponds to $r_s=D$. Fig. 3 instead uses $J_m(\pi\,\mathrm{St}\,M\sin\phi)$, i.e. $r_s=D/2$ (lipline): its axis reads "St M π sin φ", the m = 0 zero sits at St M sin φ ≈ 0.77 (= 2.405/π), and at M = 0.9, φ = 30° the m = 1 level relative to m = 0 is −16.9 dB at St 0.2 and −8.5 dB at St 0.5 from $J_1/J_0(\pi\,\mathrm{St}\,M\sin\phi)$, matching Fig. 3a (≈ −17 and ≈ −8.5 dB), against −10.6 dB at St 0.2 with $2\pi$. The ring at D/2 is also what [cavalieri-2013](cavalieri-2013-wavepackets-velocity-field-turbulent-jets.md) used for its Fig. 9 (M 0.6).
- **Acoustic matching** (p. 177): a wavenumber component α at frequency ω radiates to $\phi=\arccos(\alpha a_\infty/\omega)$, so only $|\alpha|a_\infty/\omega\le 1$ (supersonic phase speed) radiates.
- **Evanescent radial decay** (Fig. 4 caption, p. 178): exponential decay "associated with $J_m(\mathrm i\beta r)$", $\beta=\sqrt{\alpha^2-(\omega/a_\infty)^2}$, with α the measured local wavenumber.
  > To verify: $J_m(\mathrm i\beta r)=\mathrm i^mI_m(\beta r)$ grows with r. The decaying outer solution is the outgoing Hankel function $H_m^{(1)}(\mathrm i\beta r)\propto K_m(\beta r)$, which is what the decaying curves in Fig. 4d must show. Treat "J_m" in the caption as a typo or loose notation.
- **Reynolds decomposition and modes** (eq. 1, p. 182):
  $$\mathbf q(\mathbf x,t)=\bar{\mathbf q}(\mathbf x)+\mathbf q'(\mathbf x,t),\qquad \mathbf q'(x,r,\theta,t)=\sum_m\sum_\omega\tilde{\mathbf q}_{m,\omega}(x,r)\,e^{\mathrm im\theta}e^{-\mathrm i\omega t}.$$
- **Mean and mode equations** (eqs. 2–3, p. 182), with $\mathcal N(\mathbf q)=0$ the compressible Navier–Stokes, continuity and energy equations:
  $$\mathcal L(\bar{\mathbf q})=R_{0,0},\qquad \mathcal L(\tilde{\mathbf q}_{m,\omega})=R_{m,\omega}\quad\forall m\ \text{and}\ \omega\ne0,m,$$
  where $\mathcal L$ is $\mathcal N$ linearised about the mean and $R_{m,\omega}$ a generalised mode-dependent Reynolds stress. $R_{0,0}=0$ for laminar solutions and $\ne0$ for turbulent means (p. 182). Eq. 3 is exact; dropping $R_{m,\omega}$ for the low (m, ω) gives linear theory.
  > To verify against the PDF: eq. 2 is printed with $\mathcal L$, but the RANS equations are the nonlinear operator applied to the mean, $\mathcal N(\bar{\mathbf q})$ (the text calls eq. 2 "the standard Reynolds-averaged equations"). The condition "ω ≠ 0, m" in eq. 3 presumably means every (m, ω) except the mean (0, 0). Both look like loose notation rather than errors of substance.
- No other numbered equations. The superdirective and Mach-wave directivity forms are given only in words (p. 177); the closed form for a Gaussian wavepacket, $\mathrm{PSD}\propto\exp[-\tfrac12k_h^2L^2(1-M_c\cos\theta)^2]$, is in [cavalieri-2019](cavalieri-2019-wave-packet-models-jet-dynamics-sound-radiation.md) (eq. 37, p. 11 there).

## Notation
- D: nozzle diameter; x, r, θ: axial, radial, azimuthal coordinates; R, φ: far-field distance and polar angle from the downstream axis.
- U_j, ρ_j, μ_j, T_j, a_j: jet exit values; a_∞, T_∞: ambient; M_j = U_j/a_j; M_∞ (or M) = U_j/a_∞; Re = ρ_jU_jD/μ_j; St = fD/U_j (St_D for forcing frequency).
- U_c: convection (phase) velocity of the wavepacket; α: axial wavenumber; ω: angular frequency; m: azimuthal mode number; β: radial decay rate of the evanescent field.
- q, q̄, q′, q̃_{m,ω}: state vector, long-time mean, fluctuation, Fourier mode; 𝒩: Navier–Stokes operator; ℒ: 𝒩 linearised about the mean; R_{m,ω}: generalised Reynolds stress (nonlinear forcing) of mode (m, ω).
- CSD: cross-spectral density; LSE: linear stochastic estimation; LST/LSA: linear stability theory/analysis; PSE: parabolised stability equations; TR-PIV: time-resolved PIV; ROM: reduced-order model; STFT: short-time Fourier transform.
- "Aft angles" here means small φ, the downstream sector where the noise peaks.

## Relevance to this project
This is the 2013 manifesto of the wavepacket programme (Poitiers and Caltech) that most of the library's jet-noise and linear-model notes later built on. It predates SPOD-as-standard ([towne-2018](towne-2018-spod-relationship-dmd-resolvent.md)), jet resolvent analysis ([schmidt-2018](schmidt-2018-spectral-analysis-jet-turbulence.md); [lesshafft-2019](lesshafft-2019-resolvent-modelling-wavepackets-turbulent-jet.md)) and trapped acoustic waves ([towne-2017](towne-2017-acoustic-resonance-potential-core-subsonic-jets.md); [schmidt-2017](schmidt-2017-wavepackets-trapped-acoustic-modes-turbulent-jet.md)), and its successor is [cavalieri-2019](cavalieri-2019-wave-packet-models-jet-dynamics-sound-radiation.md). It is about shock-free mixing noise: there is no screech, BBSAN, feedback or impingement, which matters for reading it against the lab's work (CLAUDE.md, "My Research Area").

**How it fits and what changed afterwards**
- *Its open problems, and their later answers:*
  - The unproved Crighton–Gaster hypothesis (p. 181) was reframed by [mckeon-2010](mckeon-2010-critical-layer-framework-turbulent-pipe-flow.md) and the resolvent work: eq. 3 here, with R_{m,ω} as forcing of the mean-flow operator, is exactly the resolvent set-up, and the "modelled, frequency-dependent Reynolds stress" asked for on p. 185 became eddy-viscosity and coloured-forcing models ([pickering-2021](pickering-2021-optimal-eddy-viscosity-resolvent-jets.md); [morra-2021](morra-2021-colour-of-forcing-resolvent-channel.md)).
  - The low-St failure of PSE visible in Fig. 8a (St 0.2, m = 0) was later traced to the Orr mechanism, which PSE cannot carry ([schmidt-2018](schmidt-2018-spectral-analysis-jet-turbulence.md); [pickering-2020](pickering-2020-lift-up-kh-orr-jets.md)).
  - "Intermittency enhances efficiency" (p. 177, 187) became the coherence-decay (jitter) result: about 30 dB more subsonic sound for a coherence length equal to the envelope length, and almost no effect at M 2 ([cavalieri-2019](cavalieri-2019-wave-packet-models-jet-dynamics-sound-radiation.md), p. 15).
  - The statement that POD of velocity does not give wavepackets (p. 180) is explained by space-only POD mixing frequencies; SPOD modes are space–time coherent and do show them ([towne-2018](towne-2018-spod-relationship-dmd-resolvent.md)). The array "POD of the CSD" of Gudmundsson & Colonius (p. 183) was already SPOD in substance (gudmundsson-2011 note).
  - The deterministic-vs-stochastic question (p. 188) is still open. A time-domain answer is now possible: HPOD of an M 0.9 jet LES shows bursts at a preferred frequency separated by quiet periods ([raiola-2025](raiola-2025-hilbert-pod.md), p. 19–21).
- *Agreements:* linear PSE on the turbulent mean to the end of the core agrees with [gudmundsson-2011](gudmundsson-2011-instability-wave-models-near-field-jets.md) and [cavalieri-2013](cavalieri-2013-wavepackets-velocity-field-turbulent-jets.md), which are the full versions of the evidence summarised here. The m ≤ 2 compactness argument is the one [cavalieri-2013](cavalieri-2013-wavepackets-velocity-field-turbulent-jets.md) uses to explain why velocity energy at m ≈ 4–11 is silent. The control results (higher-m, higher-St forcing quieter) agree with [samimy-2006](samimy-2006-plasma-actuators-high-speed-jet-flow-noise-control.md) (m = ±1 at St_DF ≈ 0.3 adds 4–5 dB; high St_DF or m cuts about 1 dB). The convective-only restriction (p. 182) agrees with [huerre-1990](huerre-1990-local-global-instabilities-spatially-developing-flows.md); the "turbulent mean spreads faster, so the gain is small" argument (p. 181–182) is the same as Michalke's divergence-capped gain ([michalke-1984](michalke-1984-survey-jet-instability-theory.md), p. 190–191).
- *Disagreement:* it rejects the two-source reading of [tam-1996](tam-1996-two-components-turbulent-mixing-noise-supersonic-jets.md) (p. 181), the earliest statement in the library of the "one jittering wavepacket" side of that debate, but without a quantitative test (p. 189). [tam-1995](tam-1995-supersonic-jet-noise.md) treats the large-scale part the same way (instability waves) but keeps a separate fine-scale source.

**For the lab workflow (CLAUDE.md)**
- *Time-resolved data* (schlieren, acoustics, LES): SPOD per (m, St) is the modern form of the array POD reviewed here; the evanescent near field is a natural reference signal for conditioning. Far-field azimuthal decomposition needs a microphone ring. Bursts need STFT, wavelets or Hilbert-based tracking, which block-averaged SPOD smears.
- *Non-time-resolved data* (snapshot PIV, single-shot schlieren): Moore-style conditional averages on a synchronised near-field microphone and LSE from a time-resolved sensor are the routes that work without time-resolved fields (p. 179–180); a POD pair suits a tone, not a broadband wavepacket.
- *SPOD → reconstruction → HPOD:* §4.2's question is exactly what the pipeline can test. Reconstruct the m = 0 or m = 1 wavepacket band from leading SPOD modes, track its instantaneous amplitude with HPOD, and correlate that with far-field bursts at the matched delay, as in Fig. 9 (iii)–(vi). Two close frequencies beating (p. 188) would show up as an envelope period of 1/Δf (time-frequency-methods topic); a nonlinear mechanism would not have that signature. In screeching jets the same logic separates tone-amplitude modulation from broadband wavepacket intermittency.

**Modelling side and the PyFR staging LES**
- The hierarchy in §3.1 (local LSA, multiple scales, PSE, global/boundary-value problems) is the menu the lab's linear models use; for shock-containing jets the Floquet and PFE extensions are on the shock-cell-models and linear-operator-methods topics. The warning that apparent wavenumbers and growth rates depend on the variable and the measurement position (p. 183) applies directly when comparing PIV, schlieren and LES with LSA.
- The Kirchhoff/near-field projection points (p. 177–178, 185) matter for any FW-H or Kirchhoff surface on the PyFR LES: keep the surface outside the vortical region but close enough to capture the evanescent field, and treat end caps that cross the jet with care.
- The PyFR runs at M_j ≈ 1.1 have U_c < a_∞, so their broadband mixing noise should follow the subsonic (superdirective, jitter-sensitive) branch reviewed here, while the screech tone and BBSAN do not.

**Rocket noise**
- Rocket plumes are hot and supersonic, so their dominant noise is the Mach-wave branch (supersonic U_c), for which this review says PSE is accurate once calibrated (p. 185) and [cavalieri-2019](cavalieri-2019-wave-packet-models-jet-dynamics-sound-radiation.md) says one coherent wavepacket per frequency suffices. That supports wavepacket-based Mach-wave source models over SP-8072-type point sources, but the review contains no data above M_j 1.92 or at rocket temperatures, and its peak-St and directivity numbers are for cold lab jets (see [lubert-2022](lubert-2022-supersonic-jet-noise-launch-vehicles-review.md) for the tenfold lower rocket Sr).
- The Viswanathan reinterpretation (p. 178) is a caution for source-location claims from arrays that sit in the propagating field, relevant to apparent-source-location fits in rocket-noise models.
