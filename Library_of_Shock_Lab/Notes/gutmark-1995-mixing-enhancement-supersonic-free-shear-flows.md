## Summary
Gutmark, Schadow and Yu review how to speed up mixing in supersonic free shear flows, whose growth rate falls to about 20 % of the incompressible value once the convective Mach number M_c exceeds about 0.8 (Fig. 1, p. 379). They first summarise what coherent structures do in subsonic layers and jets (p. 376–378). They then cover what compressibility changes: linear stability theory (2-D waves stabilise, oblique and helical waves take over, confinement and shocks add new modes), the reference Mach numbers, the loss of organised 2-D structures and pairing above M_c ≈ 0.5–0.6, and the still-unsettled effect on fine-scale mixing (p. 378–384). The core of the review sorts enhancement methods into four families: excitation of shear-layer instabilities (passive and active), three-dimensional nozzles, streamwise (axial) vorticity, and shock–shear-layer interaction (p. 384–408). Two tables list their conditions and the gain each achieved (Tables 1–2, p. 386–388), and Fig. 1 plots some of them against the unforced growth-rate band. Methods that amplify 2-D waves lose effect as M_c rises, while those that inject streamwise vorticity or work through baroclinic torque hold up or improve, although most methods were tested at too few conditions to prove it (p. 409–410). Screech recurs throughout: it is what makes underexpanded rectangular jets spread fast (St_D ≈ 0.12 flapping), it can be strengthened by paddles, cylinders, cavities and collars to raise mixing, and suppressing it lowers mixing (p. 384–392, 395). The authors close by warning that nearly all methods were judged on the growth rate of the layer, an upper bound on mixed fluid, and that stirring and molecular mixing, which matter for combustion, were rarely measured (p. 412).

## Key findings
- **Subsonic background** (§1.1–1.2, p. 376–378):
  - Large-scale 2-D structures from KH instability control entrainment and mixing in all free shear flows (Brown & Roshko 1974). Linear theory puts the initially amplified band at 0 < St_i = f_i θ₀/U₀ < 0.04 (Michalke 1965) (p. 376).
  - Pairing spreads the layer and lowers the structure frequency. Entrained fluid stays largely unmixed during a vortex's life (Dimotakis & Brown 1976), but pairing mixes intensely. A secondary spanwise instability gives streamwise "ribs" and the "mixing transition" (Bernal & Roshko 1986; Breidenthal 1980) (p. 376–377).
  - In a jet the potential core of an initially laminar jet ends at about 5 diameters (or slot widths). The jet-column (preferred) mode there has St_D = 0.25–0.5, typically the second or third subharmonic of the initial shear-layer frequency (Crow & Champagne 1971; Ho & Huerre 1984). Jets also carry azimuthal modes whose interactions break the structures down (Cohen & Wygnanski 1987) (p. 377).
  - Large structures help bulk mixing but hinder fine-scale mixing; exciting a combination of modes can enhance both (p. 377).
  - Passive control changes the geometry at separation (trip wires, convoluted splitter plates, square, rectangular and elliptic nozzles). Active control forces the flow mechanically, acoustically, hydrodynamically, thermally or by free-stream modulation, and works best inside the natural instability band. Crow & Champagne's preferred-mode forcing raised jet growth; the right frequency can enhance or suppress growth (Oster & Wygnanski 1982; Ho & Huang 1982) (p. 377–378).
- **Compressibility** (§1.3, p. 378–380):
  - M_c is the Mach number of each stream relative to the large structures. With equal static pressure and γ the two values are equal, M_c = (U₁ − U₂)/(a₁ + a₂) (Bogdanoff 1983; Papamoschou & Roshko 1988) (p. 378).
  - Layers below M_c ≈ 0.5 look incompressible, with 2-D organised structures (Clemens & Mungal 1992a). Pairing was seen at M_c = 0.51 but not at 0.86 (Elliott et al. 1993). At higher M_c the layer becomes highly 3-D with hardly any organised 2-D structure; reacting jets behave the same (Barlow et al. 1992) (p. 378).
  - **Growth-rate collapse** (Fig. 1, p. 379): the normalised spreading rate falls to an asymptote close to 20 % of the incompressible value for M_c > 0.8. The band combines Chinzei et al. 1986, Papamoschou & Roshko 1988, Hall et al. 1991 and Clemens & Mungal 1992a.
  - One proposed cause is the loss of upstream and cross-stream acoustic communication inside the layer at high Mach number (Morkovin 1987; Papamoschou 1990) (p. 379).
  - Above M_c ≈ 0.5 the structures convect at close to U₁ or U₂ instead of an intermediate speed (Papamoschou 1991), which contradicts the stagnation-point model behind M_c. Shocklets on one side of the structures, giving asymmetric convection and entrainment, are a possible explanation (Hussaini et al. 1986; Lele 1989; Dimotakis 1991), but there was no direct experimental evidence for them (p. 379–380).
  - Density ratio alone cannot explain the drop: a density ratio of 49 changes incompressible growth by less than a factor of 2 (Brown & Roshko 1974). Shocks crossing density gradients add baroclinic vorticity (p. 380).
- **Linear stability theory** (§2.1, p. 380–381):
  - *Planar layers.* LST supports M_c as the correlating parameter (Sandham & Reynolds 1990; Zhuang et al. 1990). The amplification of 2-D waves falls with M_c and tends to zero as M_c → ∞ (Ragab & Wu 1989; Zhuang et al. 1990). The trend matches experiment, but measured growth levels off at about 20 % instead of zero (p. 380).
  - Three explanations are offered for the mismatch (p. 380–381):
    1. Oblique waves are more amplified as M_c rises, and above M_c ≈ 0.6 the layer amplifies 3-D disturbances most (Groppengiesser 1970; Sandham & Reynolds 1990).
    2. LST is for unbounded layers, experiments are confined. Vortices moving supersonically relative to a stream radiate sound that reflects from the walls, and coupling with the duct's acoustic modes creates new, mostly 3-D, unstable modes (Soetrisno et al. 1990; Tam & Hu 1991).
    3. At supersonic M_c, shocks appear, development is nonlinear, and vortex formation is predicted to be suppressed (Ragab & Sheen 1992).
  - *Round jets* (p. 381): Michalke (1971) found the axisymmetric mode stabilising as M rises. Heating destabilises subsonic jets but stabilises supersonic ones (Koshigoe et al. 1987). Viswanathan & Morris (1992) confirmed this, found the helical mode can dominate at supersonic speeds, and, with helical waves included, obtained an asymptote of **50 %** of the incompressible growth rate for M_c > 0.75, against 20 % for planar layers.
  - *Non-circular jets* (p. 381): eccentricity (aspect ratio), the azimuthal distribution of momentum thickness and the local radius of curvature matter (Koshigoe et al. 1988). Three necessary conditions are given for vortex deformation, self-induction and axis switching to enhance mixing: (1) eigenfunctions localised without excessive overlap; (2) comparable amplification rates for different eigenmodes; (3) sufficient phase-speed difference between those eigenmodes.
  - *Jets, mode competition* (§2.3.2, p. 383): above M ≈ 0.8 the helical mode dominates when R/θ ≤ 6; for R/θ > 100 both modes are equally amplified, which the authors use to explain why both appear in low- and high-Re jets (McLaughlin et al. 1975; Lepicovsky et al. 1987). In cold and moderately hot jets (temperature ratio up to 2.5) the large structures are KH instability waves.
- **Reference Mach numbers** (§2.2, p. 381–382):
  - The relative Mach number M_r = 2(U₁ − U₂)/(a₁ + a₂) (Rudy & Birch 1974) and M_c both correlate growth "reasonably well". M_c is more useful when γ differs between streams; with equal γ, M_r = 2M_c (p. 381–382).
  - M_c's convection speed comes from equating the stagnation (dynamic) pressures of the two streams in the structure frame. That assumes organised structures with stagnation points and isentropic flow, so M_c "may not be a physical quantity" for highly compressible, disorganised, 3-D layers (p. 382).
  - Dimotakis (1991) modified the model for shocklets with a shock-strength parameter. Barre (1994) found the eddy-shock model applies only to confined layers with shear-layer thickness above about 0.1 of the test-section height (p. 382).
- **Large-scale structures** (§2.3, p. 382–384):
  - *Planar layers.* Organised 2-D structures were seen only at low M_c (0.28, Clemens & Mungal 1992a; 0.51, Elliott et al. 1992), with braids. Unlike incompressible layers, streamwise and spanwise structures have the same scale. Above M_c ≈ 0.6 the layer is highly 3-D with some oblique structures (p. 382).
  - Schlieren shows elongated structures tilted with the fast stream, without pairing (Chinzei et al. 1986; Papamoschou & Roshko 1988). Mach-wave radiation into one stream is indirect evidence of structures convecting supersonically relative to it (Hall et al. 1993) (p. 382–383).
  - Simulations agree: reduced coherence, 3-D structure, no pairing (Guirguis et al. 1987; Sandham & Reynolds 1990), and shocks in the layer, stronger when confined (Soetrisno et al. 1990; Ragab & Sheen 1992) (p. 383).
  - The "colayer" model of Planche & Reynolds (1992), from LST and DNS of reacting layers: two sets of structures, one on each side, each mixing with its own stream, driven by baroclinic torque, without roll-up or pairing (p. 383).
  - *Jets.* Schlieren of supersonic jets at M_c 0.08–2.2 shows poorly organised structures leaning towards the fast stream (McIntyre & Settles 1991; Gutmark et al. 1991b). For M_c > 1 the structures convect supersonically relative to one stream; the broadband spectral peak moves to lower frequency downstream as they grow (McIntyre & Settles 1991) (p. 383).
  - *Off-design jets* (p. 383–384): shock-cell–shear-layer interaction raises spreading (Glass 1968), and larger pressure mismatch changes spreading and internal structure a great deal. The authors attribute this to acoustic feedback from shock–vortex interactions downstream, forcing the shear layer at the lip (Powell 1953; Umeda et al. 1987). Compressibility above M_c ≈ 0.5 slows the mixing of an underexpanded jet and lengthens its shock-containing region; higher exit turbulence does the opposite (Chuech et al. 1989).
- **Fine-scale mixing is unresolved** (§2.3.3, p. 384):
  - Three-dimensionality suggests more fine-scale mixing, loss of pairing suggests less, and the data conflict.
  - Up to 25 % *more* efficient small-scale mixing at M_c = 0.6–0.8 than incompressible (Dutton et al. 1990; Clemens et al. 1991), a similar trend in an axisymmetric layer (Clemens & Paul 1993).
  - No clear trend: similar at M_c 0.63 and 1.49, lower at 0.98 (Messersmith & Dutton 1992).
  - *Less*: 31 % molecularly mixed fluid at M_c = 0.96 against 49 % incompressible (Hall et al. 1991).
  - From M_c 0.4 to 2 all turbulence quantities fall in level and lateral extent (Elliott & Samimy 1990; Goebel & Dutton 1990).
- **Assessment caveat** (§3, p. 384–385): the effectiveness measures are "crude": usually only the change in spreading rate or axial velocity decay. Some studies rest on flow visualisation alone. Fig. 1 and Tables 1–2 nonetheless show "a certain augmentation" is possible over a wide range of M_c.
- **Passive excitation of instabilities** (§3.1.1, p. 385–392):
  - *Cavities* (Yu et al. 1994; Fig. 2, p. 385, 389): open rectangular and semicircular cavities beside the exit of a fully expanded M 2 round jet (M_c = 0.85) produce organised structures and **3×** the shear-layer growth (Table 1: δ′/δ′₀ = 2.9 at M_c 0.86, 1.5 at 1.36). The forcing frequencies fit either a convective-acoustic feedback or the cavity's normal modes, and the gain depends strongly on frequency.
  - *Screech enhancement* (p. 385, 389): a circular cylinder less than 8D downstream of an underexpanded jet strengthens the screech loop, and the stronger screech comes with large-scale vortices and faster spreading (Umeda et al. 1987). Paddles a few equivalent diameters downstream of an M 1.4, 5:1 rectangular nozzle at its design pressure, parallel to the long side near the jet edges, raise the mixing rate by **48 %** by raising screech through their feedback (Rice & Raman 1993a; Table 1: ṁ/ṁ₀ = 1.48 at M_c 0.7). Conversely, baffles that block the feedback path suppress screech and change the mixing of an underexpanded jet (p. 389).
  - *Collar (whistler) nozzles* (p. 389): a sudden-expansion collar excites jet modes set by jet velocity and collar size (Selerowicz et al. 1991). On a 3:1 rectangular jet at M 1.45 and 1.85 it excites strong flapping and doubles the centreline Mach-number decay rate (Schreck 1993; Table 1: M_CL/M_CL0 = 0.57 at x/D_e = 10, M 1.85).
  - *Lip thickness* (p. 389–390): on choked underexpanded jets over M 1–1.6, lips of 0.015″ to 0.625″ change spreading little but alter screech significantly, put down to sound reflected from thick lips forcing the shear layer (Ponton & Seiner 1989; journal version [Ponton 1992](ponton-1992-nozzle-exit-lip-thickness-plume-resonance.md)).
  - *Nozzle-wake forcing* (p. 390): at M_c 0.24 and Re above 10⁶, the most coherent structures appear when the shear-layer instability matches the unstable wake mode behind the nozzle rim, St_D = 0.25; the wake mode is tuned by lip thickness (Yu et al. 1993, analysed with LST by Gutmark et al. 1993).
  - *Curved layers* (p. 390): fast stream on the inside gives more 3-D structure and 30 % more growth (Taylor–Görtler and Rayleigh–Taylor; Island & Mungal 1992).
  - *Multiple jets* (p. 390): neighbouring supersonic jets resonate in symmetric or flapping modes (Wlezien 1987; Tam & Seiner 1987), raising the growth of some modes. Morris (1989) found resonance depends on separation, mixing-layer thickness and St, and is strongest when the separation is below one jet diameter. Twin M 1.8 rectangular jets merge shock cells at design without changing mixing; overexpanded, at a separation of two equivalent diameters, the jet width grows more than 2× relative to one jet (Zeierman et al. 1992; Table 1: D′₂₋jets/D′₁₋jet = 1.78 at M_j 1.2).
  - *Spatial forcing of ducted layers* (p. 390–391): a free layer becomes neutrally stable at high M_c, but a ducted one couples with duct acoustic modes and stays unstable. Tam & Hu (1989, layers in a rectangular channel) found two families each of instability and acoustic modes and proposed driving a parametric resonance with Mach waves from a wavy wall (Tam & Hu 1991); a few per cent of the wavelength in amplitude was predicted to give substantial growth. Experiments reduced the coherence of the structures but not the growth rate (Gilreath et al. 1993). Simulations of a confined, pressure-mismatched layer show wall-reflected waves from the splitter tip raising spreading while disrupting the natural structures (Guirguis et al. 1987; Fig. 3).
  - *Duct and ejector acoustics* (p. 391–392): duct resonance of an M 2.95 jet in an ejector excites transverse duct modes as NPR goes from over- to underexpanded, enhancing spread and secondary mixing (Abdel-Fattah & Favaloro 1988; Quinn 1977). In an underexpanded rectangular jet ejector, the screech tone at the most unstable St_D = 0.11–0.14 gives the highest mixing and ejector performance; matching the tone to the duct acoustics is secondary and makes the dominant tones vary "in a staged manner" (Hsia et al. 1988).
- **Active excitation** (§3.1.2, p. 392–395):
  - *Glow discharge* (Martens et al. 1994): M₁ = 3 and 4, M₂ = 1.2; no effect at M_c 0.5; at 0.64, 2-D forcing (electrodes at 90°) raises the normalised spreading rate from 0.032 to 0.079 and 3-D forcing (25° and 45°) to 0.137, low values attributed to a transitional layer (p. 392).
  - *Counterflow suction* (Strykowski & Krothapalli 1993; Fig. 4): suction around an M_d 2 jet at T₀ 300 and 720 K (13 % and 7 % of jet velocity) halves the potential core and raises shear-layer growth by nearly 60 % at M_c 0.82, with organised, azimuthally unstable structures penetrating to the axis by 5D. The authors attribute the gain to a convective-to-absolute transition of the initial layer; stability calculations put that transition at lower suction when the jet is lighter than the counterflow, and the heated jet responds at lower suction (p. 392–394).
  - *Acoustic drivers* (p. 394): an elliptic focusing radiator nearly removes the 5D potential core of an M 0.9 jet and drops the centreline velocity by over 40 % at 10D, most effective at St 0.25–0.3 (Borisov & Gynkina 1975). Sound excites a compressible layer (M < 0.6) best as a narrow beam at 50°–80° to the flow (Tam 1978). Internal excitation of cold and heated jets up to M 1.4 speeds axial decay, best at St_D = 0.4–0.45 with organised helical structures and no vortex interactions; excitability falls with temperature (Lepicovsky et al. 1986).
  - *Piezoelectric actuators* (p. 394): 5 kHz wedges on convergent jets, M 0.7–1.2, most effective at AR 13. Forcing below the most amplified shear-layer frequency (f_ex/f_i < 1) excites flapping; above it controls jet orientation (Kibens & Glezer 1992).
  - *Pulsed and flapping jets* (p. 394–395): in subsonic ejectors, pulsing (St_D 0.05–0.65) gives thrust augmentation of 1.9 against 1.35 steady; Coanda flapping at St_D 0.01 gives 1.65 against 1.5 (Binder & Didelle 1981). A flip-flop 8:1 rectangular convergent jet up to M_j 1.8 at St_D < 0.01 displaces the whole layer and spreads no more than the steady screeching jet (Raman et al. 1993).
- **Three-dimensional jets** (§3.2, p. 395–397):
  - *Rectangular slot jets* spread fast at moderate underexpansion (M_j 1.4–1.8), where screech forms large vortices alternating on the two wide sides; the flapping raises spreading and mixing, at **St_D = 0.12** (Krothapalli et al. 1986; Gutmark et al. 1990; Fig. 5) (p. 395).
  - Internal divergence along the wide side of rectangular C-D nozzles doubles spreading over 0.5 < M_c < 1.8 (Gutmark et al. 1991b; Table 1 gives δ′/δ′₀ = 1.96–3.7 at M_c 0.5–2.25) (p. 395).
  - Sonic and underexpanded **elliptic** jets spread slightly faster than rectangular ones of the same AR. The vertices of the rectangle reduce coherence and so weaken self-induction, but their streamwise vorticity should make small-scale mixing more intense in the rectangular jet (Schadow et al. 1989) (p. 395).
  - *Triangular and square jets* combine large-scale mixing on the flat sides with small-scale mixing at the corners. The flat sides spread faster than the corners, so the jet switches axes; corner curvature deforms the vortices into streamwise vortices; supersonic combustion heat release rose (Gutmark et al. 1991a) (p. 395).
  - *Multistep nozzles* (rearward steps before the exit) give 6× the initial turbulence level and steadier M 2.5 combustion (Schadow et al. 1990) (p. 396).
  - *Tabs, bevels and asymmetric exits* on convergent nozzles up to M_j 1.6 (Wlezien & Kibens 1988): one short tab or mild bevel deflects the jet without changing spreading; long or paired tabs, or steep bevels, spread the jet strongly one way and contract it the other; multiple tabs expand part of the pressure internally and speed core decay (p. 396–397).
  - *Bevelled M 1.4 rectangular nozzles* (Rice & Raman 1993b): single bevels give marginal gains; double bevels raise entrainment by over 50 % in the first 10 D_e and split the jet into two sub-jets while keeping axial thrust (p. 397).
- **Streamwise (axial) vorticity** (§3.3, p. 397–405):
  - *Lobed mixers* (Fig. 6): in an M 1.5, hot ejector the mixer gives near-ideal pumping, nearly twice a conventional ejector (Tillman et al. 1992; Table 1: ratio 1.77). In free layers the mixing gain of a forced mixer falls as M_c rises, although compressibility barely changes the streamwise vorticity field (J. K. Elliott et al. 1992, experiment and computation) (p. 397–399).
  - *Ramped injectors* (Fig. 7): swept ramps beat unswept ones through larger, stronger streamwise vortices while keeping fuel injection nearly parallel for thrust (Northam et al. 1989, 1991; Drummond et al. 1989; Davis & Hingst 1991) (p. 399).
  - *Oblique-structure devices* (p. 399): Papamoschou (1991) proposed an effective M_c,eff = M_c cos β for structures travelling at angle β, and LST of a skewed layer gave "a certain increase" in spatial growth (Lu & Lele 1993). But vortex generators, 40° sawtooth trailing edges and inclined trip wires changed growth by no more than 5 %.
  - *Vortex generators on a splitter plate* (M 5/3): up to 30 % more spread, but from a thicker initial layer, with no change in growth rate; wedges had an insignificant or adverse effect (Dolling et al. 1992). A toothed 3-D splitter plate gives 53 % more thickness between M 1 helium and M 2.5 air; the tooth span should be at least the initial boundary-layer thickness (Fernando & Menon 1993) (p. 401).
  - *Natural streamwise vortices in underexpanded jets* lie between the intercepting shock and the jet boundary, are strong enough to mix ambient fluid into the supersonic region, and may come from tiny nozzle imperfections (Novopashin & Perepelkin 1989). The authors infer that larger vortex generators should be effective (p. 401).
  - *Tabs* (1–2 % blockage each): two tabs work best (Bradbury & Khadem 1975, subsonic). In supersonic jets tabs enhance mixing and reduce screech (Ahuja & Brown 1989). Delta tabs make counter-rotating pairs that distort the jet cross-section (Samimy et al. 1993; Zaman 1993); two tabs bifurcate the jet and more tabs impose their own symmetry (Fig. 8, M_j 1.63). On rectangular (AR 3–8) and elliptic nozzles tabs can control or completely suppress axis switching, and raise or lower entrainment; the gain is in the near field, and spreading beyond x/D = 10 is unchanged or slightly lower (Zaman 1994). Shocks from the tabs also contribute (Wishart et al. 1993) (p. 401–402).
  - *Notched nozzles* shed trailing vortices that form low-speed side jets in the notch plane, speed centreline decay and reduce noise because the sources are wrapped in low-speed turbulence (Pannu & Johannesen 1976) (p. 403).
  - *Tapered-slot nozzle* (Gutmark et al. 1992): an M 2 C-D nozzle from a 3:1 rectangular throat to a round exit, run at M 1–2.5. Minor-plane spreading rises 12–70 % depending on M; major-plane spreading is higher below M 1.8 and lower above. Above all, it does not screech anywhere in its range, which the authors attribute to the absence of coherent shocks off-design (p. 403).
  - *Splayed nozzle*: half the potential-core length of an AR 3.7 rectangular nozzle at M 1.5, 1000 °F, in an M 0.5 stream (Tillman et al. 1991) (p. 403).
  - *Ramp nozzle* (Yu et al. 1992): five swept ramps in a C-D nozzle raise growth by 40 % at M_c 0.23 and nearly 80 % at 0.86, by creating large-scale structures (p. 403).
  - *Swirl*: up to 60 % more spreading, proportional to swirl number, at M_c 0.7–1.9 (Naughton & Settles 1992), against an earlier null result attributed to weaker swirl (Povinelli & Ehlers 1972) (p. 404).
  - *Injection into a supersonic stream* (§3.3.3, p. 404–405): normal injection penetrates and mixes well at the cost of pressure losses, no thrust and hot spots; tangential injection is the reverse. Combining them (Drummond & Mukunda 1988, numerical; King et al. 1989, +70 % growth behind a step) and staging jets behind a step (Fletcher & McDaniel 1989) use one jet's bow shock to help the other. For slanted jets the jet-to-effective-back-pressure ratio is the strongest control on spreading (Mays et al. 1989); elliptic injectors beat round ones through contact area and streamwise vorticity (Kopchenov & Lomkov 1992).
- **Shock–shear-layer interaction** (§3.4, p. 405–408):
  - Shocks in a plane normal to the layer interact with the splitter-tip separation and make a mixing streamwise vortex (Clemens & Mungal 1992b) (p. 405–406).
  - A wedge shock on the M 5 boundary layer before separation thickens the layer near the origin, but growth relaxes to the undisturbed value within a few thicknesses (Shau & Dolling 1989) (p. 406–407).
  - Bow shocks from cylinders in a layer at M_c 0.51 and 0.86 change growth only when the cylinder sits near the origin and blocks half the layer (Samimy et al. 1990) (p. 407).
  - Oscillating shocks at 60–240 kHz on a helium jet in M 3 air change the vortical structure and raise mixing (Kumar et al. 1989; Wood et al. 1990) (p. 407).
  - *Baroclinic mixing* (p. 407–408): shocks crossing a density interface (e.g. hydrogen in air) generate vorticity through −(∇p × ∇ρ)/ρ² (Marble et al. 1987). In a shock tube one shock crossing a helium jet nearly doubles molecular mixing and two crossings more than triple it (Budzinski et al. 1992). Contoured-wall injectors (ramps and troughs, M 1.7 helium into M 6 air) add streamwise vorticity both baroclinically and through a pressure-driven cross-stream shear layer, lifting the fuel off the wall (Marble et al. 1990; Waitz et al. 1992; Fig. 10).
- **Flow parameters** (§3.5, p. 408–410):
  - *Initial conditions*: tripping the boundary layer widens the band over which M 0.8 jets respond to excitation (Lepicovsky 1990); tabs work with laminar or turbulent boundary layers if taller than the layer (Zaman et al. 1994); the growth of compressible homogeneous sheared turbulence forgets its initial acoustic level (Blaisdell et al. 1993, DNS); planar-layer growth is insensitive to exit pressure mismatch (Hall et al. 1993) (p. 408–409).
  - *Convective Mach number* (p. 409–410): techniques that rely on 2-D wave amplification should weaken with M_c, those using streamwise vorticity or baroclinic torque should strengthen. The sparse data agree: cavity forcing (2-D) is stronger at M_c 0.86 than 1.4; the ramp nozzle (3-D) gains more at 0.86 than 0.23; the forced mixer's gain rises with velocity difference.
  - *Temperature and pressure gradient* (p. 410): heating shortens the potential core slightly (Lau 1980), but mostly through the exit boundary layer (Lepicovsky 1990). A favourable streamwise pressure gradient raises mixing-layer growth markedly, possibly through baroclinic torque (Abe et al. 1992).
- **Applications** (§4, p. 410–412): scramjet combustor length (parallel injection mixes too slowly); ejectors for thrust augmentation, noise suppression and pressure recovery (chemical lasers, Dutton et al. 1982); **noise**: the HSCT needed about 20 dB lower perceived noise (Seiner & Krejsa 1989), and squeezing the Concorde nozzle in the horizontal plane had already reduced sideline noise; metal powder atomisation; thrust vector control.
- **Closing comments** (§5, p. 412): mixing has three stages: bulk entrainment by the large structures (which also sets layer growth), stirring at smaller scales (the mixing transition) and molecular mixing at the smallest scales. Growth rate is only an upper bound on mixed fluid; methods meant for combustion must be judged on stirring and molecular mixing too.

## Setup / method
This is a review with no new data. Its quantitative content is Fig. 1 and Tables 1–2, compiled from the cited studies; the authors give no uncertainty, station or definition beyond the table footnotes.

- **Scope and organisation** (p. 375–376, 384–385): §1 subsonic background and compressibility; §2 supersonic shear layers (LST, reference Mach numbers, large-scale structures, fine-scale mixing); §3 enhancement, in four families (excitation of instabilities, 3-D jets, axial vorticity, shock interactions) plus flow-parameter effects; §4 applications; §5 closing comments. Table 1 covers jets, Table 2 planar mixing layers.
- **Enhancement measures used across the tables** (Table footnotes, p. 388): growth-rate ratio δ′/δ′₀ (δ′ = dδ/dx, subscript 0 the unenhanced reference); thickness ratio δ/δ₀ at a given x/δ_IN; centreline Mach number ratio M_CL/M_CL0 at a given x/D_e; mass-flow (entrainment) ratios ṁ/ṁ₀ and ṁ′/ṁ′₀; jet-width growth D′/D′₀; potential-core length L_pc/L_pc0; centreline total-temperature ratio T/T₀; ejector pumping ratio. All are time-averaged mean-flow quantities.
- **Diagnostics behind the reviewed data** (from text and captions): spark and continuous schlieren (Figs 1, 5), planar Mie scattering (Fig. 2), condensation imaging (Figs 4, 8), filtered Rayleigh scattering (Elliott et al. 1992), planar laser-induced iodine fluorescence and injectant mole fraction (Fletcher & McDaniel 1989; McDaniel & Graves 1988, Fig. 9), Pitot and total-temperature surveys (Figs 6, 8), tracer-gas volume fraction (Fig. 7), molecular-mixing measurements (Hall et al. 1991; Clemens et al. 1991; Dutton et al. 1990), spectra of velocity fluctuations (McIntyre & Settles 1991) and near-field microphones for screech. No phase-locked or modal analysis appears.
- **Modelling and simulation covered:**
  - *Inviscid LST of compressible mixing layers* (Groppengiesser 1970; Ragab & Wu 1989; Sandham & Reynolds 1990; Zhuang et al. 1990): unbounded, parallel; 2-D growth → 0 as M_c → ∞, oblique modes dominate above M_c ≈ 0.6 (p. 380). Skewed layers (Lu & Lele 1993) (p. 399).
  - *Confined layers* (Tam & Hu 1989, 1991; Soetrisno et al. 1990): duct acoustic modes couple with the layer and create new, mostly 3-D, modes; wavy-wall Mach waves can drive a parametric resonance (p. 381, 390–391).
  - *Round jets* (Michalke 1971; Koshigoe et al. 1987; Viswanathan & Morris 1992): compressibility stabilises; heating stabilises supersonic jets; helical modes give a 50 % asymptote (p. 381); helical dominance for R/θ ≤ 6 at M > 0.8 (p. 383).
  - *Non-circular jets* (Koshigoe, Tubis & Ho 1988): linear stability of elliptic-core jets gives the three necessary conditions for self-induction and axis switching (p. 381).
  - *Absolute instability with counterflow* (Strykowski & Krothapalli 1993): the convective-to-absolute threshold falls as jet density falls (p. 392–394).
  - *Nozzle wake vs shear layer* (Gutmark et al. 1993): LST of a supersonic jet shear layer to match the lip-wake mode (St_D = 0.25) (p. 390).
  - *Convection-velocity models*: isentropic stagnation-point M_c (Papamoschou & Roshko 1988); shocklet-corrected model (Dimotakis 1991) and its non-isentropic version (Barre 1994) (p. 382).
  - *Simulations* (2-D and 3-D, details not given in the review): reduced coherence and no pairing (Guirguis et al. 1987; Sandham & Reynolds 1990); shocks in confined layers (Soetrisno et al. 1990; Ragab & Sheen 1992, nonlinear); colayer DNS (Planche & Reynolds 1992); confined-layer reflected waves (Guirguis et al. 1987, Fig. 3); lobed-mixer computations (J. K. Elliott et al. 1992); computed hydrogen and helium mass fractions for ramp and contoured-wall injectors (Northam et al. 1991, Fig. 7; Waitz et al. 1992, Fig. 10); skewed-nozzle and combined-injection computations (Rao & Heiba 1990; Drummond & Mukunda 1988); homogeneous sheared turbulence DNS (Blaisdell et al. 1993). The review gives no resolution, boundary-condition or validation details for any of them.
  - *How well models match experiment*: LST gets the trend of growth against M_c but not the 20 % asymptote unless oblique modes (planar) or helical modes (jets, 50 %) are included (p. 380–381). The confined-layer parametric-resonance prediction was not borne out in growth rate (p. 391). Shocklets predicted by simulation lacked direct experimental evidence (p. 380, 383).
- **Screech within the review.** Screech is treated only as a mixing agent: its loop (Powell 1953) is described in one sentence (p. 384), and its frequency and staging are not modelled. The screech-related evidence is: rectangular-jet flapping at St_D 0.12 (p. 395); enhancement by cylinders and paddles and suppression by baffles (p. 385, 389); collar-driven flapping (p. 389); lip-thickness effects (p. 389–390); tabs that reduce screech while raising mixing (p. 401); the screech-free tapered-slot nozzle (p. 403); the ejector tone (p. 392).

## Key equations
Checked on the rendered pages (p. 378, 380, 382, 399, 407).

- **Convective Mach numbers** (p. 378): $M_{c1}=\dfrac{U_1-U_c}{a_1},\quad M_{c2}=\dfrac{U_c-U_2}{a_2}$, with U_c the convection velocity of the structures. For equal static pressures and specific-heat ratios, $M_{c1}=M_{c2}=M_c=\dfrac{U_1-U_2}{a_1+a_2}$ (also the Table 1–2 definition, p. 388).
- **Relative Mach number** (p. 381–382, after Rudy & Birch 1974): $M_r=\dfrac{2(U_1-U_2)}{a_1+a_2}=2M_c$ for equal γ.
- **Effective convective Mach number of oblique structures** (p. 399, after Papamoschou 1991): $M_{c,\mathrm{eff}}=M_c\cos\beta$, with β the propagation angle of the structures relative to the mean flow.
- **Shear-layer Strouhal numbers** (p. 376–377): initial band $0<St_i=f_i\theta_0/U_0<0.04$ (Michalke 1965); preferred mode $St_D=f_pD/U_0=0.25$–$0.5$.
- **Baroclinic torque** (p. 380): $-\dfrac{1}{\rho^2}(\nabla p\times\nabla\rho)$.
- **Vorticity equation used for shock-enhanced mixing** (p. 407, after Marble et al. 1987), as printed: $\dfrac{D\bar\omega}{Dt}=-\dfrac{\nabla p\times\nabla\rho}{\rho^2}+(\bar\omega\cdot\nabla)\bar u-\bar\omega(\nabla\cdot\bar u)+\nu\nabla^2\bar\omega+(\nabla\nu)\times\nabla^2\bar u$. The first term is the baroclinic torque; the others are stretching and tilting, dilatation and (simplified) viscous diffusion.

> The viscous terms of the printed vorticity equation are a simplified form for variable viscosity, not the full one; the review uses the equation only to name the baroclinic term. Check against p. 407 if quoting it.

## Relevance to this project
This review is the standard map of supersonic mixing enhancement as it stood in 1994, written by the China Lake group whose rectangular, elliptic and triangular nozzles sit in the background of the screech literature. For this library it matters on four threads: screech as a mixing agent, non-circular nozzles and axis switching, linear stability of compressible shear layers, and passive control (tabs, lips, bevels) that the screech-suppression papers also use. Its data are all time-averaged spreading and decay numbers, so it says much about *how much* mixing changes and little about *why*.

**Screech and mixing (agreements and conflicts)**
- The review's picture, screech-driven flapping at St_D ≈ 0.12 making underexpanded rectangular jets spread fast (p. 395), is the summary of [Krothapalli 1986](krothapalli-1986-screech-tones-mixing-underexpanded-rectangular-jet.md) (St ≈ 0.12 at peak screech, minor-axis spreading of 36°) and of Gutmark et al. 1990. [Alkislar 2003](alkislar-2003-screeching-rectangular-jet-stereo-piv.md) later measured it in an AR 4 C-D jet (minor-axis spreading 0.065 → 0.11 and 0.33 with screech, p. 135–136).
- Enhancing the loop raises mixing and blocking it lowers mixing (cylinders, paddles, baffles, p. 385, 389), in line with Glass's 50–100 % higher axial velocity without feedback and Rice & Raman's factor-of-4 change in core length as reported by [Raman 1998](raman-1998-supersonic-jet-screech-review.md) (p. 93–95). The numbers differ between reviews: this one gives +48 % mixing rate for the paddles (ṁ/ṁ₀ = 1.48, p. 389), while [Raman 1999](raman-1999-supersonic-jet-screech-half-century-powell-present.md) gives "about 30 %" more mass flux for induced screech, at a thrust penalty of nearly 20 % (p. 562). The stations and configurations are not given in either, so the two may describe different cases.
- The paddles work on a rectangular nozzle *at design pressure* (p. 385, 389), so the "screech" there is an induced supersonic edge tone with no shock cells (Raman 1999's reading, p. 562), not shock-cell screech.
- Zaman's finding that only the B (flapping/helical) stage raises mass flux by about 60 % ([Raman 1998](raman-1998-supersonic-jet-screech-review.md), p. 94, 97) post-dates this review; together they say that mixing gain depends on the screech *mode*, not only its amplitude. This review never separates modes.
- Tabs that raise mixing *and* reduce screech (Ahuja & Brown 1989, p. 401) and the screech-free tapered-slot nozzle (p. 403) fit [Norum 1983](norum-1983-screech-suppression-supersonic-jets.md)'s split between suppression that disrupts the shock train and suppression that acts on receptivity. A single small tab can instead move every tone without killing it ([Johnston & Edgington-Mitchell 2016](johnston-2016-underexpanded-jet-intrusive-tab-visualisation.md)), so tab effects on screech are configuration-specific.
- Lip thickness changes screech much more than spreading (p. 389–390), consistent with [Ponton 1992](ponton-1992-nozzle-exit-lip-thickness-plume-resonance.md), whose journal version maps the mode ranges. The ejector result that matching the screech tone to duct acoustics makes the dominant tones vary "in a staged manner" (Hsia et al. 1988, p. 392) is an early, unexplained observation of installation-driven tone selection.
- Twin-jet resonance (Zeierman et al. 1992; Morris 1989, strongest for separations below one diameter, p. 390) agrees with [Seiner 1988](seiner-1988-dynamic-pressure-loads-twin-supersonic-plume-resonance.md), where suppressing the coupled B mode in one plume slows the spreading of both.

**Non-circular nozzles and axis switching**
- The claims that elliptic jets spread slightly faster than rectangular jets of the same AR because the rectangle's vertices reduce coherence (Schadow et al. 1989, p. 395), and that flat sides spreading faster than corners drives axis switching in triangular and square jets (Gutmark et al. 1991a, p. 395), are self-induction arguments without phase-resolved data. Modern work replaces them with forced DNS ([Suzuki 2026](suzuki-2026-coherent-structures-axis-switching-elliptical-jets.md): stronger forcing moves the switch upstream) and resolvent streaks ([Marshall 2024](marshall-2024-prediction-streaks-rectangular-jets.md): corner vortices probably not linear streaks).
- Zaman's tabs can control or completely suppress axis switching (p. 402), a direct test of the streamwise-vortex explanation of switching that [Alkislar 2003](alkislar-2003-screeching-rectangular-jet-stereo-piv.md) later proposed (p. 136–140, 144). Both papers trace natural streamwise vortices in underexpanded jets to nozzle imperfections (Novopashin & Perepelkin 1989; this review p. 401, Alkislar p. 136).
- The trailing-edge cutouts of [Samimy 1998](samimy-1998-rectangular-jet-trailing-edge-control.md) are the next step from the notched, bevelled and tabbed nozzles here.
- Koshigoe, Tubis & Ho's three conditions for mixing through self-induction (p. 381) are testable with the Floquet LSA the group now runs on elliptic and rectangular mean flows (see [non-axisymmetric-jets](../Topics/non-axisymmetric-jets.md)): eigenfunction overlap, growth rates and phase speeds of se₁/ce₁ (or AS/SA) modes are exactly what those analyses output. That link is mine.
- Fig. 5's inset, widths at x/D_e = 8 peaking near M ≈ 1.5 for the 3:1 rectangular and elliptic minor axes and staying near 1–1.6 for the round jet, is a cheap benchmark for the group's AR 2 ellipse and rectangular nozzles ([Mitchell 2013](mitchell-2013-near-field-structure-underexpanded-elliptic-jets.md) and later PIV): measure half-widths against NPR and compare with the screech amplitude.

**Linear stability of compressible shear layers**
- The 20 % asymptote agrees with [Papamoschou & Roshko 1988](papamoschou-1988-compressible-turbulent-shear-layer.md) (≈ 0.2 by M_c ≈ 0.6–0.8), whose data are inside the Fig. 1 band. The review adds the theory side: inviscid 2-D LST tends to zero growth, and oblique modes (planar) or helical modes (jets, a 50 % asymptote per Viswanathan & Morris 1992) are needed to approach the data (p. 380–381). That fits the [linear-models](../Topics/linear-models.md) caveat that vortex-sheet KH growth is unreliable at high M_c.
- For jets, Michalke's compressible results (helical dominant above M ≈ 0.8 when R/θ ≤ 6; both modes equal for R/θ > 100, p. 383) extend the incompressible picture in [Michalke 1984](michalke-1984-survey-jet-instability-theory.md). For screech this means the exit boundary-layer thickness affects which azimuthal KH mode leads; whether it affects A/B/C stage selection is my question, not the review's.
- Counterflow suction producing an absolute instability (p. 392–394) is an early application of [Huerre & Monkewitz 1990](huerre-1990-local-global-instabilities-spatially-developing-flows.md)'s local criterion to supersonic jets. The same machinery now underlies the absolute-instability reading of screech.
- The confined-layer modes (Tam & Hu 1989, layers in a rectangular channel, JFM 203; not the same paper as the library's [Tam & Hu 1989](tam-1989-three-families-instability-waves-high-speed-jets.md) on three wave families in free jets, JFM 201) are a duct analogue of the guided waves that close the screech loop.

**Methods and data (CLAUDE.md workflow)**
- Every measure in Tables 1–2 is a time-averaged mean-flow quantity: spreading rate, thickness, centreline Mach number, entrainment, core length. All suit non-time-resolved data (Pitot surveys, single-shot schlieren, Mie or condensation images, PIV means). Frequencies (screech St_D 0.12, ejector tones 0.11–0.14, preferred-mode forcing 0.25–0.45) come from microphones or forcing settings.
- Nothing in the review resolves time: no phase-locking, modal decomposition or intermittency. The causal claims (screech raises mixing, cavities organise structures, suction triggers absolute instability) rest on comparing mean flows with and without the device.
- *Non-time-resolved PIV of the group's jets:* POD-pair phase recovery would give the phase-resolved structure behind each mean-flow gain, and the half-width growth rate δ′ in both planes is the review's own metric for comparison.
- *Time-resolved schlieren and the SPOD → reconstruction → Hilbert POD pipeline:* the review's central link, screech amplitude to spreading, can be tested instantaneously. Reconstruct the screech band, track its Hilbert envelope, and condition short-time means (half-width, potential-core length) on that envelope, as was done per tone for the AR 2 ellipse in [Mazharmanesh 2026](mazharmanesh-2026-triadic-interaction-mean-flow-deformation-screech.md). This would separate amplitude from mode, which the review's tables cannot. Mode-switching or staged tones (Hsia's ejector, p. 392) would show up as changes in the conditioned mean.

**PyFR staging LES and modelling**
- The LES is a round convergent jet, so the review's non-circular material does not apply directly. Two points do: (1) if A1 and A2 states are bistable at one NPR, their spreading rates and core lengths can be compared state by state, testing whether mixing depends on the stage as Zaman's B-mode result suggests for round jets; (2) the exit boundary-layer thickness (R/θ) sets which azimuthal KH mode is favoured (p. 383), so it should be recorded and held fixed across continuation steps. Both are my suggestions.

**Rocket noise and impinging jets**
- The noise motivation (HSCT, Concorde nozzle shaping, notched nozzles that wrap sources in low-speed flow, p. 403, 411) and the heated-jet results (heating stabilises supersonic jets, p. 381; excitability falls with temperature, p. 394) feed the [jet-noise-mechanisms](../Topics/jet-noise-mechanisms.md) and rocket-noise threads only indirectly; the jet-noise companion is [Tam 1995](tam-1995-supersonic-jet-noise.md). Mach-wave radiation as evidence of supersonically convecting structures (p. 382–383) is the same mechanism that dominates heated rocket-plume noise.
- Umeda et al.'s cylinder impingement strengthening screech and spreading (p. 385) and the cavity-resonance forcing (p. 385) are jet–surface feedback loops in the sense of [impinging-jets-and-edge-tones](../Topics/impinging-jets-and-edge-tones.md), used deliberately for mixing.
- Modern active control of high-speed jets (plasma actuators, [Samimy 2006](samimy-2006-plasma-actuators-high-speed-jet-flow-noise-control.md)) follows the review's prescription of forcing inside the natural instability band.
