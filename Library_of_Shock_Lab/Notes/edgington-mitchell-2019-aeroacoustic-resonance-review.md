## Summary
A review of the two classical supersonic jet resonances, free-jet screech and impingement tones, written by the group leader just before the guided-jet-mode and k_s1/k_s2 work reshaped the field. The first half is history: Powell's phase and gain criteria and phased-array model, Panda's standing wave, Tam's weakest-link model, staging, the effects of nozzle shape, lip thickness and temperature, shock motion and splitting, and the long-running dispute over source location and directivity. It then covers Powell's, Henderson's and Krothapalli's impingement work, recent ultra-high-speed (UHS) schlieren, PIV and LES, and passive and active suppression (p. 118–152). The second half assesses each of the four processes of the loop in turn (p. 152–175): the downstream KH wavepacket and how shocks complicate its modelling; sound generation by shock–vortex interaction, especially shock leakage, and by the wall-jet shocklet in impinging jets; the upstream path, either free-stream sound or the upstream guided jet mode (k_TH⁻); and receptivity at the lip. The outlook (p. 175–177) judges the downstream process settled, generation tied to unsteady shock motion but not universally explained, the upstream process "on the cusp of revelation", and receptivity the likeliest to be fully modelled first. It also notes that most of Raman's 1998 open questions, staging above all, were still unanswered. No new analysis is presented, but several previously unpublished group visualisations are (Figs 2, 5, 11, 12, 38 and the colour schlieren of Figs 7, 10, 18).

## Key findings

### Framework: four processes, two criteria (p. 119–123)
- **Four processes** (p. 120; Fig. 3, p. 122): (i) *downstream*: energy carried downstream, usually as a hydrodynamic perturbation; (ii) *generation*: conversion of that energy into an upstream-propagating disturbance, "also typically responsible for the generation of acoustic tones"; (iii) *upstream*: propagation to a point where the base flow is sensitive; (iv) *receptivity*: forcing of that point to make a new downstream disturbance.
- **Phase criterion** (eq. 1, p. 120). N/f = h/U₁ + h/U₂ + ψ, where ψ is any extra delay at the lip or in generation. N can be counted spatially (concurrent disturbances of both directions) or temporally (new disturbances of one direction produced while one disturbance completes a cycle) (p. 120–121).
- **Gain criterion** (eq. 2, p. 121). q_d η_g η_u η_r ≥ 1. The loop gain can exceed one only during the start-up transient. q_d (shear-layer amplification) is the only term above one; the rest are efficiencies (p. 121, 152).
- **Staging, after Powell** (p. 122). Because N is an integer and some gains depend on frequency, the gain is sometimes maximised by changing N, which gives a frequency jump. Example: an AR 2 converging elliptical jet jumps at M_j ≈ 1.06, 1.4 and 1.45 (Fig. 4, p. 123; data from [Edgington-Mitchell, Honnery & Soria 2015](edgington-mitchell-2015-staging-screeching-elliptical-jets.md), Int. J. Aeroacoust. 14).
- **History** (p. 122–123). Stability theory made the big advance after Powell, since the frequency dependence of wavepacket growth couples eqs. 1 and 2. Progress slowed after a "flurry" at the end of the 20th century, partly because the data needed were hard to get, and interest has now revived.

### Screech: Powell's model and its three derivations (p. 124–130)
- **Powell's observations** (p. 124): the tone's wavelength relates to shock spacing; the effective source is in the jet some distance downstream; the fundamental is strongest upstream and the first harmonic at the sideline.
- **Phased array** (eqs. 3–4, p. 124–125). Three or four stationary monopoles at the shock tips give the directivities of the fundamental and harmonic. The result is highly sensitive to M_c and λ_L (Norum 1983, PDF held, not yet indexed), but parameters can be found that give upstream and sideline peaks. Requiring the fundamental to peak exactly upstream gives **eq. 5**, f = U_c/[s(1 + M_c)] (p. 125).
- **Eq. 5 has three derivations**: (a) maximum upstream directivity of the array; (b) a pure timing argument from eq. 1 with ψ = 0, source at h = ns and n = N (p. 125); (c) Tam's frequency–directivity argument, eq. 12 (p. 129). So its accuracy "does not necessarily indicate that its theoretical framework is correct" (p. 128). [Powell et al. 1992](powell-1992-oscillation-modes-choked-circular-jets.md) later dropped the strict maximum-upstream condition, because the frequency of maximum feedback shifts where the instability depends steeply on frequency (quoted, p. 125; agreement with Tam, p. 129).
- **Standing wave** (eqs. 6–8, p. 125–126). Counter-propagating waves at one frequency form a travelling interference pattern with a stationary envelope. It is credited to Westley & Woolley and was first studied in detail by [Panda 1999](panda-1999-experimental-investigation-screech-noise-generation.md).
  - k_sw = k_A + k_L gives f_s = U_c/[λ_sw(1 + M_c)] (eq. 8). Eq. 8 is "an exact relation" for counter-propagating waves, whereas eq. 5 rests on assumptions about the flow. Both hold only when λ_sw equals the shock spacing, and whether the two lengths match "appears to be related to the mode staging process" (p. 126, citing the group's 2015 AIAA paper and [Mercier 2017](mercier-2017-screech-feedback-loop-underexpanded-round-jets.md)).
  - The standing wave modulates wavepacket velocity (Bell et al. 2016) and pressure (Panda), but this is hard to separate from shock modulation ([Tan 2017](tan-2017-broadband-velocity-fluctuations-screeching-jets.md)) (p. 126).
  - The author prefers to say that the downstream wave is modulated by the upstream wave at λ_sw. This is two-way coupling: η_u modulates q_d, which in turn sets η_u. Such coupling makes predictive amplitude models "exceedingly difficult" (p. 126).
- **Tam's weakest link** (eqs. 9–13, p. 127–130).
  - BBSAN as a phased monopole array (Harper-Bourne & Fisher) gives eq. 9. [Tam & Tanna 1982](tam-1982-shock-associated-noise-cd-nozzles.md) replace it with a weak interaction of large structures with the quasi-periodic shock cells, radiating as Mach waves: eq. 10 gives the same peak frequencies, but the decay of the higher Fourier components k_n explains the missing harmonics (p. 127–128). A spread ΔU of convection speed gives the bandwidth (eq. 11), which narrows as the observer moves upstream (p. 128).
  - As θ → 180°, eq. 10 tends to eq. 5 and the band shrinks to a line, so screech is the upstream limit of BBSAN ([Tam 1986](tam-1986-bbsan-screech-relationship.md); p. 128).
  - *Phase.* Tam et al. argue the loop phase need not be exactly 2πn, since the "wavemaker" is set by the flow, not a solid edge. It could move through shock motion, which the author doubts because shock motion is phase-locked (§2.8), or through a change of the shocks that generate (§2.9) (p. 128).
  - *Amplitude.* The wave need only be "strong enough", not maximal upstream; Norum's measured directivities peak off-axis and differ between modes (Fig. 6, p. 128–129). The author finds this "difficult to argue against" (p. 128).
  - *Weakest link.* Receptivity at the lip is the weakest link, so only the narrow band that radiates towards the lip can sustain screech (eq. 12, identical to eq. 5; p. 129). Its advantage is that it ties to stability theory and needs no observed inputs (p. 129). The semi-empirical fit is eq. 13, with the fully expanded diameter from eq. 14 (p. 129–130).
  - None of eqs. 5, 12 or 13 predicts staging jumps (p. 130).

### Screech: staging (p. 130–133)
- **Stages** (p. 130). Rising NPR gives two toroidal modes (A1, A2), then precessing flapping (B, D) or helical (C) modes (Fig. 7). All round-jet stages belong to m = 0 or m = ±1.
  - The "tilted toroidal" A2 of early schlieren is probably m = 0 plus a weak m = ±1, since A2's pressure range may also contain B.
  - C is m = ±1; B and D are two helical modes with a fixed phase relation, i.e. flapping.
  - Precession "has resisted classification": the plane locks for a time and then jumps in some facilities, and rotates smoothly in others.
- **Facility specificity** (p. 130–131). The Lyon jets show precessing flapping but never a helical mode (André et al.; [Mercier 2017](mercier-2017-screech-feedback-loop-underexpanded-round-jets.md)), and the precession argues against a nozzle defect. Other facilities show toroidal and helical modes but no flapping. Monash sees both ([Edgington-Mitchell 2014, helical mode](edgington-mitchell-2014-coherent-structure-helical-screech-mode.md); [Edgington-Mitchell 2014, Mach disk](edgington-mitchell-2014-underexpanded-jet-mach-disk-shear-layer.md); [Tan 2017](tan-2017-broadband-velocity-fluctuations-screeching-jets.md)).
  - Mode selection is very sensitive to lip thickness, boundary-layer thickness and flange size, which papers should report more clearly (p. 130). V. Jaunet (Pprime, personal communication) suggests that lip thickness decides whether the helical mode appears (p. 131).
- **Coexistence against exclusion** (p. 131). Short-time FFTs (Raman 1997), wavelets (Walker et al. 1997) and Hilbert analysis (Bell et al. 2017) show that several tones can exist simultaneously. At some conditions, though, the jet jumps intermittently between modes and they never coexist (Raman 1997; [Mancinelli 2019](mancinelli-2019-screech-prediction-upstream-modes.md)).
- **[Shen & Tam 2002](shen-2002-three-dimensional-numerical-simulation-jet-screech.md)** (p. 131–132). In their simulation the weakest link predicts A1 and B but not A2 and C, so they proposed a second loop closed by a neutral acoustic jet mode. Later work confirms the mode matters but finds it in A1 and A2 alike, so it does not explain staging ([Edgington-Mitchell 2018](edgington-mitchell-2018-upstream-acoustic-modes-screech-closure.md); p. 132, 170–171).
- **Gao & Li 2010** (p. 132). In computational aeroacoustics they count N = 5 concurrent cycles for flapping and 6 for helical, with the effective source at the 5th shock cell. With these, eq. 15 matches λ/D for stages A0–D over M_j ≈ 1.05–1.9 (Fig. 8). The match is a posteriori, but it suggests that "the shock spacing may remain the appropriate length scale" (p. 132).
- **Two length scales, two stages** (p. 133).
  - Singh & Chatterjee: in their simulations A1 obeys eq. 5 with the shock spacing, and A2 obeys eq. 8 with the standing-wave spacing.
  - In AR 2 elliptical jets λ_sw equals the shock spacing in the B-like stage but not in the C-like stage ([Edgington-Mitchell et al. 2015](edgington-mitchell-2015-staging-screeching-elliptical-jets.md), Int. J. Aeroacoust.). Similar mode-dependent length-scale relations are reported for twin jets ([Knast 2018](knast-2018-coupling-modes-underexpanded-twin-axisymmetric-jet.md); Bell et al. 2017) and round jets (Mercier 2017).
  - "This may be significant, or it simply may be a result of changes in the N value."
- **Verdict on staging** (p. 133). Staging "is still best understood in terms of Powell's original description": eqs. 1 and 2 satisfied together, with jumps from a change of azimuthal mode or of N.

### Screech: nozzle shape, lip, temperature (p. 133–136)
- **Shape** (p. 133–134). Round and rectangular jets both have a symmetric mode slightly off design and antisymmetric modes further off design. In rectangular jets the antisymmetric mode is usually flapping about the major axis.
  - Most rectangular jets do not stage beyond that switch ([Tam 1988](tam-1988-shock-cell-screech-rectangular-non-axisymmetric-jets.md)), but **thicker lips bring staging and multiple simultaneous tones** (Walker et al. 1997; Thethy et al. 2017, AIAC) (p. 133).
  - Elliptical jets axis-switch like rectangular jets but without corner vortices, and their shocks are complex (Fig. 9) (p. 133–134).
  - A flapping mode locked to a preferred axis gives strongly azimuthal directivity (Fig. 10). Downstream of the axis switch a jet may sustain helical modes ([Edgington-Mitchell 2015](edgington-mitchell-2015-multimodal-instability-weakly-underexpanded-elliptic-jet.md)), but "it is unclear whether this mode can develop close enough to the nozzle to produce screech" (p. 134).
- **Lip** (p. 134–135).
  - Norum: thicker lips raise screech amplitude.
  - [Ponton & Seiner 1992](ponton-1992-nozzle-exit-lip-thickness-plume-resonance.md): a thicker lip slightly lowers the A1 and B frequencies and raises C; amplitudes rise; and screech persists to higher NPR.
  - Raman 1997 ("Cessation of screech"): at high NPR the expanding jet boundary blocks the feedback path to a thin lip, and a thicker lip intercepts the waves, so screech reappears.
  - The author concludes the lip acts mainly acoustically (receptivity), not through entrainment. He cites reflectors near the nozzle that enhance (Powell 1954), suppress (Nagel et al. 1983) or shift (Raman, Panda & Zaman 1997) screech (p. 135).
- **Temperature** (p. 136).
  - Shen & Tam 2000: at fixed NPR heating raises U_c and the frequency but leaves St, shock spacing and shock strength unchanged, and eq. 13 stays predictive.
  - Amplitude: very hot jets often do not screech and amplitude falls in some studies, but rectangular-jet studies report increases with temperature ratio (Gojon et al. 2017; Chen et al. 2018).

### Screech: shock motion, splitting, source and directivity (p. 136–140)
- **Shock motion is phase-locked to the cycle** (Seiner's condition for stationary-source models). Panda 1998 and André et al. 2012 confirm it (p. 136–137).
  - Panda's four drivers: near-field pressure modulation by upstream waves; sound transmitted into the jet; sonic-line pressure and position changed by vortices; and the core velocity field changed by vortices (p. 136).
  - Upstream-wave pressure alone gives only small motion beyond the first cell. Sound transmitted into the jet visibly moves the oblique shocks in impinging jets (Fig. 38), but less than vortices do (p. 136–137).
  - Passing vortices dominate. They make large pressure perturbations at the sonic line (an unsteady degree of off-design) and change its curvature, and so the shock reflection angle (p. 137).
- **Mode-dependent motion** (p. 137). A modes oscillate axially, flapping modes transversely and helical modes circularly. Shocks also distort: the core moves most in A modes, and shocks tilt in flapping and helical modes (Fig. 25). André et al. found the link between tone amplitude and shock-oscillation amplitude differs by mode.
- **Shock splitting** (Westley & Woolley; Panda 1998) (p. 137–138). As a shock moves downstream a weak shock forms upstream of it, strengthens as the first fades, then itself moves downstream.
  - Fig. 11: 4th cell, A1, NPR 2.15. Fig. 12: 3rd cell, C mode, NPR 3.4, where right- and left-running waves alternate.
  - So the steady spacing may not be the effective spacing in axisymmetric oscillation, and a stationary shock pattern "is at best a convenient fiction" (p. 138).
- **Effective sources** (p. 138–139). The harmonic is much weaker than the fundamental at Θ = 60° but nearly as strong at 90° (Fig. 2, NPR 4.2). Powell et al. 1992 placed an effective source from schlieren, moving downstream with NPR, yet kept a phased array. Raman's single equivalent source is "not in conflict" with it (quoted).
- **Helical directivity** (p. 139). Umeda & Ishii propose a source orbiting the sonic line at the shock reflection points, sweeping a Mach cone, which explains the peak 30° off upstream. Their schlieren pointed to the 3rd cell, but a single source could not give the upstream peak, so they adopted multiple sources.
- **Source location** (p. 139).
  - Raman 1997 (rectangular jets, near-field amplitude and phase): the 3rd and 4th shocks are most closely tied to amplitude.
  - Mercier et al. 2017: effective source at the 3rd or 4th shock tip over a wide range of conditions.
  - Tam, Parrish & Viswanathan 2014 put emission in the core, explaining harmonic directivity through nonlinear instability-wave × shock terms. The author states his belief that the sound is made at the shock tips. Lele 2005 showed Tam's wave model is equivalent, under some conditions, to distributed sources (p. 139–140).
- **"There is no consensus view"** (p. 140). Some disagreements are only different routes to the same physics. Others, such as helical directivity (a moving shock-tip source against a different feedback path), are "currently irreconcilable" and await the generation mechanism.

### Impingement (p. 140–148)
- **Origins** (p. 140–141). Marsh 1961 found tones above a critical Mach number. [Ho & Nosseir 1981](ho-1981-dynamics-impinging-jet-feedback-phenomenon.md) closed the subsonic loop with free-stream sound; [Tam & Ahuja 1990](tam-1990-theoretical-model-discrete-tone-impinging-jets.md) closed it with jet acoustic modes. Frequency varies continuously with stand-off, with jumps between stages (Fig. 14, NPR 3).
- **Powell 1988** (p. 141–142).
  - *Small plates:* tones only at certain stand-offs, set by where the plate falls in the shock structure. In an expansion region an unstable stand-off shock forms, whose oscillation is the source (a Hartmann–Sprenger analogy).
  - *Large plates:* a source at the plate itself, and eq. 16 with phase lag p. The gain criterion applies unchanged (Fig. 15).
- **[Henderson & Powell 1993](henderson-1993-tones-choked-jet-impinging-flat-plates.md)** (large plates; p. 142–143). Multiple simultaneous tones in most conditions. Staging depends more on stand-off than on NPR. Screech returns for h/D > 5.
  - NPR decides switches between axisymmetric and helical modes, and jumps are changes of N (L1 from 4 to 6 cycles at NPR 2.70, from 4 to 5 at 2.36), with 3 ≤ N ≤ 8.
  - An upstream reflector that cancels the wave at the lip suppresses the tone, which proves nozzle–plate feedback "fairly conclusively". Krothapalli 1985 had seen screech and impingement tones together in rectangular jets.
- **Henderson & Powell 1996** (small plates; p. 143). The reflector cannot suppress these tones. They occur only where a stand-off normal shock truncates the oblique shocks, and the feedback runs between plate and stand-off shock, not to the nozzle. In the linear analyses of Mørch 1964 and Kuo & Dowling 1996, stand-off-shock oscillation makes entropy fluctuations; their convection, plus pressure waves, returns pressure waves to the shock.
- **[Henderson 2002](henderson-2002-flow-structure-supersonic-impinging-jets.md)** (p. 143–144). Tone production goes with a periodic "collapse" of the stand-off shock. Zones of silence occur where the plate sits in the free-jet shock train, and none occur in the ideally expanded jets of Krothapalli et al. 1999. A recirculation bubble (Ginzburg et al.) is not needed.
- **Krothapalli et al. 1999** (JFM 392, PDF held, not yet indexed; p. 144). The first PIV of a supersonic impinging jet: a near-design C-D jet with weak shocks still gives strong tones, and eq. 16 fits only with a phase lag.
- **Henderson, Bridges & Wernet 2005** (JFM 542, PDF held, not yet indexed; p. 144). PIV shows recirculation does not correlate with tones, and wall-jet fluctuations at 1.3D from the axis produce the upstream sound.
- **Ultra-high-speed imaging** (p. 144–147).
  - Triple decomposition (Fig. 16): tone fluctuations are modulated by shocks and by the standing wave.
  - One full cycle in UHS schlieren (Fig. 17; NPR 2.3, h/D 2.66, m = 0; frames 20 µs apart; [Mitchell 2012](mitchell-2012-visualization-acoustic-feedback-loop-impinging-jet-schlieren.md)). A KH vortex reaches the plate and drives the stand-off shock upstream; an acoustic wave is emitted, reaches the lip and seeds a new vortex.
  - [Powis 2016](powis-2016-impingement-tones-shock-instabilities-plug-nozzle.md) (plug nozzle): the stand-off shock either oscillates almost sinusoidally or splits, depending on stand-off. Colour schlieren shows split and coalesced stand-off shocks for an m = 0 tone (Fig. 18). This is the same mechanism as free-jet splitting (p. 145, 147).
- **LES era** (p. 145–147). Experiments drove understanding until about 2010; numerics have dominated since.
  - Dauptain et al. 2010, 2012: mean flow and feedback captured.
  - Uzun et al. 2013: DMD of heated and isothermal near-design jets gives toroidal rings at the tone, strongest near the nozzle.
  - Gojon et al. (planar 2016) and [Bogey & Gojon 2017](bogey-2017-feedback-loop-upwind-waves-ideally-expanded-impinging-jets.md) (round, ideally expanded): tones match an acoustic-jet-mode loop. In underexpanded cases wall-jet structures correlate with stand-off-shock motion (as in Henderson 2005), helical and axisymmetric modes coexist with two spectral peaks, and tone strength is strongly intermittent (Gojon & Bogey 2017).
- **Geometry** (p. 147–148).
  - Inclined plates (blast deflectors) offer many feedback path lengths.
  - Convex cylinders replace helical modes with flapping locked normal to the axis, though axisymmetric modes remain at some conditions (Fig. 19; z = 3.5D, NPR 3.0 axisymmetric, NPR 3.4 flapping). Concave surfaces sometimes suppress the resonance, by an unclear mechanism ([Mason-Smith 2015](mason-smith-2015-underexpanded-jet-impinging-cylindrical-sections.md)).
  - Thick lips sustain both m = 0 and m = 1 tones, a thin lip only m = 1, and that m = 1 loop closes by reflection off a flange upstream of the nozzle, not at the lip ([Weightman et al. 2019](weightman-2019-nozzle-external-geometry-azimuthal-mode-impinging-jet.md); p. 148).

### Suppression (p. 148–152)
- Suppression works by lowering any of the four efficiencies until eq. 2 fails (p. 148–149).
- **Tabs** (p. 149–150) eliminate screech. Each makes a streamwise vortex pair, driven by the pressure gradient upstream of the tab. *How* they break the loop is unclear: lost azimuthal coherence, a weaker shock structure (generation), slower wavepacket growth, or altered receptivity. A single small tab changes the shock structure a great deal (Fig. 20, NPR 2.6; [Johnston 2016](johnston-2016-underexpanded-jet-intrusive-tab-visualisation.md)). Tabs cost high-frequency noise and thrust.
- **Chevrons** (p. 149–151) probably act in the same way. Underexpanded jets interact even with non-penetrating chevrons because the flow turns outwards at the lip (Fig. 21, NPR 4.2; Humphrey & Edgington-Mitchell 2016, in `Papers_to_get.md`).
- **Active control** (p. 150–152).
  - Glow discharge at low Re, LAFPA plasma actuators (Ohio State; Samimy et al. 2007, JFM 578, PDF held, not yet indexed; related report [Samimy 2006](samimy-2006-plasma-actuators-high-speed-jet-flow-noise-control.md)) and spinning valves or plugs force the jet away from its natural frequency, which lowers q_d.
  - Steady microjets add streamwise vorticity and weaken azimuthal vorticity, mainly reducing growth and probably receptivity (Alvi et al. 2008). They have more authority over helical than toroidal instabilities (Kumar et al. 2013).
  - Pulsed microjets combine frequency forcing with receptivity changes. "Fluidic chevrons" and "fluidic inserts" complete the list.

### The four processes (Section 5)
**Downstream (p. 152–159).**
- The only process that adds energy, and the least controversial; linear stability matches the measured initial growth ([Cavalieri 2013](cavalieri-2013-wavepackets-velocity-field-turbulent-jets.md); p. 152).
- Compressible stability: Lessen et al. 1965 (neutral disturbances in a compressible jet); [Michalke 1984](michalke-1984-survey-jet-instability-theory.md); and [Tam & Hu 1989](tam-1989-three-families-instability-waves-high-speed-jets.md), with three families (KH, supersonic instability, neutral subsonic waves). A vortex sheet captures all three, and at the moderate M_j of resonance studies KH dominates (Fig. 22) (p. 153).
- **Morris 2010 trends** (p. 154; Figs 23–24): spatial growth falls with Mach number, and so does the most unstable frequency. m = 0 is most unstable at low supersonic Mach number, m = ±1 at higher. Heating lowers supersonic growth. Thicker shear layers lower both the growth rate and the most unstable frequency.
  - The Mach dependence of growth is "a major component of staging", and the thickness dependence explains much of suppression (p. 154).
  - Locally parallel analysis is validated in subsonic ([Gudmundsson & Colonius 2011](gudmundsson-2011-instability-wave-models-near-field-jets.md)) and supersonic (Sinha et al. 2014) jets. The wavepacket framework ([Jordan & Colonius 2013](jordan-2013-wave-packets-turbulent-jet-noise.md)) handles mixing noise and resonance with a few low-order azimuthal modes ([Schmidt 2017](schmidt-2017-wavepackets-trapped-acoustic-modes-turbulent-jet.md); [Towne 2017](towne-2017-acoustic-resonance-potential-core-subsonic-jets.md); [Jordan 2018](jordan-2018-jet-flap-interaction-tones.md); p. 154).
- **Global stability** (p. 154). Resonance will be understood only by combining global and local analysis.
  - Nichols & Lele 2011: global modes of an ideally expanded cold jet include the upstream subsonic modes.
  - Beneddine et al. 2015: a laminar 2-D underexpanded jet is globally unstable, and the analysis captures the thicker-lip/louder-tone link, "something not possible with local analysis".
  - Stegeman et al. 2016 is also cited.
- **Measuring growth** (p. 154–156).
  - Wavepackets are a small fraction of the fluctuation energy ([Jaunet 2017](jaunet-2017-two-point-coherence-wave-packets-turbulent-jets.md)), so they must be educed by decomposition ([Taira 2017](taira-2017-modal-analysis-fluid-flows-overview.md)).
  - POD is the most used. In resonant flows the leading pair (90° phase offset) is a travelling wave at the tone, so the cycle can be phase-reconstructed from statistically independent snapshots (Fig. 25; [Oberleithner 2011](oberleithner-2011-swirling-jet-vortex-breakdown-stability.md)). Triple decomposition (eq. 17) isolates the coherent part (Figs 16, 27).
  - DMD ([Schmid 2010](schmid-2010-dynamic-mode-decomposition.md)) ranks modes by growth rate rather than energy and may find unstable low-energy near-nozzle modes. It has been applied to screeching and impinging jets (p. 156).
- **Shocks** (p. 156–159).
  - Locally parallel analysis cannot capture shocks at all. Global or weakly non-parallel analysis can include them, but not their nonlinear behaviour. POD and DMD ROMs fail near shocks ([Weightman 2017](weightman-2017-phase-lag-supersonic-jet-impingement.md)) (p. 157).
  - The shock–turbulence literature (Ribner; Lee, Lele & Moin; Andreopoulos et al. 2000; Ryu & Livescu 2014; Sandham 2016) leaves even planar shocks unresolved (p. 157). Jet turbulence ranges from one-component to near-isotropic, and the shocks change its topology with "no clear pattern" (Lumley triangle from LES, Fig. 26, NPR 4.2, C mode; p. 157–158).
  - POD still extracts the coherent kinematics. Coherent energy is monotonic growth plus an oscillation from the shocks or the standing wave (Fig. 27, NPR 3.4, C mode). Coherent axial length scales are modulated far more than transverse ones, while random-part scales barely are (Fig. 28; [Tan 2017](tan-2017-broadband-velocity-fluctuations-screeching-jets.md)) (p. 157–159).
  - Verdict: how far shocks invalidate linear models "is presently unclear" (p. 159).

**Generation (p. 159–168).**
- **Candidate conversions** (p. 159–160): convected density or entropy inhomogeneities (Kuo & Dowling), wave–shock interaction, wave–body interaction, and direct wavepacket radiation.
  - Acoustic analogies have handled Mach waves, BBSAN ([Tan 2019](tan-2019-equivalent-bbsan-source-reconstruction-screeching-jets.md); [Wong 2019](wong-2019-coherence-decay-wavepacket-bbsan.md)) and shock–vortex sound. Lighthill's doubt about shock emission (quoted) was too modest, but the source term remains the challenge (p. 160).
- **Vortex and infinite shock** (p. 160–162).
  - Moore 1953 and Ribner 1954 (linear): the shock distorts and emits strongly. Ribner 1959: the wave is cylindrical with azimuthal variation.
  - Hollingsworth (shock tube) gave the first experiment. Dosanjh & Weeks 1965 measured azimuthal variations above 10 dB, modelled as a monopole + dipole + quadrupole.
  - Meadows et al. 1991 and Ellzey et al. 1995 describe a two-stage process: a quadrupole-like "precursor" from the induced field, then distortion of the shock in the vortex core, with Mach stems and triple points for strong vortices. DNS (Guichard et al. 1995; Inoue & Hattori 1999) and Euler simulations (Grasso & Pirozzoli 2000) agree, so the process is inviscid.
  - Two mechanisms result. (1) The shock translates with the induced velocity, strengthening when it moves downstream and weakening when it moves upstream, and radiates compressions and rarefactions; this suffices for weak vortices. (2) A strong vortex bends the shock into regular or Mach reflection, and once the vortex has passed the non-normal parts "must become propagative" and decay into sound (Fig. 29).
- **Vortex and reflecting shock: shock leakage** (p. 162–165).
  - Manning & Lele 1998 (DNS of an oblique shock with instability waves in a finite shear layer): one cylindrical wave from the shock tip; the tip moves on a circle in the vortex's sense; the compression forms during the shock's upstream motion. There is no quadrupole, because the parts of the shock above and below the vortex do not move oppositely at a tip (p. 162).
  - A detonation-driven starting jet (Fig. 30, TU Berlin, frames 100 µs apart) shows the process "writ-large". The reflected shock ends in the starting vortex; as the vortex leaves, the shock must move along its normal, curve, and propagate back upstream (p. 162–163).
  - Manning & Lele 2000 kept "shock leakage" through progressively simpler models: linearised Euler, a Gaussian wave in place of the shock, a Stuart-vortex shear layer, and geometrical acoustics. Strong local vorticity turns the wavefront normal back into the core (total internal reflection), while a strong enough perturbation lets it leak (p. 163–164).
  - [Suzuki & Lele 2003](suzuki-2003-shock-leakage-vortex-laden-mixing-layer-screech.md) (DNS and eikonal): leakage depends on the incidence angle and on the vorticity at the reflection point (p. 164).
  - Shariff & Manning 2013 (ray tracing between parallel vortex trains): with constant moderate vorticity the first leak is several cells downstream. Leakage is set by incidence angle (itself fixed by upstream reflections) and local vorticity, "rather than simply being a function of where the vorticity is strongest" (Fig. 31; p. 164).
- **Status of leakage** (p. 164–165). "Widely, though not universally accepted." Evidence: LES (Berland, Bogey & Bailly 2007), the group's UHS schlieren and the Lyon schlieren. Shariff–Manning ray tracing on phase-reconstructed PIV vorticity predicts leaks where schlieren sees them (Fig. 32, A2, clearest at x/D ≈ 2.5; Edgington-Mitchell et al. 2018 AIAA, the conference precursor of [Edgington-Mitchell 2021, shock leakage](edgington-mitchell-2021-generation-screech-tones-shock-leakage.md)).
- **Impinging jets** (p. 165–167).
  - Subsonic tones show that surface impact alone can make sound.
  - Henderson 2005: the source is a pulsatile wall-jet boundary at 1.3D, driven by arriving wavepackets and correlated with, but not necessarily driven by, stand-off-shock motion. Glaznev 1977 had described a "membrane" simple source.
  - [Weightman et al. 2017](weightman-2017-phase-lag-supersonic-jet-impingement.md) confirmed r ≈ 1.3D with UHS schlieren, but the mechanism is a **shocklet** formed in the wall jet as a vortex passes, which must then become propagative. The lag between shocklet formation and emission explains the phase lag of eq. 16 (Fig. 33, NPR 5.0 hemisphere, frames 12 µs apart).
  - The shocklet is "not universal". Other cases radiate from the edges of the stand-off shock (Fig. 34, NPR 3.4, z = 5D; [Weightman 2019, thesis](weightman-2019-sound-production-impinging-supersonic-jets.md); Weightman et al. 2019). Gojon et al. 2016 see sound in the direct impingement region and need no phase lag, which supports "at least two different" mechanisms. That near-impingement source may be leakage at the stand-off shock or wavepacket–plate impact correlated with shock motion (p. 166–167).
- **Other mechanisms** (p. 167–168). [Towne 2017](towne-2017-acoustic-resonance-potential-core-subsonic-jets.md) and [Schmidt 2017](schmidt-2017-wavepackets-trapped-acoustic-modes-turbulent-jet.md) show that potential-core contraction reflects and transmits waves (as in Rienstra's ducts). [Jordan 2018](jordan-2018-jet-flap-interaction-tones.md) closes jet–edge tones by edge scattering and by lip scattering or an impedance-change reflection. For shock-containing jets there is "reasonable evidence" that much of the upstream energy comes from unsteady shock motion, but mean-flow reflections "cannot be excluded" (p. 168).

**Upstream (p. 168–173).**
- Historically least studied; free-stream sound was assumed because of the strong near-nozzle tones and the waves seen in schlieren (p. 168).
- **The k_TH⁻ wave** (p. 168–169). Tam & Hu's upstream neutral acoustic mode has amplitude in both core and shear layer (Fig. 35).
  - [Tam & Ahuja 1990](tam-1990-theoretical-model-discrete-tone-impinging-jets.md) (quoted) closed subsonic impinging loops with it. Supporting experiments: Lepicovsky & Ahuja 1985 found the frequency insensitive to co-flow, which a free-stream return would not be. Panickar & Raman 2007 extended the model to m = 1 at high subsonic M.
- **Trapped modes** (p. 170). Towne, Schmidt and Jordan found duct-like modes in the core: k_d⁻ (upstream), k_T⁺ (downstream) and k_p⁻ (like k_TH⁻ but propagative in another band). Their cut-on and cut-off restrict some of them to high-subsonic jets (Fig. 36).
- **Screech** (p. 170–171).
  - Shen & Tam proposed k_TH⁻ closure more than 15 years earlier but could not verify it.
  - [Gojon 2018](gojon-2018-oscillation-modes-screeching-jets.md) (LES): k_TH⁻ signatures for A1 and C match the vortex sheet.
  - [Edgington-Mitchell 2018](edgington-mitchell-2018-upstream-acoustic-modes-screech-closure.md) (PIV): signatures for A1 and A2, from a POD triple decomposition and a spatial FFT at the tone (Fig. 37, A2 at NPR 2.25), with all axisymmetric tones inside the k_TH⁻ band. Since both A stages show the wave, different closures "were unlikely to provide an explanation for mode staging".
  - [Mancinelli 2019](mancinelli-2019-screech-prediction-upstream-modes.md): tones lie between the branch-point and saddle-point tracks, and k_TH⁻ loops predict them better than free-stream loops.
- **Impingement** (p. 171–172). Gojon et al. 2016 and Bogey & Gojon 2017 explain most LES tones, including their toroidal or helical form, by a modified Powell loop with vortex-sheet upstream waves, and see k_TH⁻ in the density field: "the strongest evidence to date".
  - But there are different paths. Free-stream waves from 1.3D sources are seen striking the lip (Henderson; Weightman et al. 2017). In the previously unpublished Fig. 38 (NPR 3.1, z = 2.66D), a wave passes right through the jet, hits the lip and starts a vortex. Waves can also close the loop by reflection off upstream surfaces (Weightman et al. 2019).
- **Conclusion** (p. 172–173). "It now seems certain" that k_TH⁻ waves play a significant role in many resonances. Open: what decides between k_TH⁻ and free-stream closure (the "simple" answer is maximum gain under the phase condition); whether the generation mechanism decides it; how strong shocks affect propagation; and how the radial structure affects receptivity.

**Receptivity (p. 173–175).**
- **Vortex-sheet theory** (Jones & Morgan; Morgan; Kerschen; Pal; Bower & Pal): receptivity is highest near the thinnest part of the shear layer (p. 173).
- **Tam 1986** (JSV 105; p. 173): excitation needs matched frequency and matched streamwise phase velocity, i.e. matched wavenumber. A plane wave normal to the layer rarely matches, but a narrow beam has a broad wavenumber spectrum.
  - Tam 1978: narrower beams excite more, and beams 50–80° from the upstream direction are most effective.
  - Tam & Morris 1985 matched instability amplitudes in an M 0.58 tone-excited jet.
- **Near the lip** (p. 173–174). Self-excited receptivity happens very close to the nozzle. Lip scattering (Morkovin) broadens the wave's wavenumber content, and reflection at the lip adds streamwise amplitude variation (Raman 1997).
  - Barone & Lele 2005 (adjoint, compressible mixing layer with a splitter plate) find receptivity depends on frequency and source position, through three routes: direct excitation; scattering at the surface; and upstream travel in the subsonic boundary layer, triggering a boundary-layer instability. Plate thickness "has a significant effect", consistent with the lip-thickness results.
- **Collective interaction** (Ho & Nosseir; Ho & Huang 1982), low-frequency shear-layer motion merging many vortices, has one high-speed observation (Diebold & Elliott 2014), never reproduced. Group UHS schlieren over many impingement conditions and LES show none. In Fig. 39 (NPR 3.2, z = 4D, frames 10 µs apart) a wave reflecting off the lip makes a sinusoidal perturbation that grows into a KH structure: classical receptivity (p. 174–175).

### Outlook: consensus and open questions (p. 175–177)
- **Downstream** = KH wavepacket, with "no evidence to suggest otherwise". Open: the effect of shocks, and interaction between wavepackets of simultaneous tones (p. 175–176).
- **Generation** is "either correlated with, or caused by unsteady shock motion". It is seen directly in some cases, with no clear causal link in others. Small changes in NPR, lip, stand-off and other parameters change the source location and mechanism. Leakage evidence is "steadily accruing", but its universality is unclear (p. 176).
- **Upstream** is "on the cusp of revelation". Acoustic jet modes "clearly play a key role" but "are not always the closure mechanism". The challenge is what decides between free-stream and jet-mode closure (p. 176).
- **Receptivity** may "ironically be the first component … to receive a complete mechanistic description", since linear models suit it. Global stability and LES are moving towards quantifying η_r (p. 176).
- **Dispersive resonance condition** (eq. 18, after Jordan 2018; p. 176–177). Upstream and downstream waves k₁, k₂ are coupled by complex reflections R₁, R₂ a distance L apart. The condition is "entirely self-contained", but needs R₁R₂ (from the generation and receptivity physics) and the dispersion relation (approximated by stability theory).
- **Raman 1998's open questions, as of 2019** (p. 177):
  1. *Staging in non-rectangular jets:* "mostly an open question". The symmetric → antisymmetric switch is predicted by stability theory, but flapping ↔ helical switching lacks a phenomenological explanation. Facility sensitivity is a hindrance, "though paradoxically may also provide a pathway".
  2. *Models for shock modulation of the downstream waves:* "no such theoretical model exists", though the extraction techniques are mature.
  3. *Upstream and receptivity:* major advances; G-JM closure is "finally gaining widespread recognition", and numerical tools are quantifying receptivity.
  4. *Coexisting tones:* separate upstream processes are "not … borne out". Both simultaneous interacting tones (Weightman et al. 2019) and mutually exclusive, rapidly switching tones (Mancinelli 2019) occur, and why each happens is unknown.
  - "Many of the questions posed by Raman in 1998 remain unanswered today."

## Key equations
All checked against rendered pages.
- **Powell phase criterion** (eq. 1, p. 120): $\dfrac{N}{f}=\dfrac{h}{U_1}+\dfrac{h}{U_2}+\psi$. U₁, U₂: mean downstream and upstream speeds; h: distance to the interaction; ψ: extra delay (a time, unlike the phase fraction p of eq. 16).
- **Gain criterion** (eq. 2, p. 121): $q_d\,\eta_g\,\eta_u\,\eta_r \ge 1$.
- **Phased-array directivity, three monopoles** (eqs. 3–4, p. 124–125): $D_f=\tfrac13+\tfrac23\cos\!\left[\dfrac{2\pi s}{\lambda_L}(1-M_c\cos\theta)\right]$, $D_h=\tfrac13+\tfrac23\cos\!\left[\dfrac{4\pi s}{\lambda_L}(1-M_c\cos\theta)\right]$, with θ from the downstream axis, M_c = U_c/a∞ and λ_L the hydrodynamic wavelength. (My check: |1 + e^{iφ} + e^{2iφ}|/3 = (1 + 2cos φ)/3 with φ = 2πs/λ_L − k_a s cos θ.)
- **Powell's screech formula** (eq. 5, p. 125): $f=\dfrac{U_c}{s(1+M_c)}$. It follows from eq. 3 when D_f = 1 at θ = 180°, or from eq. 1 with ψ = 0, h = ns, n = N.
- **Standing wave** (eqs. 6–8, p. 126): $k_{sw}=k_A+k_L$, $\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{sw}}=\dfrac{1}{\lambda_A}+\dfrac{1}{\lambda_L}$, with λ_A = a∞/f_s and λ_L = U_c/f_s, giving $f_s=\dfrac{U_c}{\lambda_{sw}(1+M_c)}$.
- **BBSAN peaks** (eq. 9, phased array, p. 127): $f_{BBSAN}=\dfrac{nU_c}{s(1-M_c\cos\theta)}$, n = 1, 2, 3, …; (eq. 10, Tam & Tanna, p. 128): $f_{BBSAN}=\dfrac{U_c k_n}{2\pi(1-M_c\cos\theta)}$.
- **BBSAN bandwidth** (eq. 11, p. 128), printed as $\Delta f=\big(k_1/2\pi(1-U_c/a_\infty\cos\theta)^2\big)\Delta U$.
  > The bracketing is ambiguous as printed. Differentiating eq. 10 with respect to U_c gives $\Delta f=\dfrac{k_1\,\Delta U}{2\pi\,(1-M_c\cos\theta)^2}$, which is the intended form (my derivation), and it narrows towards θ = 180° as stated.
- **Weakest-link screech frequency** (eq. 12, p. 129): $f_s=\dfrac{U_c k_n}{2\pi(1+M_c)}$; with k₁ = 2π/s this is eq. 5.
- **Tam et al. semi-empirical formula** (eq. 13, p. 129): $\dfrac{f_s D_j}{U_j}=\dfrac{0.67}{\sqrt{M_j^2-1}}\left[1+0.7M_j\left(1+\dfrac{\gamma-1}{2}M_j^2\right)^{-1/2}\left(\dfrac{T_a}{T_r}\right)^{-1/2}\right]^{-1}$, with T_a ambient and T_r reservoir temperature. My check: cold, M_j = 1.5 gives 0.32.
- **Fully expanded diameter** (eq. 14, p. 130): $\dfrac{D_j}{D}=\left[\dfrac{1+\frac12(\gamma-1)M_j^2}{1+\frac12(\gamma-1)M_d^2}\right]^{\frac{\gamma+1}{4(\gamma-1)}}\left(\dfrac{M_d}{M_j}\right)^{1/2}$. My check: M_d = 1, M_j = 1.5 gives 1.085.
- **Gao & Li wavelength** (eq. 15, p. 132): $\lambda_s=\dfrac{ns}{N}\,\dfrac{1+M_c}{M_c}$, with ns the effective source distance (n = 5) and N the concurrent cycles (5 flapping, 6 helical). It is eq. 1 with ψ = 0, U₂ = a∞, h = ns, λ_s = a∞/f.
- **Powell impingement criterion** (eq. 16, p. 142): $\dfrac{N+p}{f}=\displaystyle\int^{h_c}\dfrac{dh}{u_c}+\dfrac{h_s}{U_s}$. u_c may vary along the downstream path h_c; U_s is the constant upstream speed over h_s; p is the phase lag (fraction of a cycle).
- **Triple decomposition** (eq. 17, p. 156): $u(x,t)=U(x)+u^c(x,t)+u''(x,t)$.
- **Dispersive resonance condition** (eq. 18, p. 176, after Jordan 2018): $e^{\Delta k_i L}=|R_1R_2|$, $\Delta k_r L+\phi=2n\pi$, $D(k,\omega,M,T)=0$. φ is the phase of R₁R₂ and D the dispersion relation.
  > Δk is not defined in the text; it is presumably k₁ − k₂ (downstream minus upstream wavenumber), the convention of Jordan 2018 and Mancinelli 2019, for which |R₁R₂|e^{−Δk_i L} = 1.

## Notation
- f, f_s: tone (screech) frequency; N: integer number of cycles in the loop; n: shock index or Fourier order; p: phase lag (fraction of a cycle, eq. 16); ψ: extra delay (time, eq. 1).
- h: distance from nozzle to interaction or plate; h_c, h_s: downstream and upstream path lengths; z: nozzle-to-plate distance; L: distance between reflection points (eq. 18).
- U₁, U₂: downstream and upstream speeds; U_c, u_c: convection speed; U_s: upstream speed; a∞: ambient sound speed; M_c = U_c/a∞; M_j, M_d: fully expanded and design Mach numbers; U_j: fully expanded velocity.
- s: shock spacing; λ_L, k_L: hydrodynamic wavelength and wavenumber; λ_A, k_A: acoustic; λ_sw, k_sw: standing wave; k_n: n-th Fourier wavenumber of the shock train; λ_s: screech wavelength.
- θ, Θ: angle from the downstream axis; D_f, D_h: fundamental and harmonic directivity.
- q_d: downstream gain; η_g, η_u, η_r: generation, upstream-transmission and receptivity efficiencies.
- D, D_j: nozzle and fully expanded diameters; T_a, T_r: ambient and reservoir temperatures; γ: ratio of specific heats.
- m: azimuthal order; (m, n): azimuthal–radial order of acoustic jet modes; k_KH⁺: KH wave; k_TH⁻: Tam & Hu upstream neutral acoustic (guided) mode; k_p⁻, k_d⁻, k_T⁺: other trapped or guided waves; R₁, R₂: complex end reflection coefficients; D(k, ω, M, T): dispersion relation.
- U, u^c, u″: mean, coherent and incoherent velocity (eq. 17).

## Relevance to this project
This review is the group's own map of the two resonance threads at the centre of the CLAUDE.md research area: screech closure and staging, and impinging-jet feedback. It is also the hinge between [Raman 1998](raman-1998-supersonic-jet-screech-review.md) and the guided-mode and shock-spectrum papers that followed. Its four-process framework (Fig. 3) is the vocabulary used by later group papers and by this library's topic pages. Read it today as a 2019 snapshot: several of its open questions have since been answered, and one of its framings has been revised.

**How it fits and what has changed since**
- **Generation and closure have been separated for free-jet screech.**
  - The review's "generation process" makes the upstream disturbance and is "also typically responsible for" the tone (p. 120). Shock leakage at the shock tips is the favoured mechanism (p. 139, 164–165), and the G-JM is treated as one of two possible return paths (p. 172–173, 176).
  - Since then, leakage has been imaged directly ([Edgington-Mitchell 2021, shock leakage](edgington-mitchell-2021-generation-screech-tones-shock-leakage.md)). But [Edgington-Mitchell 2022](edgington-mitchell-2022-unifying-theory-jet-screech.md) and [Nogueira 2024](nogueira-2024-guided-jet-waves.md) assign closure to the KH–shock triad at k_kh − k_s feeding the G-JM inside the jet. Leaked fronts are born outside the jet and cannot feed the G-JM (Nogueira 2024, p. 14, 32), so leakage is the radiation, not necessarily the return (see [screech](../Topics/screech.md), "Leakage as closure").
  - So for free-jet screech the review's η_g (the loop) and the tone source are now two processes. The four-process diagram still holds if "generation" is read as the triad.
  - For impinging jets the review's view stands: the source location decides the return path (p. 172). That is the later reconciliation of the "two schools" ([Edgington-Mitchell 2025](edgington-mitchell-2025-vortices-shocks-nonlinear-waves-impinging-jet-resonance.md), p. 14–25; Nogueira 2024, p. 31–33).
- **Staging: the review holds the seeds of the k_s1/k_s2 answer.** Its two length-scale observations (p. 133) are what [Edgington-Mitchell 2022](edgington-mitchell-2022-unifying-theory-jet-screech.md) (p. 11) and [Nogueira 2022](nogueira-2021-closure-mechanism-a1-a2-screech.md) later formalised: the standing wavenumber follows k_s1 in A1 and B and k_s2 in A2 and C.
  - A1 obeys Powell's formula with the shock spacing and A2 Panda's formula with λ_sw (Singh & Chatterjee).
  - The elliptical jet's λ_sw equals the shock spacing in the B-like stage but not in the C-like stage.
  - The review's alternative, "changes in the N value", is the same statement in loop-count form, as [Mercier 2017](mercier-2017-screech-feedback-loop-underexpanded-round-jets.md)'s m/N = 5/4 shows ([screech-staging-and-mode-switching](../Topics/screech-staging-and-mode-switching.md)). The review's conclusion that the G-JM in both A stages rules out Shen & Tam's two-closure explanation (p. 132, 171) is now consensus.
- **Coexistence or exclusion** (open question 4, p. 177). Since quantified for A1/A2 as exclusive switching with seconds-long dwell times, each state on its own short-time mean ([Mancinelli 2019](mancinelli-2019-screech-prediction-upstream-modes.md); [Mazharmanesh 2026](mazharmanesh-2026-triadic-interaction-mean-flow-deformation-screech.md)). Why some tones coexist and others exclude each other is still open.
- **Shock modulation** (open question 2: "no such theoretical model exists", p. 177). Since then Floquet ([Nogueira 2021, absolute instability](nogueira-2021-absolute-instability-shock-containing-jets.md)), parabolised ([Nogueira 2022, wavepacket modulation](nogueira-2021-wavepacket-modulation-shock-containing-jets.md)) and global or resolvent models with shocks ([Edgington-Mitchell 2021, waves](edgington-mitchell-2020-waves-in-screeching-jets.md); [Farghadan 2026](farghadan-2026-wave-interactions-screeching-jet.md)) do this.
  - The review's "two-way coupling" reading of the standing wave (p. 126) was refined by Edgington-Mitchell 2021, waves: most of the standing wave and core modulation is G-JM–KH interference rather than shock modulation of KH growth.
- **Receptivity first?** The prediction that receptivity would be the first process modelled completely (p. 176) has not come true in this library. [Mancinelli 2021](mancinelli-2021-complex-valued-resonance-model-screech.md) still fits |R₁R₂| ≈ 0.1–0.3 and only proposes a Wiener–Hopf model. Barone & Lele's adjoint (p. 174) remains the template; no library paper computes η_r for a screeching nozzle.
- **Eq. 18** became the working framework of [Mancinelli 2019](mancinelli-2019-screech-prediction-upstream-modes.md), [Mancinelli 2021](mancinelli-2021-complex-valued-resonance-model-screech.md), [Jaunet 2019](jaunet-2019-dynamics-round-jet-impingement.md) and [Stavropoulos 2026](stavropoulos-2026-jet-edge-interaction.md).
- **Agreements and contradictions.**
  - On source location the review agrees with [Panda 1999](panda-1999-experimental-investigation-screech-noise-generation.md), Mercier 2017 and [Suzuki & Lele 2003](suzuki-2003-shock-leakage-vortex-laden-mixing-layer-screech.md), and contradicts [Tam 1986](tam-1986-bbsan-screech-relationship.md) and Tam et al. 2014 (core emission).
  - On impinging receptivity its rejection of collective interaction (p. 174) agrees with [Karami 2020](karami-2020-acoustic-hydrodynamic-waves-underexpanded-impinging-jets.md) (p. 12–13) and contradicts [Ho 1981](ho-1981-dynamics-impinging-jet-feedback-phenomenon.md).
  - Its rule that Mach-dependent amplification drives staging (p. 154) cannot separate A1 from A2, which share m = 0 ([screech-staging-and-mode-switching](../Topics/screech-staging-and-mode-switching.md) item 1).
- **Rectangular jets with thick lips stage** (p. 133). This caveat is missing from the non-axisymmetric thread, which states that AR ≥ 4 jets do not stage (Edgington-Mitchell 2022, p. 18–19). The two fit if a thick lip changes receptivity enough to sustain a weaker k_s2 loop (my reading).

**Methods and data for the lab workflow (CLAUDE.md)**
- **Non-time-resolved (PIV, single-shot schlieren).** POD-pair phase reconstruction, triple decomposition, the k_x split of the reconstructed field (Fig. 37), coherent-energy and length-scale maps against the shock positions (Figs 27–28), and ray tracing on reconstructed vorticity (Fig. 32). All need one dominant, steady tone. Phase reconstructions smooth out shocks and acoustic fronts, and cannot show switching, coexistence or cycle-to-cycle variation.
  - The standing-wave map log₁₀ σ(dρ/dx) (Fig. 5) needs only uncorrelated schlieren images.
- **Time-resolved data.** UHS schlieren at 8–20 µs frame spacing resolves single events: leakage, shock splitting, the shocklet, receptivity at the lip (Figs 11–12, 17, 33–34, 38–39). The review recommends short-time FFT, wavelets and Hilbert analysis for intermittent switching (p. 131), and mentions DMD (p. 156), but no SPOD, BMD or HPOD.
- **For the SPOD → reconstruction → HPOD pipeline**, the review supplies four targets:
  1. Exclusive against simultaneous tones (open question 4): rebuild each tone band from SPOD and test anti-correlation of the HPOD amplitudes.
  2. Shock oscillation phase-locked to the cycle and mode-dependent (axial in A, transverse in flapping, circular in helical; p. 137). Shock splitting at the 3rd–4th cells should appear as a cycle-dependent second shock in the reconstructed field, and its amplitude can be tracked per cycle.
  3. Intermittent impinging tone strength (Gojon & Bogey 2017; p. 147) and the shocklet lag that sets the phase lag (p. 166). Cycle-to-cycle jitter of that lag should appear as instantaneous-frequency wander ([impinging-jets-and-edge-tones](../Topics/impinging-jets-and-edge-tones.md), "The phase lag").
  4. Precession of B and D flapping planes (p. 130), as a slow phase drift between the two leading HPOD modes.
- **Reporting.** Record lip thickness, boundary-layer thickness and flange or exterior geometry in every campaign (p. 130). This is the same lesson as [Bell 2021](bell-2021-intermittent-modal-coupling-screeching-twin-jets.md) and [Weightman et al. 2019](weightman-2019-nozzle-external-geometry-azimuthal-mode-impinging-jet.md).

**Modelling side and the PyFR staging LES** (`Simulations/PyFR_Staging_Continuation/`)
- Powell's framing, eqs. 1–2 together, with a jump when the gain-maximising N changes (p. 122, 133), is the hypothesis behind the continuation driver's search for staging limits. A saddle-node fit of 1/t_switch tests whether the exchange is a bifurcation, which eqs. 1–2 do not describe.
- Checks of the review's claims in converged A1 and A2 states:
  - Shock oscillation is axial and phase-locked, with splitting at the 4th cell in A1 near NPR 2.15 (Fig. 11).
  - The A2 "tilted toroid" is m = 0 plus weak m = ±1 (p. 130): azimuthal decomposition of the A2 state tests this directly (my suggestion).
  - The first leak moves from the 3rd to the 4th cell on staging (Fig. 32; Edgington-Mitchell 2021, shock leakage).
  - Gao & Li's integer N, read from lip-line phase.
  - Heating should leave St unchanged at fixed NPR (Shen & Tam 2000; p. 136).
- Model the nozzle lip, exterior and any flange as built (p. 130, 134–135, 148). The lip effect is mainly acoustic, through receptivity and reflection, so a sponge where the experiment has a reflecting face changes the loop. Projecting the near-lip LES field onto KH and G-JM eigenfunctions would estimate the R₁, R₂ of eq. 18, i.e. the η_r the review expected to be quantified first.
- Do not use Tam's eq. 13 to set expectations in the A1/A2 range: it is calibrated for helical stages above M_j ≈ 1.3 ([screech-frequency-and-global-modes](../Topics/screech-frequency-and-global-modes.md)).
- The review's caution that POD and DMD ROMs fail near shocks (p. 157) applies to any reduced model built from the LES.

**Rocket noise.** The review motivates impingement by VTOL and rocket launch, blast deflectors and flame-deflector LES (Tatsukawa et al. 2016; p. 147). Full-scale pads show no impingement tones or screech ([Lubert 2022](lubert-2022-supersonic-jet-noise-launch-vehicles-review.md)). Several mechanisms here are candidate reasons beyond masking:
- very hot jets often do not screech (p. 136);
- zones of silence depend on where the plate sits in the shock train (p. 143–144);
- inclined plates spread the feedback path lengths (p. 147);
- the stand-off-shock and shocklet sources need specific shock structures (p. 165–167).
Kuo & Dowling's entropy-wave loop (p. 143) may matter more in hot, combusting plumes than in cold lab jets (my inference).
