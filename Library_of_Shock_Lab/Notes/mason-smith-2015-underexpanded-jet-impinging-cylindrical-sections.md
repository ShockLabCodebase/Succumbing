## Summary
The paper asks how a cylindrical impingement surface changes the shock structure and the resonance of an underexpanded jet (p. 612). A 15 mm converging nozzle at NPR 2.2–4.2 fires at a flat plate and at convex and concave aluminium half-cylinders of radius 2.5d, mostly at h/d = 3.5. Schlieren (1000 images per case: mean, RMS, two-point correlation) is combined with one microphone (p. 612–614). Curvature barely changes the mean shock structure but changes the dynamics a great deal (p. 611). On the convex cylinder a flapping oscillation at NPR 3.4 stays in one plane, normal to the cylinder axis, while on the flat plate the flapping plane precesses; the 0.40-family tone frequency hardly changes (p. 617). On the concave half-cylinder no tone passes the 10 dB criterion for NPR 2.9–3.9, and at NPR 3.4 the jet looks undisturbed (p. 614, 617). After rejecting plate position and acoustic interference, the authors propose that the wall jet, turned back into the entrainment field by the concave surface, thickens and disturbs the initial shear layer and so lowers its receptivity, as nozzle tabs or microjets do. Their support is a coefficient of variation of schlieren intensity in the entrainment region about three times higher on the concave plate (0.2 against 0.06, Table 1, p. 620–621). There is no model or simulation; Powell's feedback equation is quoted only to interpret trends.

## Key findings
- **Mean shock structure changes little with curvature** (Fig. 4, p. 614–615; h/d 3.5).
  - At NPR 3.0 the shock cells sit at similar axial positions on the flat plate and the convex cylinder in both views. The second cell is not clearly defined, which suggests an unsteady structure. A strong dome-shaped shock in the first cell indicates collapse of the downstream structure (Henderson 2002, ref. 7) (p. 614).
  - On the convex cylinder viewed off-axis the plate shock is upturned at its ends, as if "draped" over the cylinder, so the structure is no longer axisymmetric (p. 614).
  - On the concave plate the structure looks stable: two shock cells and a plate shock are clearly defined, the plate shock is again upturned at its ends, and the first shock cell sits further downstream than on the other plates (p. 614). The authors add that "a stable plate shock reduces flow velocity considerably before impact", and that the collapse of the plate shock forces the shock cells from downstream (Henderson, Bridges & Wernet 2005, ref. 8) (p. 614). They do not return to this source-side point in Sec. 3.3.
  - My reading of Fig. 4: the first-cell shock sits at x/d ≈ 1.3 (NPR 3.0, flat and convex) and ≈ 1.45–1.5 (NPR 3.4), against ≈ 1.6 on the concave plate at NPR 3.4. The concave plate's circular profile reaches the image edges (y/d = ±2.5) at x/d ≈ 1.0, as the geometry requires (Setup).
  - From NPR 3.0 to 3.4 the jet's spread after the first cell is much reduced in the convex off-axis view, while in the on-axis view the boundary widens. Shocks are sharper off-axis, i.e. they move less in that plane, so shock motion on the convex plate is not axisymmetric at NPR 3.4 (p. 614).
- **RMS images separate shock motion from convected structures** (Fig. 5, p. 614, 616).
  - At NPR 3.0 the first cell on the flat and convex plates stays axially fixed but switches between sharply angled oblique shocks and a dome-shaped shock. The convex plate shock looks larger off-axis, so curvature alters the plate shock even where it leaves the first cell alone (p. 614).
  - At NPR 3.4 the flat plate and the convex on-axis view show fluctuations of the first and downstream cells. Off-axis the fluctuations are localised and the spread is reduced (p. 614).
  - On the concave plate fluctuations are confined to the shocks, i.e. no coherent structures convect along the jet (p. 614). The text says "NPR 3.0 and NPR 3.4", but the concave images are at NPR 2.9 and 3.4 (Figs 4–5 captions).
- **Tone map at h/d = 3.5** (Fig. 6, p. 614, 617; table below). Tones are found with a 10 dB peak-finding threshold; only the dominant fundamental is plotted, though "multiple tones are present at many cases" (p. 614).
  - **Concave: no tone for NPR 2.9–3.9.** Tones appear at NPR 2.3–2.8 (St ≈ 0.40–0.41) and 4.0–4.2 (St ≈ 0.42–0.43), each close to the flat plate's St (p. 614; Fig. 6).
  - Because the flat and convex plates at the same h do produce tones, the authors argue that this zone of silence is not tied to where the plate sits in the free-jet shock-cell structure, unlike Henderson's zones of silence (p. 614, 618).
  - The flat and convex tones lie on lines of "essentially invariant" Strouhal number, and the rise of St with NPR is put down to faster convection in Powell's equation (p. 614). From the figure the rise is small: 0.401 → 0.411 for the convex plate over NPR 3.4–4.0 and 0.418 → 0.419 for the flat plate; the flat plate's NPR 2.8–3.3 branch falls slightly (0.564 → 0.558) (my reading).
  - **Branches (my reading of Fig. 6; not discussed by the authors).** The flat plate jumps from ≈ 0.41 (NPR 2.2, 2.4–2.7) to ≈ 0.56 (2.3 and 2.8–3.3) and back to ≈ 0.42 (≥ 3.4). The convex cylinder gives 0.55–0.65 at NPR 2.2–2.4, ≈ 0.40 at 2.5–2.8, **≈ 0.278 at 2.9–3.3**, 0.40–0.41 at 3.4–4.0 and ≈ 0.585 at 4.1–4.2. Over NPR 2.9–3.3 the convex tone is half the flat-plate tone to within 1% (ratio 0.495–0.497). The jump between NPR 3.3 and 3.4 coincides with the change from the axisymmetric (NPR 3.0) to the flapping (NPR 3.4) mode found below.
  - The abstract's "oscillation frequency is largely unaffected" by convex curvature (p. 611) holds for the 0.40-family (4% below the flat plate at NPR 3.4) but not at NPR 2.2–2.4, 2.9–3.3 or 4.1–4.2, where flat and convex dominant tones differ by up to a factor of 2 (my reading).
- **Mode type from instantaneous images and two-point correlation** (Figs 7–9, p. 615–617).
  - At NPR 3.0, coherent structures and acoustic waves are symmetric about the centreline on the flat and convex plates; at NPR 3.4 they are of opposite phase on the two sides (p. 615–616). On the concave plate neither waves nor coherent structures can be seen, consistent with the absence of tones (p. 616).
  - The correlation maps at NPR 3.0 are symmetric, with the same sign on both sides of the jet: an axisymmetric mode (p. 617).
  - **Convex, NPR 3.4: flapping in a fixed plane.** The on-axis view (image plane xy, line of sight along the cylinder axis) gives a distinctly antisymmetric map; the off-axis view (xz) stays symmetric. So the jet flaps in the plane orthogonal to the cylinder axis, i.e. the flapping plane's normal is parallel to the cylinder axis (p. 617, 621). This contrasts with free underexpanded jets, whose flapping plane need not stay fixed (Tam 1995, ref. 21) (p. 617).
  - **Flat plate: the flapping plane precesses.** Single images show every orientation between an asymmetric shear-layer pattern (flapping plane parallel to the image, Fig. 7 right) and symmetric bands across the jet (flapping plane orthogonal to the image, Fig. 9 left), so the two-point correlation of the flat-plate flapping case is weak (p. 617).
  - Curvature "appears not to influence the type of instability" but stops the precession, possibly by preferentially destabilising the shock and so altering the feedback to the nozzle (p. 617). This is offered without a test.
  - **Band spacing in Fig. 8 (my reading, not discussed by the authors).** At NPR 3.0 the flat and convex maps, in both views, are almost identical: alternating arched bands across the whole field, positive near x/d ≈ 0.3 and 2.1 and negative near 1.3 and 2.9, an axial period of about 1.8–1.9d. That matches the acoustic wavelength at St ≈ 0.56 (λ/d = 1.10/St ≈ 1.96, eq. 4) and not at the convex plate's plotted tone of 0.278 (≈ 3.9d). At NPR 3.4 the convex on-axis antisymmetric pattern has a longer period, about 2.8–3d, consistent with St 0.40 (2.7d). So the convex jet at NPR 3.0 seems to carry the same ≈ 0.56 oscillation as the flat-plate jet, and its 0.278 "dominant" tone may be a subharmonic (period doubling) of it.
- **Weak concave tone at NPR 3.4** (Fig. 10, p. 617, 620). Strong tones at St 0.418 (flat) and 0.401 (convex) coexist with a weak one at St 0.419 on the concave plate, whose jet "appears undisturbed from its mean structure" (Fig. 5, bottom right) (p. 617).
  - Read from the vector data (caption offsets undone: concave −10 dB, flat −20 dB): convex 129.6 dB at St 0.401 with a second harmonic of 128.5 dB at 0.801; flat 123.4 dB at 0.415 and 112.8 dB at 0.832; concave 110.1 dB at 0.418, only about 8 dB above its local broadband level, which is why it is absent from Fig. 6 (my reading).
  - The convex second harmonic is only 1.1 dB below the fundamental, against 10.6 dB for the flat plate. The microphone's azimuth relative to the cylinder axis is not stated; for a fixed-plane flapping mode the fundamental radiates antisymmetrically and the harmonic symmetrically, so the ratio depends on that azimuth (my point).
  - The convex spectrum also has weak peaks at St ≈ 0.158 and 0.265 and at sidebands of every harmonic: 0.557 ≈ 0.401 + 0.158, 0.667 ≈ 0.401 + 0.265, 0.958 ≈ 0.801 + 0.158, 1.467 ≈ 1.202 + 0.265, 1.869 ≈ 1.603 + 0.265, 2.269 ≈ 2.003 + 0.265. A weak component near 0.265, close to the convex 0.278 branch at NPR 2.9–3.3, therefore seems to coexist and interact quadratically with the flapping tone. The flat spectrum has weak peaks at 0.147, 0.227, 0.263, 0.567 and 0.680 ≈ 0.415 + 0.265 (my reading and interpretation; the paper does not discuss these peaks).
- **Mechanisms for suppression on the concave plate** (p. 617–621).
  - *Plate position* (Henderson's zones of silence, ref. 8): rejected because flat and convex plates at the same h produce tones (p. 618).
  - *Reflection and destructive interference at the lip* (Henderson & Powell 1993, ref. 6): rejected because, when Henderson and Powell's reflector suppressed a tone, other tones were amplified, whereas the concave spectrum has no stronger peak than the flat or convex spectra at any St (p. 618).
  - *Reduced receptivity of the shear layer* (favoured). Microjets suppress tones by thickening the initial shear layer and adding streamwise vorticity (Alvi et al. 2008, ref. 1). Low-speed jets on concave cylinders lose their ring vortices, which was attributed to the wall jet recirculating into the shear layer (Cornaro et al. 1999, ref. 3) (p. 612, 618–619). The concave surface turns the turbulent wall jet into the entrainment field. This could disturb the shear layer less impulsively than the acoustic feedback does and increase its mixing, as nozzle tabs do when they suppress screech (Reeder & Samimy 1996, ref. 18). A thicker shear layer is less receptive and the loop breaks (p. 619–621).
- **Evidence from the entrainment field** (Figs 11–12, Table 1, p. 620–621; h/d 4.5, NPR 4.0, a zone of silence for both curved plates).
  - On the convex plate the wall jet is carried away from the nozzle, and the intensity variations in the entrainment region are mainly peaks and troughs of axially travelling waves. On the concave plate they are much more varied and random, strongest at the mixing layer where the wall jet leaves the plate (Fig. 7, bottom), and are attributed to vortical structures in the recirculated wall jet (p. 620).
  - **Coefficient of variation σ/μ of intensity in a region beside the nozzle exit: 0.2 concave, 0.06 convex** (Table 1, p. 621), from 1000 images, about 80 million samples (p. 620). The authors read the factor of about 3 as mixing in the recirculating turbulent wall jet, while noting that schlieren cannot measure the flow properties behind it (p. 621).
  - Fig. 12 (convex, h/d 4.5) shows a resonating jet with acoustic waves at NPR 3.8 and a quiet one at NPR 4.0, to show that the comparison is made without tones (p. 620–621).
- **Conclusions** (p. 621): mean shock structures are asymmetric on the convex plate and depend on NPR; convex curvature constrains flapping to a single plane whose normal is parallel to the cylinder axis; concave curvature suppresses impingement tones, attributed to recirculation of the turbulent wall jet into the entrainment field. The abstract generalises these results to "moderate standoff distances (h/d < 5)" (p. 611), but only h/d 3.5 and 4.5 were tested.

## Setup / method
- **Facility** (Fig. 2, p. 612–613). Continuous compressed air (for NPR < 5) through a plenum with honeycombs and mesh screens to a converging nozzle, exit diameter d = 15 mm. NPR is held by a Fairchild 100 High Flow regulator (delivery stability < 0.007 bar per 7 bar of upstream variation) and measured with an RS-461 transducer (0–10 bar, ±0.25%). The schematic is adapted from [Mitchell et al. 2013](mitchell-2013-near-field-structure-underexpanded-elliptic-jets.md) (ref. 15). Supply temperature, lip thickness, contraction and exit boundary layer are not given; Re is not stated.
  - The exit velocity, u_e = 310 m/s, is taken from the PIV of [Edgington-Mitchell et al. 2014](edgington-mitchell-2014-coherent-structure-helical-screech-mode.md) (ref. 5) (p. 613–614), whose 15 mm nozzle has a 5 mm lip (t/D 0.33). That suggests, but does not establish, the same nozzle; the group's earlier 15 mm impinging nozzle had a lip of about 2.5 mm, t/d ≈ 0.17 ([Mitchell et al. 2012](mitchell-2012-visualization-acoustic-feedback-loop-impinging-jet-schlieren.md) note). A sonic exit at 310 m/s implies T₀ ≈ 287 K (my arithmetic).
- **Surfaces** (p. 612–613). Convex and concave aluminium half-cylinders of radius 37.5 mm (2.5d) and length 115 mm, and a flat plate (size not given). All are wider than 2d and so count as "large plates" in Henderson's sense (ref. 7). They sit on a positioning rig; h is set with gauge blocks between the nozzle lip and the geometric impingement point, and the measured h is used (p. 613).
  - Geometry (my derivation): for the concave plate at h/d 3.5 the cylinder axis lies at x/d = h/d − 2.5 = 1.0, so the plate's rims are at x/d ≈ 1.0, |y/d| = 2.5 (as seen in Fig. 4), and the wall jet leaves the plate 1d from the nozzle plane, heading back towards it. At h/d 4.5 the rims are at x/d ≈ 2.0.
- **Coordinates and views** (Fig. 3, p. 612–613). Origin at the nozzle-exit centre, x along the jet axis, z parallel to the cylinder axis. The convex cylinder is imaged in the xy plane ("on-axis", line of sight along the cylinder axis) and the xz plane ("off-axis"). Flat-plate results use r. The concave plate is shown only on-axis (xy).
- **Conditions.** Tone map: NPR 2.2–4.2 in 0.1 steps at h/d 3.5 (Fig. 6). Imaging at h/d 3.5: NPR 3.0 (flat and convex) or 2.9 (concave), and 3.4; chosen because their spectra differ considerably although the pressures are close, so the changes in shock structure are not due to NPR alone (p. 614). Entrainment comparison: h/d 4.5, NPR 4.0 (concave and convex), plus convex NPR 3.8 (Figs 11–12). M_j, Re and temperature are not stated (my isentropic M_j: 1.33 at NPR 2.9, 1.36 at 3.0, 1.45 at 3.4, 1.56 at 4.0).
- **Schlieren** (p. 613). Section 2.3 is titled "Shadowgraph and schlieren apparatus", but only schlieren is described and shown. Toepler Z-type; two 200 mm parabolic mirrors, focal length 2 m; Phlatlight CBT-120 LED, which gives less chromatic aberration than flash-lit schlieren and allows many exposures in quick succession (Willert et al. 2012, ref. 22). PCO Dimax camera, 12-bit, 20 px/mm, 1.28 µs integration time. Knife edge perpendicular to the jet axis (axial density gradients; expansion dark, compression bright, p. 614), 70% cut-off. 1000 images per plate–height–NPR case. The frame rate is not stated; the images are used as an ensemble, not as a time series.
- **Acoustics** (p. 613). G.R.A.S. 46BE ¼" free-field microphone aimed at the impingement point, in the nozzle-lip plane, 16d from the impingement point (≈ 15.6d from the jet axis and ≈ 103° from the downstream jet axis seen from the impingement point, my geometry). Its azimuth relative to the cylinder axis is not stated. NI USB-6212 16-bit DAQ with LabVIEW, 200 kHz, 500,000 samples (2.5 s). Spectra by FFT with a sliding Hann window; block length, overlap and frequency resolution are not given. An impingement tone is a peak at least 10 dB above the local SPL (p. 613, 614). The Fig. 10 data end at St 4.838, the 100 kHz Nyquist frequency for St = f d/(310 m/s) (my check), so u_e is fixed at 310 m/s for every NPR.
- **Image analyses** (all need only independent snapshots; none needs time resolution):
  - mean and RMS of the 1000 images (Figs 4–5, p. 614). Blurred shocks in the mean and bright shocks in the RMS indicate motion;
  - two-point correlation coefficient of intensity with one reference pixel (eq. 5, Fig. 8, p. 616–617). The reference lies in the initial shear layer at r/d ≈ 0.5 (x/d ≈ 0.2 at NPR 3.0, ≈ 0.1 at NPR 3.4, read from the figure), except for the convex off-axis case at NPR 3.4, where it is on the axis at x/d ≈ 0.95 (read from figure) because the disturbances do not reach the visible shear layer (p. 617). The authors stress that the correlated quantity is a path-integrated density gradient, not a fluid property, so the maps are a visualisation (p. 617);
  - coefficient of variation σ/μ of intensity over a region of interest, pooled over images and pixels (≈ 80 million samples): x/d 0–1, y/d 1–2, beside the nozzle exit (Fig. 11, read from figure; p. 620). At 20 px/mm a 1d × 1d square is 300 × 300 px, so 1000 images give 9 × 10⁷ samples, consistent with the stated 80 million (my check).
- **Modelling side.** None. Powell's feedback equation (eq. 2) is stated (p. 612) and used only in words to explain the St trend (p. 614). There is no stability calculation, no base flow, no LES, and no acoustic or receptivity model. The proposed receptivity mechanism rests on analogy with microjets, tabs and low-speed concave impingement (p. 618–619). The introduction adds Tam & Ahuja's view that flapping is the sum of two counter-rotating helical oscillations (ref. 20) (p. 612).

## Key equations
- Nozzle pressure ratio (eq. 1, p. 611): $\mathrm{NPR}=p_0/p_a$.
- Powell's feedback equation (eq. 2, p. 612): $\dfrac{N+p}{f}=\displaystyle\int_0^{h'}\frac{\mathrm{d}h}{c_0}+\int_0^{h'}\frac{\mathrm{d}h}{u_{\mathrm{con}}}$, with N the number of disturbances per cycle, p the combined phase lag at source and nozzle, h′ the nozzle-to-source distance, c₀ the ambient sound speed and u_con the convection speed.
- Strouhal number (eq. 3, p. 613): $St_{\mathrm{nozzle}}=f d/u_e$, with u_e = 310 m/s (p. 614). So **f = St × 20.67 kHz** for every case (my conversion, d = 15 mm).
- Wavelength for comparison with earlier λ/d data (eq. 4, p. 614): $\lambda/d=c_a/(St\,u_e)$. With c_a ≈ 340 m/s this is λ/d ≈ 1.10/St (my value; c_a is not stated). The text's remark that St = d/λ for a cold choked jet assumes u_e = c_a, which holds only if the stagnation temperature exceeds ambient; with T₀ = T_a, u_e ≈ 0.91c_a.
- Two-point correlation (eq. 5, p. 616–617): $R_{x,y}=\dfrac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\mu_x)(y_i-\mu_y)}{n\,\sigma_x\sigma_y}$, over n images, with μ and σ the mean and standard deviation of each pixel's intensity.
- Coefficient of variation (p. 620): $(\sigma/\mu)_{\mathrm{roi}}$ of intensity over the region of interest.
- **Conversion to the f D_j/U_j convention** (my calculation; isentropic, γ = 1.4, T₀ ≈ 287 K): $St_j=St_{\mathrm{nozzle}}\,(D_j/d)\,(u_e/U_j)$, with factor 0.822 at NPR 3.0, 0.803 at 3.4, 0.797 at 3.6 and 0.788 at 4.0. For example, the NPR 3.4 tones become St_j ≈ 0.336 (flat) and 0.322 (convex), and the NPR 3.0 tones 0.461 (flat) and 0.229 (convex).
- **Powell counts (my estimate, not in the paper).**
  - With p = 0, h′ = h = 3.5d, c₀ = 340 m/s and an assumed u_con = 0.55U_j, eq. 2 gives N + p ≈ 2.2–2.3 for the convex 0.278 branch, 3.2–3.5 for the 0.40–0.43 branches, 4.5–4.9 for the 0.55–0.585 branches and 5.6–5.7 for the convex 0.63–0.65 tones at NPR 2.2 and 2.4.
  - With Powell's constants as used by [Henderson & Powell 1993](henderson-1993-tones-choked-jet-impinging-flat-plates.md) (p = 0, M_con,u = 0.73, loop length h + 0.8d), the same branches give 2.6, 3.7–3.9, 5.1–5.4 and 5.9–6.0.
  - Neither set falls cleanly on integers. At a fixed NPR the 0.40 and 0.56 branches are 1.3–1.4 apart in the first set, so with one p and one u_con they are not simply N and N + 1 of one loop. u_con is not measured, and a ±0.05U_j change moves N + p by about ±0.2.

## Notation
- d: nozzle exit diameter (15 mm); h: stand-off distance (nozzle lip to geometric impingement point); x, y, z: axial, transverse and cylinder-axis coordinates; r: radial coordinate for the flat plate.
- NPR = p₀/p_a; u_e: nozzle exit velocity (310 m/s); c₀, c_a: ambient sound speed; f: frequency; St (St_nozzle) = f d/u_e; λ: acoustic wavelength.
- N, p, h′, u_con: Powell's disturbance count, phase lag, source distance and convection speed.
- R_{x,y}: correlation coefficient; μ, σ: mean and standard deviation; (σ/μ)_roi: coefficient of variation in the region of interest.
- "On-axis" = xy view (line of sight along the cylinder axis); "off-axis" = xz view.
- My notation: M_j, D_j, U_j (isentropic fully expanded values), St_j = f D_j/U_j.

## Relevance to this project
This is the group paper behind a choice that runs through the later impinging-jet work: a convex half-cylinder of radius 37.5 mm locks the flapping plane, so planar PIV and single-view schlieren see the same instability every time. [Weightman et al. 2017](weightman-2017-phase-lag-supersonic-jet-impingement.md) (p. 3), the user's thesis ([Weightman 2019](weightman-2019-sound-production-impinging-supersonic-jets.md), p. 2–3, 29–30) and [Edgington-Mitchell et al. 2025](edgington-mitchell-2025-vortices-shocks-nonlinear-waves-impinging-jet-resonance.md) (p. 6–7) all cite it for that reason, and the thesis also cites it for the finding that a concave surface often gives no tone. It belongs to the CLAUDE.md core area: impinging supersonic jets, resonance and feedback, studied with schlieren and acoustics. See [impinging-jets-and-edge-tones](../Topics/impinging-jets-and-edge-tones.md) and [schlieren-and-density-imaging](../Topics/schlieren-and-density-imaging.md).

**How it fits and what has changed since**
- **Locking the flapping plane.** The evidence here is the two-view two-point correlation at one condition (NPR 3.4, h/d 3.5; p. 617). Weightman's PIV on the same kind of cylinder (D_c = 5D, i.e. the same 37.5 mm radius) at NPR 3.6, h/d 3.5 confirms it independently: one POD pair (23.3% + 19.0%) with a ring phase portrait and an antisymmetric transverse fluctuation, i.e. a stationary flapping mode (Weightman 2017, p. 7–8).
- **Frequency cross-check (my arithmetic).** That PIV case's 8300 Hz tone is St = 0.402 in this paper's convention. Fig. 6 gives 0.407 at NPR 3.6, about 8.41 kHz, 1.3% higher. That is close agreement for a nominally similar rig, cylinder and stand-off, though Weightman's cylinder is listed as 7D (105 mm) long against 115 mm here, and this paper does not state the lip.
- **Precession on a flat plate.** The flat-plate flapping plane wanders (p. 617), which is why a cylinder was needed for phase-averaged PIV. It bears on the mode labels in [Weightman et al. 2019](weightman-2019-nozzle-external-geometry-azimuthal-mode-impinging-jet.md), where a single dominant POD pair on a flat plate (NPR 3.4, z/D 5) was classed as helical because a precessing flapping mode would spread its energy over more modes (p. 431–432). The two papers are at different stand-offs, so they do not conflict directly. A pure helix looks antisymmetric from every view, so the single images with symmetric bands (Fig. 9) favour flapping (or an m = 0 admixture) here; but neither paper has the cross-plane or azimuthal-array data that would settle helical against precessing flapping.
- **Branches and mode type.** The flat and convex plates switch between St ≈ 0.28, 0.40–0.43 and 0.55–0.65 with NPR at fixed h, and the axisymmetric-to-flapping change between NPR 3.0 and 3.4 coincides with a branch jump (Fig. 6; my reading). This resembles the parallel L1–L3 lines with different azimuthal modes of [Henderson & Powell 1993](henderson-1993-tones-choked-jet-impinging-flat-plates.md) (p. 311–318, 322–323), and the alternating axisymmetric and helical St bands of the [Bogey & Gojon 2017](bogey-2017-feedback-loop-upwind-waves-ideally-expanded-impinging-jets.md) LES (p. 577–582). My Powell counts (Key equations) do not put the branches cleanly on integers, the same looseness the topic page records for Henderson & Powell and Ho & Nosseir.
- **The convex 0.278 branch: subharmonic or low-band loop? (my analysis).** Two readings fit the data. (i) It is half the flat-plate tone at the same NPR to within 1%, the NPR 3.0 correlation maps of both plates show the band spacing of St ≈ 0.56 rather than 0.278 (Key findings, Fig. 8), and a weak ≈ 0.265 component interacts quadratically with the flapping tone at NPR 3.4 (Fig. 10). That points to period doubling of the ≈ 0.56 loop. (ii) Converted to St_j it is ≈ 0.23, at the top edge of the (0, 1) guided-mode band (St 0–0.22 at M_j 1.40, cold vortex sheet, [Tam 1990](tam-1990-theoretical-model-discrete-tone-impinging-jets.md) note), so an axisymmetric loop closed by the (0, 1) mode is also possible. A bicoherence of the NPR 2.9–3.3 records, or a spectrum showing whether 0.556 is present at NPR 3.0, would decide it.
- **Guided-mode band test (my calculation, using the cold vortex-sheet bands in the Tam 1990 note).** At NPR 3.0 the flat plate's axisymmetric tone is St_j ≈ 0.46, inside the (0, 2) band (St 0.43–0.54 at M_j 1.40), so guided closure is possible. At NPR 3.4 the flapping tones are St_j ≈ 0.32–0.34, 9–13% below the (1, 1) band (0.369–0.371 at M_j 1.42). This repeats the thesis pattern (helical tones below (1, 1), toroidal near (0, 2)) and favours external closure for the flapping tone, though weakly, since shear-layer thickness lowers the bands by up to about 10% and the band values are for M_j 1.40–1.42, not 1.36 and 1.45.
- **Concave suppression: receptivity or source?** The receptivity explanation is plausible but untested.
  - The shear-layer thickness was never measured, and the σ/μ evidence comes from a different condition (h/d 4.5, NPR 4.0) in which both curved plates are silent. It shows that recirculated, turbulent wall-jet fluid lies in the line of sight beside the exit, not that the lip layer is thicker.
  - The paper's own images and text fit a source-side explanation equally well. On the concave plate the shock structure is "stable", with two clear cells and a steady plate shock, and fluctuations are confined to the shocks (Figs 4–5, p. 614); Sec. 3.1 itself notes that it is the collapse of the plate shock that forces the shock cells (p. 614). That resembles the silent case of [Henderson et al. 2002](henderson-2002-flow-structure-supersonic-impinging-jets.md) (the conference precursor of ref. 8), where a second, dome-shaped stand-off shock and a stagnation region persist without a tone (p. 4).
  - The later group work puts the upstream waves at the stand-off shock (leakage), in the wall-jet shocklet and at vortex–wall impact ([Weightman 2017](weightman-2017-phase-lag-supersonic-jet-impingement.md), p. 4–10; [Karami 2020](karami-2020-acoustic-hydrodynamic-waves-underexpanded-impinging-jets.md), p. 9–12; Edgington-Mitchell 2025, p. 14–24). A concave surface, which turns the wall jet up an adverse, centrifugally unstable wall, could weaken all three. So the rejection of the Henderson-type mechanism is incomplete: flat and convex plates at the same h are not a control for a concave surface that changes the stand-off structure itself.
  - The concave plate's acoustics (a cylindrical reflector whose centre of curvature lies 1d from the nozzle) were not examined beyond the "no amplified tone elsewhere" argument (my points).
- **Tone suppression by geometry.** The result belongs with the lip and foam results of [Weightman et al. 2019](weightman-2019-nozzle-external-geometry-azimuthal-mode-impinging-jet.md) (p. 428–441), the reflector and plate-size results of Henderson & Powell 1993 (p. 314–319) and the tone-free z/D < 3.2 range of the plug nozzle in [Powis et al. 2016](powis-2016-impingement-tones-shock-instabilities-plug-nozzle.md): small geometric changes near either end of the loop decide whether a tone exists. Of these, this is the only one that changes the impingement end.
- **Time-resolved predecessor.** [Mitchell et al. 2012](mitchell-2012-visualization-acoustic-feedback-loop-impinging-jet-schlieren.md) used the same rig and camera family (PCO Dimax, LED schlieren) for a flat plate at NPR 3.2, z/d 4; its Fig. 1 schematic is reused here.

**Methods and data for the lab workflow (CLAUDE.md)**
- **Non-time-resolved schlieren** is used throughout: mean, RMS, single-pixel two-point correlation and a region-of-interest σ/μ, each from 1000 independent images. These suit PIV-like ensembles and need no time resolution. Two caveats transfer: correlation of a precessing mode in one view washes out, and every statistic is of a path-integrated gradient. A POD-pair phase portrait of the same 1000 images, as the thesis did with PIV, would give the oscillation phase, which the single-pixel correlation does not. The band spacing of a correlation map is a cheap check on which tone the images carry (Fig. 8 reading above).
- **Two orthogonal views** (on-axis and off-axis) identified the flapping plane. This is the cheap two-view version of the multi-view approach on the schlieren topic page, and a cylinder makes the single-view case valid.
- **Time-resolved data** would answer what this paper could not.
  - SPOD of long-record TR schlieren at the tone, in both views, would give the mode shape and the k_x sign of the upstream waves.
  - On the flat plate, simultaneous orthogonal views or an azimuthal microphone ring with a Hilbert transform would give the instantaneous flapping-plane angle, i.e. the precession rate and whether it is steady or jumps.
  - For the concave plate, band-pass Hilbert envelopes of a long microphone record would show whether the weak 0.42 tone is a steady weak loop or intermittent bursts; SPOD → reconstruction → HPOD would show the same in the images.
  - The Fig. 10 sidebands (n × 0.401 + 0.265 and + 0.158) and the 0.278 = 0.56/2 coincidence are BMD and bicoherence targets: they test whether the low-St component is a subharmonic or an independent loop coexisting with the flapping loop.

**Modelling side and the PyFR staging LES**
- The paper has no model. Testable model questions: a global or resolvent analysis of the impinging mean flow with and without the recirculated wall jet (or with a thickened inflow layer) would test the receptivity claim; a guided-mode band test at the right M_j and temperature would say which tones need in-core closure; and an LES of the concave case would show whether the stand-off shock or the shear layer stops oscillating first.
- PyFR staging runs (`Simulations/PyFR_Staging_Continuation/`) are free jets, so there is no direct link. The general lesson that the inflow shear-layer state and the surroundings near the lip decide whether a loop survives applies there, as it does to Weightman et al. 2019.

**Rocket noise.** A concave surface that turns the exhaust back towards the nozzle is a laboratory analogue of flame buckets and trenches. Here it removes tones by the authors' recirculation mechanism, or by a steadier stand-off structure (my reading). This adds a candidate to the impinging-jets topic page's open question of why full-scale pads show no tones ([Lubert 2022](lubert-2022-supersonic-jet-noise-launch-vehicles-review.md), p. 16–20), though full-scale plumes are hot, overexpanded and usually meet inclined deflectors.
