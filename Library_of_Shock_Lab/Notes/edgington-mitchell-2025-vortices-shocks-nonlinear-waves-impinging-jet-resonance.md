# Vortices, shocks and non-linear acoustic waves: the ingredients for resonance in impinging compressible jets

- **Authors:** D. M. Edgington-Mitchell, J. L. Weightman, S. Mazharmanesh, P. Nogueira (all Shock Lab, Monash University Dept of Mechanical and Aerospace Engineering; p. 1). The second author is the user.

## Summary
The paper asks why some studies close the impinging-jet feedback loop with the guided jet mode (and predict tones well with it) while others see a nonlinear, shock-like freestream wave born in the wall jet, outside the jet core (p. 3–5). Using high-speed schlieren and shadowgraph of four nozzles (round and AR 2 rectangular, converging and converging–diverging) impinging on flat, cylindrical and hemispherical surfaces, it catalogues the upstream waves: nonlinear duct-like waves inside subsonic jets, single and double nonlinear freestream waves, merging pairs of shock-like waves, and diffuse waves that coalesce into one sharp front (p. 7–14, 23). It then assigns generation mechanisms, all driven by the same KH vortex: shock leakage at the stand-off shock, direct vortex sound at the wall (in-core source, so guided-mode band limits apply), wall-jet boundary displacement (hypothesised for the diffuse waves) and the transient wall-jet shocklet (p. 14–24). Because the guided jet mode only governs sound generated inside the jet core (Nogueira et al. 2024), in-core vortex sound explains why guided-mode models work for subsonic and some supersonic jets, while sources outside the core (shocklet, leakage) explain the tones those models miss (p. 3–4, 15–17). The conclusion is that the two schools of thought were describing different mechanisms, which can coexist even within a single resonance cycle; no clean map of mechanism against NPR or z/D could be found (p. 12–14, 21, 25).

## Key findings
- **Four (then five) families of upstream waves** (p. 7, Fig. 2; p. 23):
  - (a) nonlinear duct-like waves (subsonic jet), (b) nonlinear freestream waves, (c) diffuse freestream waves that steepen or coalesce, (d) merging nonlinear freestream waves (p. 7).
  - §4.4 adds double nonlinear freestream waves that stay separate, giving five families: duct-like, single nonlinear freestream, double nonlinear freestream, merging nonlinear, coalescing diffuse (p. 23).
- **Duct-like waves are nonlinear** (p. 8, Fig. 3):
  - High-subsonic jets, z/D = 2, M = 0.86–0.90, Re 3.0–3.7 × 10⁵.
  - The upstream waves are the Tam & Hu (1989) / [Tam & Ahuja (1990)](tam-1990-theoretical-model-discrete-tone-impinging-jets.md) duct-like modes (first seen experimentally by Li et al. 2024), but here strong enough to look like a train of upstream-moving shocks. In still frames m = 0 cases resemble stationary shock cells of an underexpanded jet; m = 1 cases trace a helical pattern through the core with no steady-flow analogue.
  - Guided-mode resonance models work well for subsonic jets, for some ideally expanded (Varé & Bogey 2023) and for some underexpanded (Ferreira et al. 2023) supersonic jets (p. 8).
  - In the Bogey & Gojon (2017) / Gojon & Bogey (2017) LES pressure fields, some underexpanded cases have pressure inside and outside the jet almost uncorrelated, so their upstream wave cannot be the guided mode; others show a waveform continuous across the shear layer, as the guided mode requires (p. 8).
- **Freestream waves are shocks** (p. 9–10, Fig. 4–5):
  - Rectangular converging nozzle, NPR 3.0, z/D_e = 3, cylinder: a nonlinear front outside the jet with an inverse-sawtooth character (p. 9).
  - Re-analysis of instantaneous PIV from Weightman et al. (2016) (round converging nozzle, cylinder, z/D = 3.5): Δu ≈ 40 m/s across the wave, so by Rankine–Hugoniot M_s ≈ 1.07, an effective SPL ≈ 175 dB. The velocity samples are not exactly normal to the wave, so M_s may be slightly higher. The velocity profiles look smeared rather than stepped because of particle lag (Melling 1997) (p. 10).
- **Sharp and diffuse waves alternate** (p. 10–12, Fig. 6–7):
  - Round C-D nozzle near design, NPR 4.0, z/D = 4.5, cylinder, Re 6.5 × 10⁵.
  - Some waves are sharp from birth (near x/D ≈ 3.5) and keep their peak amplitude and half-width as they travel upstream (p. 10).
  - In the same image sequence, other cycles start as a diffuse group of waves near x/D ≈ 3.5–4 that coalesce into one sharp front by x/D ≈ 2, with the half-width shrinking and peak rising (p. 10–11).
  - Whether this is classical steepening, coalescence of distributed sources, or both cannot be told from the data; propagation distances (< 10D) and small nozzles make propagation-driven steepening insignificant otherwise (p. 3, 11).
  - Switching between sharp and diffuse forms can happen over **one** resonance period, and no link to the amplitude or shape of the KH wavepacket could be found (p. 11).
- **Merging of shock-like waves** (p. 12, Fig. 8): at NPR 4.8, z/D = 4.5, Re 8.6 × 10⁵, two shock-like waves from different sources merge into one; consistent with KZK-predicted merging at grazing incidence and Mach-stem formation (Willis et al. 2023; Marchiano et al. 2007; Karzova et al. 2015).
- **No parametric map** (p. 12–13, Fig. 9): at a single condition (NPR 2.5, z/D = 3.0, Re 5.9 × 10⁵) the jet produces single waves, double waves that coalesce and double waves that don't, within a short time. Shock-like waves appear at both the low and high ends of the NPR range and over a range of z/D.
- **Vortex sound → guided jet mode** (p. 14–15; literature synthesis, no new data):
  - A vortex ring hitting a wall radiates quadrupole sound (Howe 2003; Nakashima & Inoue 2008); a compressible ring with trailing jet gives a pressure maximum on its centreline at the wall (Minota et al. 1997, 1998). An m = 0 wavepacket likewise peaks on the axis. So the effective source is in the jet core near the wall (p. 14–15).
  - Sound generated in the core can only travel upstream as the duct-like or guided jet mode (reflection/transmission at the shear layer; Nogueira et al. 2024), so this mechanism is subject to the guided-mode frequency band. This explains the robust guided-mode predictions and the good Curle's-analogy match of Varé & Bogey (2022a, 2023) (p. 15).
  - Colliding compressible vortex rings, even subsonic ones, can produce weak shocks (Minota et al. 1998; Bauer et al. 2022), so vortex–wall impact is also a candidate for some shock-like waves. The authors assume the linear guided-mode band still applies qualitatively to these weak shocks (p. 15).
- **Wall-jet shocklet** (p. 15–17, Fig. 10):
  - New shadowgraph (rectangular jet on a cylinder, NPR 5.5, z/D = 3.5, Re 7.8 × 10⁵) confirms the Weightman et al. (2017b) mechanism: the KH vortex entering the wall jet forms a transient shock; as the vortex moves on and removes its induced velocity, the shocklet tilts retrograde and moves upstream, launching a nonlinear compression wave (p. 15–17).
  - Location and motion are strongly case dependent; the shocklet moves radially outward with Mach number. It is not visible for NPR ≤ 3.0 in most datasets, possibly only because the turbulent wall jet hides it (p. 17).
  - It appears to be consistently responsible for the closing wave when **NPR ≥ 4.5 and z/D ≤ 4.0** (p. 17). Proposed existence condition: the wall jet fluctuates between supersonic and subsonic as vortices pass. Hypothesis: no role in subsonic and low-supersonic jets, increasing role with Mach number; curved surfaces (faster wall jet) may lower the threshold, though the shocklet is also seen on a flat plate (p. 17).
  - The source is well outside the jet core, so the guided-mode frequency band is irrelevant; the wave is a shock at birth and decays by diffraction (p. 17).
- **Shock leakage at the stand-off shock** (p. 17–19, Fig. 11):
  - Unpublished ray-tracing (Shariff & Manning 2013 model) on measured velocity fields supports leakage at the stand-off shock; Varé & Bogey (2022b) saw it in rocket-jet impingement LES. The Shariff & Manning model predicts significant leakage for the Weightman et al. (2017b, 2019) PIV (p. 17–18).
  - Caveat: an upstream-moving shock generated in the core by vortex–wall impact would also displace the stand-off shock and could look like leakage in schlieren. The wall jet hides where the vortex hits the wall, so the two cannot always be separated; they may even be synchronised. Cases with the stand-off shock far enough from the wall show leakage before the vortex reaches the wall (p. 18).
  - Rectangular jet on a cylinder, NPR 3.4, z/D = 3.0, Re 6.0 × 10⁵, frames 20 μs apart: leakage begins just after the large KH vortex passes, as in screech (p. 18).
  - **New form of leakage** not seen in screeching jets: the leaked front is split, because the much stronger impinging-jet vortices rotate part of it back towards the jet core. The inner segment slows in the shear layer and is transmitted into the jet; only the outer segment carries on upstream (p. 18). Leakage then starts on the opposite side for the next half-cycle (p. 18).
- **Dual mechanisms from one or two vortices** (p. 19–23, Fig. 12–15):
  - All mechanisms are driven by the same vortical structure: vortex sound by its wall interaction, leakage by its passage through the stand-off shock, the shocklet by its passage into the wall jet. The vortex-sound contribution depends on how far from the wall the mean flow turns; leakage depends on shear-layer vorticity strength and stand-off-shock shape (annular or not); shocklet conditions remain unclear but involve vortex strength and wall-jet speed (p. 19–20).
  - NPR 5.5, z/D = 3.5: in one cycle a shocklet wave (from the **previous** vortex) is followed by a second shock-like wave from leakage or vortex–wall interaction; the two travel separately with different directions (p. 20, Fig. 12).
  - NPR 6, z/D = 3.5, Re 8.5 × 10⁵: two waves born at y/D ≈ −2 (shocklet) and y/D ≈ −1 (another shocklet or vortex sound) merge, explaining Fig. 8 (p. 21, Fig. 13).
  - NPR 3.8, z/D = 3.0, Re 6.7 × 10⁵: at one condition pairs sometimes merge and sometimes don't; turbulent jitter in KH phase and amplitude spreads the merging condition over a range of frequencies (p. 21, Fig. 14). A third wave with a different origin and direction is also seen (p. 22).
  - Round jet on a hemisphere, NPR 2.5, z/D = 3.0: part of a leaked front on the −y side is rotated almost 90°, crosses the jet **below** the stand-off shock in the subsonic region, and emerges on the +y side just behind the next leakage wave. The pair then propagates upstream: the transverse wave radiates mostly radially, the leakage wave mostly straight back to the nozzle (p. 22–23, Fig. 15). The part crossing above the stand-off shock merges into a nascent oblique shock where the retrograde flow is supersonic (p. 22).
- **Henderson's wall-jet membrane source** (p. 23–24): not linked directly to any observed wave. The authors hypothesise it produces the diffuse waves: Henderson et al. (2005) suggests the boundary moves subsonically (so no shock) and the source is spread over a non-compact region, so it would launch weaker distributed waves that later coalesce.
- **Not every wave closes the loop** (p. 24–25): when two strong waves come from one vortex, usually only one interacts with the shear layer at the nozzle; the other misses the nozzle or arrives weak. A few cases show complicated amplitude modulation that might mean a more complex feedback. So some strong tonal components measured at an observer may be by-products of resonance, not part of it (p. 25).
- **Trends, cautiously** (p. 25): the shocklet strengthens at higher Mach number and shorter z/D, and the effective source moves radially outward with Mach number. No clear relation between mechanism and Mach number or azimuthal mode (or rectangular equivalent) was found; higher Mach number can also switch resonance off entirely (Henderson 2002).

## Setup / method
- **Facility:** Gas-Jet Facility at Monash University, unheated dry air through a flow-conditioned plenum; details deferred to earlier papers (p. 6). The authors are all from the Shock Lab, but the paper doesn't name the lab space where the rig sits, so this is a **Shock Lab group facility**.
- **Nozzles** (Table 1, p. 6): see Data tables. Two round and two AR 2 rectangular nozzles, each with a converging and a converging–diverging variant. The round nozzles are those of Weightman et al. (2017a, 2019); the sharp-throated rectangular C-D nozzle is the one used for single and twin rectangular jet control at OSU (Webb et al. 2023; Samimy et al. 2023, 2024) (p. 5).
  - All four have relatively thick lips; resonance is very sensitive to lip thickness (Weightman et al. 2019). Rectangular lips are non-uniform: the converging one has a circular outer lip, the sharp-throated one a rectangular lip with thickness proportional to each axis (p. 6).
  - Re = U_j D_J/ν differs by about 2× between the smallest and largest nozzles at the same NPR; the lowest is still O(10⁵), and Re effects are assumed negligible (McLaughlin et al. 1977; Ozawa et al. 2020) (p. 5–6).
- **Impingement surfaces** (p. 6–7): flat plate (large parametric sweeps; only selected results shown); a cylinder of radius 37.5 mm, used for most visualisations because it locks the flapping axis of a round jet (Mason-Smith et al. 2015); a hemisphere of radius 37.5 mm, which minimises path integration through the wall jet and so makes the shocklet easiest to see. Results on curved surfaces differ quantitatively from a flat plate, but all phenomena shown are also seen on flat plates, less clearly (p. 6–7).
- **Imaging** (p. 7):
  - Z-type Toepler schlieren; shadowgraph by removing the knife edge and slightly defocusing. Intensity ∝ ∂ρ/∂x (schlieren) or ∇²ρ (shadowgraph).
  - Pulsed LED illumination (Willert, Mitchell & Soria 2012), 500 ns pulses.
  - Photron Fastcam SA-Z 2100k, 50–200 kfps, resolution varying. These are **time-resolved** data.
  - Fig. 4 combines ∂ρ/∂x and ∂ρ/∂y schlieren taken separately and manually phase-matched, because each knife-edge orientation misses parts of the front (p. 9). Fig. 11 likewise combines non-simultaneous schlieren and shadowgraph, manually phase-matched (p. 18).
  - Fig. 2 composites join two images O(10⁻⁵ s) apart along a red dashed line (p. 7).
- **Processing** (all simple, no modal decomposition):
  - Streamwise intensity profiles across a wave, averaged over 40 px vertically, tracked in a Lagrangian frame to follow peak amplitude and half-width (Fig. 6d, 7d; p. 10).
  - "A posteriori correlation-based phase average" of shadowgraph (Fig. 11, 12). The correlation template, number of images and phase bins aren't stated (p. 18, 20).
  - Fig. 15 uses a post hoc phase average over a region of interest that moves with the wave: explicitly "not a true phase average", built to emphasise features that are clear only in animation (p. 22).
  - PIV (Fig. 5) is re-used from Weightman et al. (2016): instantaneous u′/U_e with profiles averaged over 20 vectors (p. 9–10). The NPR of that case is left blank in the text ("NPR = (p₀/p∞)"); the Weightman thesis cylinder case suggests NPR 3.6 (p. 9).
  - Shock strength from Rankine–Hugoniot on the PIV velocity jump (p. 10).
- **Modelling side:** no new simulation or stability analysis. The modelling content is a synthesis:
  - Guided jet mode as reflection/transmission of in-core sound at the shear layer, with a finite frequency band (Nogueira et al. 2024; linear, infinitesimal amplitude) (p. 2–3, 15).
  - Vortex-sound theory (Howe 2003) and Curle's analogy with surface pressure only, as used by Varé & Bogey (2022a) on LES (p. 4, 14–15).
  - Ray-tracing shock-leakage model of Shariff & Manning (2013) (vorticity strength controls leakage; Suzuki & Lele 2003), applied to PIV in unpublished work (p. 17–18).
  - KZK equation for merging at grazing incidence (Willis et al. 2023) (p. 12).
  - LES evidence cited: Gojon & Bogey (2017), Bogey & Gojon (2017), Gojon et al. (2016), Varé & Bogey (2022a, b, 2023), Ferreira et al. (2023) survey (p. 3–4, 8, 17); receptivity simulations of Karami et al. (2020a, b): near-impulsive lip forcing gives a broadband high-frequency response that becomes one large vortex within the first diameter (p. 2).

## Key equations
No numbered equations. The quantitative step is the normal-shock estimate (p. 10):

$$\Delta u = \frac{2a_\infty}{\gamma+1}\left(M_s - \frac{1}{M_s}\right), \qquad \frac{\Delta p}{p_\infty} = \frac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}\left(M_s^2-1\right)$$

> My reconstruction of the relations behind "Δu ≈ 40 m/s → M_s ≈ 1.07 → SPL ≈ 175 dB" (the paper gives only the numbers). Check: with a∞ = 343 m/s, γ = 1.4, M_s = 1.07 gives Δu ≈ 38.7 m/s and Δp ≈ 17 kPa; 20 log₁₀(Δp/√2 / 20 μPa) ≈ 175.6 dB, so "effective SPL" appears to treat the jump as a sinusoid peak. How the paper defined effective SPL isn't stated.

Powell's resonance model (four legs: downstream KH propagation, reflection at the plate, upstream propagation, receptivity at the nozzle) is invoked in words, with the shocklet formation time explaining the phase lag p (p. 2, 5).

## Notation
- NPR = p₀/p∞; NPR_D design NPR; z/D (or z/D_e) nozzle-to-surface distance; D_e equivalent exit diameter; t lip thickness.
- M_s shock Mach number; Δu velocity jump across the wave; U_e exit velocity.
- KH: Kelvin–Helmholtz. Guided jet mode: discrete upstream acoustic mode (Tam & Hu 1989), here understood as reflection/transmission of in-core sound at the shear layer (Nogueira et al. 2024); "duct-like" when totally reflected, radially decaying outside otherwise (p. 15).
- Stand-off shock: the near-plate normal shock (continuous) or annular plate shock. Wall-jet shocklet: transient shock embedded in the wall jet.
- m: azimuthal mode number (0 toroidal, 1 helical).

## Relevance to this project
This is the user's own group paper (second author) and the direct sequel to his thesis: [weightman-2019](weightman-2019-sound-production-impinging-supersonic-jets.md). It reframes the resonance and feedback work in CLAUDE.md for impinging jets: the question is not "guided mode or freestream wave?" but "where is the upstream wave generated?".

- **Continuity with the thesis:**
  - The shocklet (thesis Ch. 7, JFM 815 R1) is confirmed over a much wider range of nozzles, surfaces and NPR, with a stated regime (NPR ≥ 4.5, z/D ≤ 4.0) and an existence hypothesis (transonic wall jet) (p. 17). The thesis cases at NPR 3.4–3.6 sit below that threshold, where the shocklet is often hidden by the wall jet; worth re-checking which mechanism dominates those datasets.
  - Shock leakage at the stand-off shock (thesis Ch. 6, ray tracing on POD-reconstructed PIV) is now called out explicitly, with the new split-front form (p. 18). The Shariff & Manning ray-tracing calculations on measured fields are said to be unpublished (p. 17); the thesis ray-tracing may be that work.
  - The Weightman et al. (2016) cylinder PIV is re-used to show the freestream wave is a weak shock, M_s ≈ 1.07 (p. 10). The NPR is missing in the text; the thesis note lists the cylinder case at z/D 3.5.
  - Lip thickness matters: all nozzles here have thick lips (t/D_e 0.3–0.6) (p. 6), consistent with the thesis finding that external geometry selects helical vs toroidal modes. This paper finds no clean link between mechanism and azimuthal mode (p. 25).
- **Henderson:** [henderson-2002](henderson-2002-flow-structure-supersonic-impinging-jets.md) is the precursor of Henderson et al. (2005), whose wall-jet "membrane" source near r ≈ 1.3D is mechanism (iii) here. This paper does not confirm it, only hypothesises it as the origin of the diffuse coalescing waves (p. 23–24). Henderson's observation that tones vanish in zones of silence, and that higher Mach can switch resonance off, is cited as a caution against reading the shocklet trend too far (p. 25). Henderson's oscillating Mach disk and collapsing annular shock relate to the stand-off-shock leakage and the annular-shock inset of Fig. 1.
- **Guided-mode literature:** this paper and [edgington-mitchell-2018](edgington-mitchell-2018-upstream-acoustic-modes-screech-closure.md) / [edgington-mitchell-2020](edgington-mitchell-2020-waves-in-screeching-jets.md) are consistent: guided-mode closure holds when the source is in the core (screech, subsonic impingement). [edgington-mitchell-2022](edgington-mitchell-2022-unifying-theory-jet-screech.md) also treats shock leakage as a nonlinear radiation mechanism in screech; here leakage is part of the loop itself. [jordan-2018](jordan-2018-jet-flap-interaction-tones.md) and [stavropoulos-2026](stavropoulos-2026-jet-edge-interaction.md) are the subsonic jet–edge analogues where guided-mode closure works. [mancinelli-2019](mancinelli-2019-screech-prediction-upstream-modes.md)'s guided-mode frequency prediction would be expected to fail for the shocklet/leakage cases. [Nogueira et al. (2024)](nogueira-2024-guided-jet-waves.md), *Guided-jet waves* (JFM 999 A47), underpins the argument. [nogueira-2021-absolute-instability-shock-containing-jets](nogueira-2021-absolute-instability-shock-containing-jets.md) gives the stability-theory side of shock-containing jets.
- **Data and method implications for the user's workflow:**
  - Everything here is **time-resolved** schlieren/shadowgraph (50–200 kfps) analysed frame by frame; no SPOD, BMD or POD. The key messages for modal analysis:
    - The upstream wave can switch between sharp and diffuse **within one cycle** (p. 11), pairs can merge or not at one condition (p. 13, 21), and a strong wave may not take part in the loop (p. 24–25). Block-averaged SPOD at the tone frequency will blend these states into one mode, and the nonlinear fronts will spread energy into harmonics. BMD is a natural tool for the harmonic and triadic content of these shock-like waves.
    - The SPOD → reconstruction → Hilbert POD pipeline could quantify cycle-to-cycle changes in the upstream wave's amplitude and phase, and test whether sharp vs diffuse waves or merging vs separate pairs correlate with KH amplitude (the paper found none by eye, p. 11). Isolating upstream waves first by the sign of k_x (as in [edgington-mitchell-2018](edgington-mitchell-2018-upstream-acoustic-modes-screech-closure.md)) or by region (outside vs inside the jet) would separate freestream from guided waves. See [raiola-2025](raiola-2025-hilbert-pod.md), [yeung-2024](yeung-2024-adaptive-spod-broadband-tonal.md), [von-saldern-2026](von-saldern-2026-band-ensemble-spod.md).
    - Diagnostic for guided vs freestream closure: coherence or phase continuity of the tone across the shear layer (p. 8). Uncorrelated inside/outside pressure means the guided mode isn't the closing wave. With schlieren this can be checked with SPOD mode phase across the shear layer.
    - Correlation-based a posteriori phase averaging (Fig. 11–12) and moving-ROI conditional averaging (Fig. 15) are useful for cycle-resolved views of time-resolved images; record the template and bin choices if reusing them, since the paper doesn't give them.
  - **Non-time-resolved PIV:** single snapshots can still measure shock strength via Rankine–Hugoniot, but particle lag smears the jump (p. 10). POD-pair phase reconstruction (thesis) would average out the alternation between sharp and diffuse waves.
  - **Schlieren practicalities:** one knife-edge orientation misses parts of curved fronts (combine ∂/∂x and ∂/∂y, p. 9); path integration through the wall jet hides shocklets on flat plates (use cylinders to lock the flapping axis, hemispheres to see the wall jet) (p. 6).
- **Modelling side:** the paper offers mechanisms, not predictions. Useful next steps it implies: (a) apply the Shariff & Manning ray tracing to stand-off shocks and publish it; (b) combine Curle's analogy on LES (Varé & Bogey) with explicit tests of which source is in-core; (c) a resonance model that switches between guided-mode band limits (in-core source) and freestream propagation from an off-axis source with a shocklet-formation phase lag. The guided-mode model is linear; whether weak in-core shocks obey its band limits is assumed, not shown (p. 15).
- **Rocket noise link:** impinging-jet waves here are shocks at birth (M_s ≈ 1.07, ~175 dB effective) with coalescence and Mach-stem merging, which ties to nonlinear propagation and crackle in launch noise ([gee-2023](gee-2023-sls-artemis-i-far-field-noise.md), [gee-2012](gee-2012-reusable-srm-noise-spectral-statistical.md)) and to plume–pad impingement in [lubert-2022](lubert-2022-supersonic-jet-noise-launch-vehicles-review.md). Varé & Bogey (2022b) saw stand-off-shock leakage in impinging rocket-jet LES (p. 17). The point that strong tonal waves measured near the nozzle may be by-products rather than part of the loop (p. 25) matters when interpreting near-pad microphones.
