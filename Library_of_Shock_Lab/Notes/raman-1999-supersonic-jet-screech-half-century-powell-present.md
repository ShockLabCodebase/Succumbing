## Summary
Raman retells fifty years of screech research, from Powell's 1951 schlieren photographs to 1998, around the four links of Powell's loop: instability growth, instability–shock interaction, acoustic feedback and receptivity at the lip (Fig. 1, p. 544). He stresses that Powell's theory holds two separate conditions, a phase criterion and an amplitude criterion (upstream-peaked directivity), and that both were meant to hold at once (p. 545–546). The review then covers early and Soviet work and the staging history (p. 547–548), shock modulation of the instability wave (p. 549–550), shock-cell models and the Tam frequency formula with its rectangular waveguide form (p. 550–552), the weak link between shock strength and amplitude, and unsteady shock motion (p. 552–554). It continues with the near-field standing wave, single-source feedback and the source shift from the 3rd to the 4th shock, lip thickness and reflectors (p. 554–557), twin-jet coupling, bevelled nozzles, mixing enhancement and induced screech (p. 557–563), and simulations up to 1998 (p. 563). The thesis is the same as in Raman 1998: frequency can be predicted once the shock spacing and convection speed are known, but amplitude cannot, and the key lies in feedback and receptivity (p. 564). No new data are presented; the figures are taken from Raman's NASA Lewis rectangular-jet work and from Suda et al. and Raman & Taghavi.

## Key findings
- **Powell's discovery and model** (p. 543–546).
  - Powell photographed a screeching jet in 1951 at Southampton with a home-made schlieren system built from chemistry stands. His 2-D nozzle (0.7 × 0.118 in, an aspect ratio of about 5.9 by my arithmetic) screeched above the audible range, so the discovery rested on schlieren alone (p. 543). He saw an asymmetric disturbance pattern and sound running upstream (p. 543–544).
  - Powell's own words (p. 544): the "alternately disposed" eddy system makes sound as it crosses the regular shock train, the interference making the emission strongest upstream, and on reaching the orifice the sound creates "embryo disturbances".
  - **Two criteria** (p. 545–546), which Raman says "many researchers have missed":
    1. *Phase criterion:* the downstream convection time plus the upstream acoustic return time is an integer number of periods. A refined version adds the delay between shock–vortex interaction and emission, and the delay in triggering a new disturbance at the lip; both could approach zero only in "very special circumstances".
    2. *Amplitude criterion:* the directivity must peak upstream (α = 180° from the downstream axis), so that waves from the downstream sources reinforce at the lip.
    Powell required both together (citing his 1953 edge-tone paper).
  - Sources are a phased array of regularly spaced monopoles; three or four equal stationary sources give directivity formulas for the fundamental (peak at 180°) and harmonic (narrow lobe near 90°, slightly below in practice) (eqs. 1–2, p. 546).
  - Powell's frequency formula (eq. 3) follows from eq. 1 by asking for maximum radiation upstream. It needs an accurate convection speed; non-uniform sound speed along the return path matters less; and the shock spacing to use is unclear when spacing varies (p. 546).
  - Powell also wrote the loop as a limit cycle: growth × shock interaction × feedback efficiency × "stream disturbance creation efficiency" (now called receptivity) = 1 (eq. 4, p. 546).
- **Early research and staging** (Fig. 2, p. 547–548).
  - Powell identified four discrete stages, A–D, in a round jet. Merle (stroboscopic light) split A into A1 and A2 and graded them: A1 unstable, A2 stable, B very unstable, C very stable, D unstable and "not always visible" (p. 547).
  - Davies & Oldfield were the first to use two microphones on opposite sides of the jet: A1 and A2 axisymmetric, B sinuous, C helical; D "resisted classification" (p. 547).
  - Powell had also noted that flapping modes precessed one way or the other "rather unpredictably" (p. 547).
  - Poldervaart and co-workers (Eindhoven) filmed the feedback loop and the effect of reflectors and external pulses. The NRC Canada group (Westley, Woolley, Chan, Lee) made phase-resolved near-field maps and films of axisymmetric and spinning modes. Chan proposed dipole rather than monopole sources (p. 547–548).
- **Soviet work** (p. 548), which Raman says found several effects "many years before" the West:
  - Sedel'nikov gave a theory of screech and computed the growth of instability ("dispersion") waves in supersonic jets.
  - Anufriev et al. measured the frequency–Mach number curve of a round jet and found that preheating lowers screech intensity and raises its frequency.
  - Glaznev found the tone perturbations three-dimensional, with amplitude changing abruptly at the shocks (shock modulation, in today's terms).
  - Bikart measured screech amplitude and frequency for design Mach numbers 1, 1.4, 2, 2.5 and 3, over- and underexpanded; his frequency curve agreed with Sedel'nikov's theory.
- **Staging is unexplained** (p. 548). NASA Langley mapped stages A–D in round jets; Norum compared stage wavelengths across researchers and Norum & Shearin their amplitudes. There are distinct frequency jumps and mode-dependent peak amplitudes. Powell, Umeda & Ishii 1992 ([Powell 1992](powell-1992-oscillation-modes-choked-circular-jets.md)) revisited the stages and gave the C/B frequency ratio against NPR. "No one to date has offered clear explanation for the mode jumps."
- **Rectangular-jet modes** (p. 548–549). Most rectangular jets oscillate antisymmetrically, with sound out of phase on the two sides (Powell; Poldervaart et al.; [Krothapalli 1986](krothapalli-1986-screech-tones-mixing-underexpanded-rectangular-jet.md); Shih et al.; Gutmark et al.). Gutmark et al. found a symmetric mode just above choking that switched to antisymmetric at M_j = 1.15. Kaji & Nishijima, Suda et al. and Nishijima & Kaji showed that rectangular jets can sustain both; Lin & Powell saw symmetric modes in choked-jet edge tones and rectangular screech.
- **Shocks modulate the instability wave** (Fig. 3, p. 549–550).
  - Westley & Woolley's films show disturbances accelerating between shocks and decelerating as they approach one, not convecting at constant speed (p. 549).
  - Two causes are offered: the mean flow speeds up and slows down through the alternating convergent–divergent "channel" of the shock train, and the shocks modulate the wave directly (p. 549–550).
  - Raman & Rice's hot-film traverse along the M = 0.4 line of a rectangular jet at M_j = 1.44 shows the screech mode growing nonlinearly, with dips just before the marked shocks and recovery after them; no stretch of sustained exponential growth matches linear theory (p. 550).
- **Shock-cell models** (Fig. 4a, p. 550–551).
  - Prandtl's linear inviscid cell length (via Pack 1950) is shorter than the diamond pattern of choked jets (Powell 1995) (p. 550).
  - Tam, Jackson & Seiner's multiple-scales model for a round jet matches Norum & Seiner ([Norum 1982](norum-1982-mean-static-pressure-far-field-acoustics-shock-containing-jets.md)) (p. 550).
  - For rectangular jets, [Tam 1988](tam-1988-shock-cell-screech-rectangular-non-axisymmetric-jets.md) solves the vortex-sheet problem by eigenfunction expansion, and Morris, Bhat & Chen 1989 use a boundary-element method for any cross-section. Both give a good first estimate of the average spacing, which Raman finds surprising since a linearly perturbed jet does not have a choked jet's structure (p. 550).
  - These models handle weak shocks only. Beyond Mach-disk formation, spacing, strength and structure vary in ways they miss, and Raman calls for a "second-generation model" (p. 551).
- **Screech frequency** (Fig. 4b, p. 552).
  - The tone sits at the centre of the narrow band that instability–shock interaction radiates towards the lip ([Tam 1986](tam-1986-bbsan-screech-relationship.md)), eq. 5; with k = 2π/s it reduces to Powell's eq. 3.
  - The formula cannot predict the round-jet A–D jumps. For rectangular jets the lowest waveguide mode (eq. 6) gives "very accurate" frequencies. In Shih et al.'s comparison, Tam's theory fits better with U_c = 0.55 than with 0.7 (in U_j units).
  - Heating raises U_c and hence the frequency; Tam et al. 1986 matched hot-jet data (Rosfjord & Toms; Krothapalli et al. 1997). Flight effects: Bryce & Pinker, Tam (p. 552).
- **Shock strength does not set amplitude** (p. 552–553). Shocks are needed, and some strength is needed for strong screech, but more strength adds nothing; at high M_j screech stops although the shocks are very strong (Raman 1997, JFM 336). Shock strength only starts screech or destabilises a particular mode in jets with complex shock structure.
- **Unsteady shocks** (Fig. 5, p. 553–554).
  - Westley & Woolley first saw "double" or "infant" shocks and recorded shock motion and the tilt of shock bases over a cycle (p. 553).
  - Suda et al. (laser schlieren, 2-D jet): a travelling shock in the 3rd cell rotates about one end and sweeps downstream, merges with the end of the cell after half a cycle, and is replaced by a new one rotating about the other end. They proposed it as "the actual source of screech" (p. 553–554).
  - Panda's phase-averaged laser shock detection: the 3rd shock moves downstream while a weaker shock appears upstream, strengthens, and replaces it (p. 554).
- **Near field and standing waves** (p. 554–555).
  - Feedback and receptivity are singled out because broadband shock noise, made by similar processes without feedback, can be predicted while screech amplitude cannot (p. 554).
  - Westley & Woolley: SPL falls from 160 dB at the jet boundary to about 130 dB at 6D; a standing wave appears, perhaps from downstream hydrodynamic and upstream acoustic waves, perhaps from an acoustic–acoustic standing wave with a flange reflection; lobes between shocks peak at shocks 3 and 4; spinning-mode screech gives a spiralling near field (p. 554). Lepicovsky & Ahuja found a near-field standing wave in subsonic edge tones too.
  - Panda (AIAA 96-1718; same title as [Panda 1999](panda-1999-experimental-investigation-screech-noise-generation.md), whose JFM version was received in April 1996, so presumably its conference form; round jet, M_j 1.19): phase-averaged maps show downstream hydrodynamic and upstream and downstream acoustic waves, with upstream waves moving "pause-and-go" through the standing wave (p. 554). The standing wavelength satisfies 1/L_sw = 1/λ_s + 1/λ_h, which turns Powell's formula into one with L_sw in place of the shock spacing; Panda, Raman & Zaman 1997 found it in rectangular and elliptic jets as well (p. 555).
  - Raman, Panda & Zaman 1997 made phase-averaged near-field maps of an AR 5, M_j 1.8 rectangular jet with an upstream reflector placed for maximum screech (p. 554).
- **Single-source feedback** (Fig. 6, p. 555–556).
  - The transverse phase at the exit plane (x/h = 0, z/h = 0) has a null region where it does not change with y, larger at M_j 1.75 than at 1.45 (p. 555).
  - A model with one "equivalent" monopole at y = h/2 (the shock tip) fits best with the source at the 3rd shock for M_j 1.45 and at the 4th for M_j 1.75 (p. 555; Fig. 6c, p. 556).
  - The shift is explained by lower amplification of the screech-frequency instability wave at higher M_j, so the wave peaks further downstream (p. 555). No stability calculation is shown here; Raman 1998 credits it to Cain & Bower.
  - Raman's question (p. 555): if one source dominates without reinforcement by its neighbours, how can screech be so strongly directed upstream? With one source the amplitude criterion "drops out" and only the phase criterion selects the frequency.
  - **Edge-tone analogy** (p. 555). Screech is then like an edge tone: replacing the nozzle-to-edge distance h by (N + p)s, with N an integer, p < 1 and s the shock spacing, makes the edge-tone formula equal the screech formula.
  - Powell's 1998 keynote argued that the feedback efficiency η_t is critical for amplitude, and predicted the slope and the constant amplitude of screech over a range of M_j in reasonable agreement with Raman 1997 (p. 555).
  - Fig. 6a is a schlieren photograph of a feedback shock leaving the 3rd shock cell during intense screech at M_j = 1.4 (p. 556).
- **Eddy whiplash** (p. 556–557). Westley (1997) proposed that an eddy suffers "whiplash" as it is retarded and accelerated through the shear region at the shock reflection point, emitting the feedback shock. Krehl et al. 1998 found that a whip's tuft accelerates from M = 1.1 to 2.19 within 45 cm and emits a "whip-tip" shock. Raman leaves the analogy open, and also asks why round jets and low-AR rectangular jets do not show the single feedback-shock emission of 2-D jets (p. 557).
- **Lip thickness and reflectors** (Fig. 7, p. 557–558).
  - The lip effect was first observed by Powell (1954) and mentioned by Merle (p. 557).
  - [Ponton & Seiner 1992](ponton-1992-nozzle-exit-lip-thickness-plume-resonance.md): screech stops at M_j 1.6 with t/D = 0.2 but persists to M_j 1.9 with t/D = 0.625, complicated by a mode switch from C to D (p. 557).
  - Raman 1997 (rectangular jets, M_j 1.75): a lip thickener (t/h 0.2 → 2) induces a large sinuous oscillation and raises the screech SPL by 23 dB (p. 557; Fig. 7, p. 558).
  - Reflectors near the exit plane strengthen screech (Powell; Poldervaart et al.; Harper-Bourne & Fisher; Norum 1984). Nagel et al. cancelled screech with an upstream reflector at odd multiples of λ/4 from the exit, attributing it to a pressure minimum of the standing wave at the exit plane; Norum showed that a properly sized reflector can break the loop (p. 557).
  - Such surfaces "could perhaps account" for lab-to-lab differences in amplitude for similar nozzles (p. 557).
- **Twin jets** (Fig. 8, p. 557–560).
  - Context: sonic fatigue on the VC-10, F-15 and B-1B, and high inter-nozzle dynamic pressures on twin-engine aircraft; stealth skins and F-22-type vectoring nozzles are at risk (p. 544, 559).
  - Work at NASA Langley (including [Seiner, Manning & Ponton 1988](seiner-1988-dynamic-pressure-loads-twin-supersonic-plume-resonance.md)), the USAF and McDonnell Douglas; Umeda & Ishii's coupled multi-jets (p. 559).
  - Raman & Taghavi 1998 (JFM 354): two rectangular nozzles side by side, narrow dimensions parallel, one movable. The exit is 34.5 × 6.9 mm (AR 5.0) by the Fig. 8 sketch (p. 560). Slightly different M_j gave completely different coupling: mode I (antisymmetric) minimised the inter-nozzle SPL, mode II raised it. The paper also explains how the jets choose a mode and documents the transition (p. 559).
- **Non-uniform exit geometry** (Fig. 9, p. 559–562).
  - History: Concorde's Olympus engines had a variable "clamshell" exhaust nozzle that could be partly closed to form a notch; inlet geometry (Brocher & Makhsud) and swirl (Yu & Chen) also affect screech (p. 559).
  - Raman's AR 5 bevelled C-D nozzles (uniform, single bevel, double bevel) show three spanwise mode types: I symmetric, II antisymmetric, III oblique (A, B). Every mode is antisymmetric in the transverse (y) direction. The spanwise shock-cell structure decides the type (p. 560).
  - The frequencies follow from Tam, Shen & Raman's waveguide approach, which allows several loops and tones at once. The difference from uniform nozzles is that the dominant waveguide modes need not be the lowest order (p. 562).
- **Screech and mixing** (p. 562–563).
  - A screeching jet looked "disintegrated" to early observers (Lighthill 1963); Hammitt found sound waves alter the shock-cell structure.
  - Glass: without feedback, the axial velocity can be 50–100 % higher than with feedback; the mixing depends on NPR through the frequency, amplitude and mode of screech, and on the presence of screech more than on the shock waves (p. 562).
  - **Induced screech**: obstacles beside the narrow dimension of an on-design rectangular C-D jet set up a supersonic edge tone, giving about 30 % more mass flux downstream at a thrust penalty of almost 20 % of ideal thrust; shaped obstacles reduce the penalty (Rice & Raman; Rice; Raman & Rice 1995) (p. 562).
  - A jet aimed into a tube closed at the far end is a different phenomenon: the Hartmann whistle (a tone with slight heating) or the Hartmann–Sprenger tube (violent oscillation, very high temperature at the closed end), depending on tube length (p. 562–563).
- **Simulations up to 1998** (p. 563).
  - *FLUMES (USAF) modular model* (McDonnell Douglas; Cain, Bower and co-workers): instability growth on tanh velocity and temperature profiles; sound from the shock–instability interaction by Kerschen & Cain's analytical model; feedback by cylindrical far-field decay; lip receptivity by a Wiener–Hopf analysis. It compared "favorably" with NASA Lewis data.
  - *NRL (Kolbe, Kailasanath, Boris)*: unsteady 3-D Euler, flux-corrected transport, virtual cell embedding on a structured grid; induced-screech frequency, amplitude and thrust loss matched NASA Lewis experiments.
  - *Manning & Lele 1998*: an isolated weak screech source, modelled as a forced supersonic shear layer hit by a single oblique, near-isentropic compression wave. The radiated waveform is a short compression followed by a longer expansion, with amplitude linear in the imposed pressure rise.
  - *Tam & Shen 1997*: axisymmetric modes of a round jet at low supersonic M_j computed with the DRP scheme, artificial selective damping and suitable boundary conditions.
  - Raman expects time-resolved 3-D calculations to lead to amplitude prediction.
- **Conclusions** (p. 563–564). Powell's ideas have stood the test of time. Instability growth and instability–shock interaction can be computed, but there is no amplitude model; the key is feedback and receptivity. Screech also shows how a self-sustained loop could raise mixing in high-convective-Mach-number flows.

## How this differs from Raman 1998
Both papers are single-author reviews by Raman from the same NASA Lewis period, with the same four-link framework, the same thesis (frequency predictable, amplitude not) and the same eqs. 1–6 under the same numbers. Raman calls 1998 the "much longer and more detailed review" (p. 547). Figs 3–9 here appear to be the same material as Raman 1998's shock-modulation, shock-spacing and Strouhal, Suda travelling-shock, feedback-shock and source-phase, thick-lip, twin-jet and bevelled-nozzle figures (by the 1998 note's descriptions; not checked against that PDF). For a later reader:
- **Only here:** the chronology of research groups (Fig. 2); the Soviet results (Sedel'nikov, Anufriev, Glaznev, Bikart; p. 548); Powell's 1951 apparatus and quote; the explicit statement that the phase and amplitude criteria are separate and both required (p. 545–546); the edge-tone analogy h = (N + p)s for single-source feedback (p. 555); Powell's 1998 amplitude prediction from feedback efficiency (p. 555); Westley's "eddy whiplash" and the whip-crack analogy (p. 556–557); the Concorde clamshell, inlet and swirl effects (p. 559); the Hartmann whistle (p. 562–563); one-line descriptions of the Manning & Lele and Tam & Shen simulations (p. 563); and the twin-nozzle exit dimensions (34.5 × 6.9 mm) in the Fig. 8 sketch.
- **Only in Raman 1998:** multiple and switching tones (short-time spectra, Morlet wavelets, bicoherence); the screech–BBSAN comparison; the hot-jet, flight and Massey frequency formulas (1998 eqs. 7–10); Chan's dipole near-field model and [Norum's](norum-1983-screech-suppression-supersonic-jets.md) directivity fits; the reflector node/antinode detuning result; bevelled-nozzle source maps and waveguide orders; twin-jet frequencies and the 15–20 dB inter-nozzle levels; Zaman's mode-dependent mixing; the NRL Euler numbers; and Cain & Bower's stability explanation of the source shift.
- **Contradictions:** none found, only a difference of emphasis. Raman 1998 says up front that screech can occur with only the phase criterion met (that note, p. 49); here the two criteria are first said to be needed together (p. 546), and the phase-only case appears later as the consequence of single-source feedback (p. 555).
Cite Raman 1998 for detail and numbers; cite this paper for the history, the two-criterion argument and the edge-tone analogy.

**Measurement methods named, and their data needs:**
- *Single-shot spark schlieren* (Figs 6a, 7, 9): shock structure and feedback shocks; one image cannot give phase or frequency.
- *Schlieren films and stroboscopic lighting* (Westley & Woolley, Poldervaart, Merle): the first time-resolved views, showing acceleration between shocks and infant shocks (p. 547–549, 553).
- *Laser schlieren and laser shock detection* (Suda; Panda): point or line measurements, phase-averaged against the tone, to follow shock motion over a cycle (p. 553–554).
- *Hot-film traverse* (Raman & Rice): spectral amplitude of the screech mode and harmonics along a mean-Mach line; needs time-resolved point data but only the narrowband amplitude is plotted (p. 550).
- *Phase-averaged near-field microphone maps* (Westley & Woolley; Panda; Raman et al.; Raman & Taghavi): a traversed microphone referenced to a fixed one. Each point is time-resolved, but the field is built from separate measurements, so it needs one steady tone (p. 554, 559–561).
- *Two-microphone and transverse phase surveys* (Davies & Oldfield; Raman): mode symmetry and source location from relative phase (p. 547, 555).

**Modelling methods described** (none is new here):
- *Powell's phased monopole array* for directivity and frequency, and his limit-cycle product (eqs. 1–4).
- *Shock cells:* Prandtl–Pack vortex sheet; Tam, Jackson & Seiner's multiple-scales model; Tam's eigenfunction-expansion vortex sheet for rectangular jets; Morris et al.'s boundary-element method for any cross-section. All are linear, for weak shocks (p. 550–551).
- *Frequency:* Tam et al.'s band-centre formula (eq. 5) with k from the shock-cell model (eq. 6 for rectangular jets; waveguide modes of higher order for bevelled nozzles).
- *Source location:* one equivalent monopole, P(r) = A e^{ikr}/r, at the shock tip; phase Δφ = k(r − r₀) compared with measured transverse phase (Fig. 6b–c, p. 556).
- *Simulations:* the FLUMES modular model (linear growth on tanh profiles, analytical shock–wave sound, cylindrical feedback decay, Wiener–Hopf receptivity), Euler FCT/VCE (NRL), shock–shear-layer interaction (Manning & Lele), DRP CAA (Tam & Shen) (p. 563). No resolution, domain or validation numbers are given here.

## Key equations
Checked against page renders (p. 546, 552, 555, 556).

- **Powell directivity of the fundamental and first harmonic** (eqs. 1–2, p. 546), three or four equal sources, α from the downstream axis:
  $$D_f = \tfrac13 + \tfrac23\cos\!\big[2\pi(s/\lambda_c)(1 - M_c\cos\alpha)\big],\qquad D_h = \tfrac13 + \tfrac23\cos\!\big[4\pi(s/\lambda_c)(1 - M_c\cos\alpha)\big]$$
  The form 1/3 + (2/3)cos φ is the array factor of three equal sources with phase step φ, (e^{−iφ} + 1 + e^{iφ})/3 (my identification).
- **Powell's frequency** (eq. 3, p. 546), M_c = U_c/c with c the ambient sound speed:
  $$f = \frac{U_c}{s\,(1 + M_c)}$$
  From eq. 1, D_f = 1 at α = 180° when (s/λ_c)(1 + M_c) = 1, and λ_c = U_c/f gives eq. 3 (my working of the step the text describes).
- **Limit cycle** (eq. 4, p. 546): $Q\,\eta_s\,\eta_t\,\eta_d = 1$, with growth (printed q in the text, Q in the equation), shock–instability interaction η_s, feedback efficiency η_t and receptivity η_d.
- **Tam's band-centre (screech) frequency** (eq. 5, p. 552), u_c convection or phase velocity, a_∞ ambient sound speed, k shock-cell wavenumber; k = 2π/s recovers eq. 3:
  $$f_p = \frac{u_c\,k}{2\pi\,[1 + (u_c/a_\infty)]}$$
- **Lowest rectangular waveguide mode** (eq. 6, p. 552), b_j and h_j the fully expanded large and small dimensions:
  $$k = k_{11} = \left(\frac{1}{b_j^2} + \frac{1}{h_j^2}\right)^{1/2}\frac{\pi}{(M_j^2 - 1)^{1/2}}$$
  > Printed as "k = k₁₁(1/b_j² + 1/h_j²)^{1/2} π/(M_j² − 1)^{1/2}", with the second "=" missing; read literally it would multiply k₁₁ by itself. The corrected form above matches Raman 1998's eq. 6 and the k_{n,m} formula of [Tam 1988](tam-1988-shock-cell-screech-rectangular-non-axisymmetric-jets.md). The corresponding spacing is s = 2π/k₁₁ = 2(M_j² − 1)^{1/2}/(1/b_j² + 1/h_j²)^{1/2} (my rearrangement).
- **Panda's standing wave** (unnumbered, p. 555): $k_{sw} = k_s + k_h$, so
  $$\frac{1}{L_{sw}} = \frac{1}{\lambda_s} + \frac{1}{\lambda_h},\qquad \lambda_s = \frac{c}{f_s},\quad \lambda_h = \frac{u_c}{f_s}\ \Rightarrow\ f_s = \frac{u_c}{L_{sw}\,(1 + u_c/c)}$$
  The last form (my rearrangement) is the text's "Powell's equation with the wavelength of the standing wave replacing the shock spacing".
- **Edge-tone analogy** (p. 555): with one dominant source, the edge distance h is replaced by $h = (N + p)\,s$, N an integer, 0 ≤ p < 1. My check: the edge-tone phase condition h/u_c + h/c = (N + p)/f gives f = (N + p)u_c/[h(1 + M_c)], and substituting h = (N + p)s gives eq. 3 for any N + p.
- **Equivalent-source phase model** (Fig. 6b, p. 556): $P(r) = A\,e^{ikr}/r$, k = 2π/λ with λ the screech wavelength; phase at distance r is φ = kr, so Δφ = k(r − r₀) relative to the point at distance r₀ from the source on the exit plane.

## Notation
- s, S: shock-cell spacing; k: shock-cell wavenumber (2π/s); k₁₁: wavenumber of the lowest rectangular waveguide mode (n = 1, m = 1).
- U_c, u_c: convection speed of the instability wave; M_c = U_c/c: convective Mach number relative to the ambient sound speed (not the compressible-shear-layer M_c); λ_c: disturbance wavelength; c, a_∞: ambient sound speed.
- f: screech frequency; f_p: band-centre frequency (Tam); α: angle from the downstream jet axis; D_f, D_h: directivity of the fundamental and harmonic.
- q (Q in eq. 4), η_s, η_t, η_d: Powell's limit-cycle factors (growth, shock interaction, feedback efficiency, receptivity).
- M_j, M_d: fully expanded and design Mach numbers; U_j: fully expanded velocity; b_j, h_j: fully expanded large and small rectangular dimensions; b, h: nozzle exit dimensions; t: lip thickness; D: round-jet diameter.
- k_sw, L_sw: standing-wave wavenumber and wavelength; k_s, λ_s: acoustic; k_h, λ_h: hydrodynamic; f_s: screech frequency.
- N, p: integer and fractional parts of the number of cycles between the nozzle and the effective edge (source) in the edge-tone analogy.
- Stages A1, A2, B, C, D (round jets); rectangular spanwise modes I (symmetric), II (antisymmetric), III A/B (oblique); twin-jet coupling modes I (antisymmetric) and II.

## Relevance to this project
The project studies resonance and feedback in Shock Lab group jets with schlieren, acoustics and PIV, and runs a PyFR LES of round-jet staging (CLAUDE.md). This paper is a short historical companion to [Raman 1998](raman-1998-supersonic-jet-screech-review.md), which remains the reference for numbers. Its value now is in three arguments the longer review states less sharply, and in its record of early observations that later work has explained.

**How the 1999 picture reads against later work**
- **Two criteria, and which one survives.** Raman insists that Powell meant the phase and amplitude criteria to hold together (p. 545–546), then shows that a single dominant source removes the amplitude criterion (p. 555). Later work moved further from the amplitude criterion. The return path is the guided jet mode inside the jet, not upstream-directed free-stream sound ([Edgington-Mitchell 2018](edgington-mitchell-2018-upstream-acoustic-modes-screech-closure.md); [Nogueira 2024](nogueira-2024-guided-jet-waves.md), p. 14–17, 32), so upstream directivity does not gate the loop. What remains is the phase condition, in the form of the Tam & Tanna triad k_kh − k_s feeding the guided mode ([Edgington-Mitchell 2022](edgington-mitchell-2022-unifying-theory-jet-screech.md), p. 12–13), with Tam's weakest link read as a saddle condition ([Nogueira 2022](nogueira-2021-closure-mechanism-a1-a2-screech.md), p. 14–15).
- **The edge-tone analogy is the integer-loop picture.** h = (N + p)s (p. 555) is the same statement as [Mercier 2017](mercier-2017-screech-feedback-loop-underexpanded-round-jets.md)'s finding that an integer number of periods spans the lip to one dominant source at the 4th shock (p. 219–224), and as [Powell 1992](powell-1992-oscillation-modes-choked-circular-jets.md)'s explanation of stage jumps as N + p moving to the next integer (p. 2833). My check above shows the analogy is exact for any N + p, so it cannot by itself select a stage; that needs the shock wavenumber the loop uses (k_s1 or k_s2; Nogueira 2022, p. 7–13). The analogy also links screech to impinging and edge tones, whose loops close on a surface ([Henderson 1993](henderson-1993-tones-choked-jet-impinging-flat-plates.md); [Jordan 2018](jordan-2018-jet-flap-interaction-tones.md)).
- **"Why so directional from one source?"** Raman's open question (p. 555) has two later answers that disagree. Ray tracing finds that one leakage source already sends its strongest rays upstream ([Edgington-Mitchell 2021, shock leakage](edgington-mitchell-2021-generation-screech-tones-shock-leakage.md), p. 21–23), while the 2-D DNS of [Suzuki & Lele 2003](suzuki-2003-shock-leakage-vortex-laden-mixing-layer-screech.md) gives mainly sideline radiation from one event (p. 162–164). Manning & Lele 1998, summarised here (p. 563), is the first computation in that shock-leakage line.
- **Source at the 3rd–4th shock, moving downstream with M_j** (p. 555): consistent with Mercier's 4th-shock A1/A2 and 3rd/4th B sources and with leakage first seen at the 3rd cell (A1, C) or 4th (A2), moving with vortex strength (Edgington-Mitchell 2021, shock leakage, p. 19–30). Raman's explanation (lower amplification at higher M_j moves the wave peak downstream) differs from the leakage view, where the emission point follows where vortices weaken the sonic-line barrier, not the wavepacket peak.
- **Shock modulation of the instability wave** (Fig. 3, p. 549–550). Raman reads the dips before each shock as nonlinear. A linear Floquet (PFE) model on a shock-containing base flow reproduces modulation at α ± jα_s ([Nogueira 2021, wavepacket modulation](nogueira-2021-wavepacket-modulation-shock-containing-jets.md)), but it fails where strong upstream waves make a standing wave, as at the screech frequency (that note, p. 17). Westley & Woolley's "acceleration between shocks" (p. 549) is most simply the interference of the KH wave with the upstream guided mode at L_sw ([Edgington-Mitchell 2021, waves](edgington-mitchell-2020-waves-in-screeching-jets.md), p. 14–23; the Panda note's reading of Panda 1999). A single-probe amplitude trace like Fig. 3 cannot separate shock modulation from this interference; a k_x split can.
- **Shock strength is not amplitude** (p. 552–553): still unexplained by any model in the library, and consistent with the topic-page view that amplitude is set by nozzle, lip and surroundings rather than by the shocks ([Ponton 1992](ponton-1992-nozzle-exit-lip-thickness-plume-resonance.md); [Powell 1992](powell-1992-oscillation-modes-choked-circular-jets.md), p. 2832–2835). The +23 dB from a lip thickener at M_j 1.75 (Fig. 7) is the largest single receptivity effect quoted in either Raman review.
- **Staging** (p. 547–548) was unexplained in 1999. Shock-spectrum peaks (k_s1, k_s2) and guided-mode closure now account for the A1/A2 and B/C jumps (Nogueira 2022; Edgington-Mitchell 2022). Raman's remark that the shock spacing to use is unclear when spacing varies (p. 546) is exactly the effect that later explains staging. Merle's stability grading (A1 unstable, A2 stable, B very unstable, C very stable, D unstable) and Powell's unpredictable precession of flapping modes (p. 547) are early observations of the switching and precession that [Mazharmanesh 2026](mazharmanesh-2026-triadic-interaction-mean-flow-deformation-screech.md) and [Nogueira 2026](nogueira-2026-flapping-motion-screeching-round-jets.md) now model.
- **Twin rectangular jets:** the Fig. 8 sketch gives the Raman & Taghavi exit as 34.5 × 6.9 mm, AR 5.0, which confirms the AR 5 that the twin-jet page takes from [Esfahani 2021, coupling modes](esfahani-2021-coupling-modes-twin-rectangular-jets.md) (p. 3). The [Raman 1998](raman-1998-supersonic-jet-screech-review.md) note says that review does not give it.

**Methods and data for the lab workflow (CLAUDE.md)**
- *Time-resolved data* (high-speed schlieren, microphones, LES): the films of Westley & Woolley and the hot-film traverse were the time-resolved tools of the period. Today's equivalents are SPOD of time-resolved schlieren with k_x splitting, which separates the shock modulation and guided-mode interference that Fig. 3 mixes, and the SPOD → reconstruction → Hilbert POD pipeline ([Raiola 2025](raiola-2025-hilbert-pod.md)) to follow screech amplitude cycle by cycle. That is the quantity a test of Powell's limit-cycle product (eq. 4) or of a receptivity change (lip thickener, reflector) would need. Nothing in this review resolves how frequency or amplitude change in time.
- *Non-time-resolved data* (PIV, spark schlieren): phase averaging against a reference microphone is the method behind the near-field maps (p. 554, 559–561) and Panda's shock motion (p. 554). It needs one steady tone, so near a staging point or with Merle's "unstable" stages it mixes states; POD-pair phase recovery has the same limit.
- *Source location by transverse phase* (Fig. 6): one fixed microphone line in the exit plane, compared with a one-source model. It assumes one source, which is the assumption leakage imaging later tested (Edgington-Mitchell 2021, shock leakage).

**Modelling side and the PyFR staging LES**
- Raman's 1999 simulation list (p. 563) separates two strands that are now merged in LES: modular models that chain growth, interaction, feedback and receptivity, and Euler or CAA simulations of the whole loop. The FLUMES chain (tanh profiles, analytical shock–wave sound, cylindrical feedback decay, Wiener–Hopf receptivity) is the ancestor of the resonance models of [Mancinelli 2019](mancinelli-2019-screech-prediction-upstream-modes.md) and [Mancinelli 2021](mancinelli-2021-complex-valued-resonance-model-screech.md), with free-stream feedback replaced by the guided mode.
- For the PyFR continuation LES (`Simulations/PyFR_Staging_Continuation/`): the lip-thickness and reflector effects (p. 557) mean the LES nozzle exterior and any upstream surfaces should match the experiment before comparing amplitudes or staging limits. The equivalent-source phase test (Fig. 6) is cheap to apply to LES pressure on an exit-plane line to check which shock radiates in A1 and in A2.

**Rocket noise.** Screech is absent from full-scale launch and static-firing data ([Lubert 2022](lubert-2022-supersonic-jet-noise-launch-vehicles-review.md)). The Soviet finding that preheating lowers screech intensity (p. 548) is one early piece of that story. The induced-screech mixing results (+30 % mass flux, 20 % thrust loss; p. 562) and the Hartmann–Sprenger tube (p. 562–563) are side topics; resonance tubes are the subject of Thethy et al. 2019 (in `Papers_to_get.md`).
