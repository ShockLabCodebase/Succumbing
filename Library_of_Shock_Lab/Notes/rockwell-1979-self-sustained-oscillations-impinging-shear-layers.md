## Summary
The review argues that all shear-layer–impingement oscillations (edge, hole and ring tones; jet–plate, jet–flap and jet–cylinder tones; shear tones and cavity tones; Fig. 1) share one mechanism. A loop of four events sustains them: disturbances are fed back from the impingement region to the sensitive region near separation, they induce vorticity fluctuations there, these are amplified by shear-layer instability, and the impingement then produces organised disturbances (p. 68–69). The authors compare the configurations through the feedback path (acoustic when the acoustic wavelength is comparable with the impingement length, "hydrodynamic" near-field when it is much longer; p. 69), the phase between separation and impingement, the frequency stages and their jumps, hysteresis, the amplitude, the minimum length for onset, and coupling with acoustic or free-surface resonators (p. 69–89). They then survey the prediction models, from Curle's L/λ = n + 1/4 through Powell's feedback-control model to linear-stability models with and without an explicit feedback condition, semi-empirical cavity formulas, vortex-sheet, finite-difference and discrete-vortex models (p. 89–93). Frequency could be predicted reasonably well for some geometries, but amplitude only in limited cases (p. 89). The review is mostly about low-speed, laminar-separation flows, but it includes the early evidence on underexpanded planar and round jets: upstream reflectors and feedback-blocking plates change the shock-cell structure (Poldervaart et al. 1974), and a plate at 6D turns a supersonic jet's near field into a standing-wave pattern (Neuwerth 1973) (p. 69–74, 87–88).

## Key findings
- **The loop has four events, and feedback makes the oscillation global** (p. 68–69). Upstream propagation of disturbances from impingement to separation, conversion into vorticity fluctuations near separation, amplification along the shear layer, and production of organised disturbances at impingement. Feedback gives "direct communication" between the two ends, "thereby ensuring that the shear-layer oscillation is a globally organized phenomenon" (p. 68–69).
- **Acoustic or hydrodynamic feedback depends on L/λ_a** (p. 69).
  - At high speed the acoustic wavelength λ is of the same order as the impingement length L, so the acoustic speed sets the feedback delay. Powell (1961) found upstream radiation significant there and modelled the fed-back waves with simple acoustic sources.
  - At low speed in gases the feedback is in the source's near field (L/λ ≪ 1), so the mechanism is "essentially hydrodynamic" within a generalised acoustic description, and an acoustic dipole models it. In liquids L/λ ≪ 1 almost always, and the feedback is purely hydrodynamic, equivalent to the Biot–Savart law (footnote 1, p. 69).
  - Whatever the fluid and speed, jet–edge oscillations show qualitatively similar frequency trends with L and U and similar hysteresis (p. 69).
- **Sources at impingement** (p. 69–71).
  - Powell (1961) treated the edge source with Lighthill's (1952) method: the pressure fluctuation is set by the fluctuating force on the edge, taken proportional to the transverse momentum of the impinging stream (p. 69).
  - At higher speed vortices form well upstream of the edge, and the straining of a vortex can itself be a source. Poldervaart et al. (1974) showed this with no edge at all: in an underexpanded planar jet (jet pressure ratio 3.5) acoustic waves radiate from where large structures are about to break down or "where a vortex interacts with a shock wave" (Fig. 2a, p. 69–70).
- **Feedback changes the shock-cell structure of underexpanded jets** (Figs 2 and 4, p. 70–74).
  - Reflectors upstream of the planar nozzle exit enhance the fed-back waves; the jet then oscillates transversely with larger amplitude and fewer shock cells are visible (Fig. 2b, p. 70). Hammitt (1961) and Glass (1968) saw similar changes, and Glass found that acoustically insulating the duct round the jet increased the number of visible shock cells (p. 70–71).
  - Plates placed between the nozzle and the downstream region block the feedback: as they are moved closer to the jet, more shock cells become visible (Fig. 4, p. 73–74). Karamcheti et al. (1969) stopped a low-speed edge tone the same way (p. 73–74).
  - Both experiments imply that most of the fed-back energy travels upstream through the fluid on either side of the jet. "For the supersonic jet ... there can be no upstream propagation through the core" (p. 74).
- **In subsonic impinging round jets the feedback can travel inside the core** (Fig. 5, p. 74). Evertz et al. (1976), Neuwerth (1973) and Wagner (1971) give evidence of substantial upstream propagation through the subsonic jet core. Fig. 5 shows upstream sound waves and downstream vortices in an M 0.85 jet on a flap. A streamlined body placed in the core at the nozzle exit attenuates this feedback (p. 74).
- **Reflectors add feedback paths** (p. 74, 76). Chanaud & Powell (1965) induced a strong radial mode of propagation by reflection in a jet–hole system (p. 74). For a planar jet, the relative phase of the disturbances arriving at the two sides of the nozzle exit decides whether the oscillation is self-sustained; asymmetric reflectors are proposed as a way to study this (p. 76).
- **Receptivity at separation** (p. 74–75).
  - Morkovin & Paranjape (1971), using a movable localised acoustic source near a planar jet, found that the local transverse pressure gradient controls conversion of pressure into vorticity fluctuations. By analogy with a perturbed viscous stagnation point, they proposed that back-and-forth motion of the detachment point converts irrotational pressure into rotational perturbations, so a sharp separation edge should be optimal (p. 74–75).
  - Bechert & Pfizenmaier (1975) measured the deflection of the dividing streamline of an axisymmetric jet under axial acoustic excitation: a roughly parabolic envelope near the edge, growing with Strouhal number. The authors note the gap between such real shear layers and thin-layer theory (Crighton 1975) with a modified Kutta–Joukowski condition (p. 75).
- **The phase criterion is not universal** (p. 75–76).
  - Early models assumed a phase of 2π(n + 1/4) between transverse velocity fluctuations at separation and impingement. It accommodates n stages, but "the value of 1/4 has never been rigorously justified" (p. 75).
  - Jet–edge: Stegen & Karamcheti (1970) found that the phase varies along the jet as a power law and that the separation–impingement differences are "substantially different" from 2π(n + 1/4) (p. 75).
  - Mixing-layer–edge and cavity: Hussain & Zaman (1977a,b) and Sarohia (1975, 1977) found about 2π(n + 1/2) (p. 75).
  - The phase also varies strongly across the shear layer. Spatial stability theory for a tanh profile (Michalke 1972) predicts this in the linear region, but near the edge nonlinearity and streamline curvature distort it (Fig. 6, p. 75). So moving the edge transversely changes the effective phase difference (p. 76).
  - All these phases are time averages. Because of jitter (below), "instantaneous phase measurements should be considered" (p. 76).
- **Vortex–edge interaction "jitters"** (Fig. 3, p. 72–73). Hydrogen-bubble images of a cavity shear layer (Re_θ0 = 106, L/θ0 = 142; Rockwell & Knisely 1978) under fixed conditions show a vortex completely clipped, partly clipped or escaping the edge, so substantial cycle-to-cycle jitter occurs. The strength and phase of clipping are tied to the feedback. Without the edge the flow is less organised and vortices pair upstream of the observation station (Fig. 3e, p. 72–73).
- **Three-dimensionality** (Fig. 7, p. 76–78). Spanwise cells in an oscillating cavity flow are about as wide as the streamwise wavelength, as Miksad (1972) found for weakly 3-D nonimpinging layers (p. 77). Other sources: vortex stretching near the stagnation region (Morkovin 1978), end-wall junctions and side walls, the azimuthal instability modes of axisymmetric systems (Morris 1976, Chan 1977; visible in Evertz et al.'s schlieren and spectra), and 3-D recirculation (p. 77–78). Streamwise vorticity introduced at separation is a promising control: spoilers at a cavity's upstream edge substantially reduce the amplitude (Rossiter 1964, Heller & Bliss 1975, Willmarth et al. 1978) (p. 78).
- **Frequency stages collapse across configurations** (Fig. 8, p. 78–80).
  - The frequency at given L depends strongly on the separation conditions: the velocity profile u(y), Re_θ0 = Uθ0/ν, and for turbulent separation the boundary-layer structure (p. 78). Two Strouhal numbers are used: S_θ0 = fθ0/U and S_L = fL/U (p. 79).
  - Jet–edge (Fig. 8a; Brown, Nyborg, Powell and Karamcheti et al. data, Re_d 643–704): with a fully developed laminar exit flow the data collapse into three stages (p. 80). My reading: stage 1 at S_L ≈ 0.45–0.5 over L/δ0 ≈ 5–19 (with a lower branch near 0.35–0.4 for 10 ≲ L/δ0 ≲ 19), stage 2 at S_L ≈ 1.0–1.1 from L/δ0 ≈ 10, and stage 3 near 1.6–1.7. S_L is almost constant within each stage, i.e. f ∝ U/L.
  - Cavity (Fig. 8b, Sarohia 1975; Re_δ0 = 2860 and 920) and mixing-layer–edge (Fig. 8c, Hussain & Zaman 1977) show the same jumps and stages (p. 79–80). Hussain & Zaman's data show "universality of both S_θ0 and S_L distributions" over a range of velocities and lengths (p. 80).
  - Brown 1937 and Powell 1961 (air), Brackenridge 1960 and Gross 1964 (water), hole and ring tones (Chanaud & Powell 1965) and the jet–wall case (Ho & Nossier 1976) show the same jumps and stages, which the authors take as evidence of one mechanism (p. 80).
- **Several peaks coexist, and a jump may be switching** (Fig. 9, p. 80–81). Spectra usually show more than one clearly defined oscillatory mode, but plots like Fig. 8 show only the strongest (p. 80). At a frequency jump, two frequencies that appear together in time-averaged spectra "may, in reality, be due to the intermittent switching from one mode to another" (Hussain & Zaman 1977a,b); the authors link this to the jitter of Fig. 3 (p. 81).
- **Hysteresis depends on the flow class** (Fig. 10, p. 81–82).
  - The L or U at which S jumps depends on the sweep direction in the hole tone (Chanaud & Powell 1965, Fig. 10) and the edge tone (Powell 1961) (p. 81).
  - No hysteresis has been seen in mixing-layer flows: cavities (Ethembabaoglu 1973, Sarohia 1977) and wedges (Hussain & Zaman 1977a,b) (p. 81).
  - Without hysteresis (wedge), the energy shifts gradually from one dominant frequency to the other as a jump is negotiated, seen at a single point in the shear layer (Hussain & Zaman 1977b). With hysteresis the shift may occur over a much shorter distance, so the whole streamwise pattern of spectra, and the energy transfer between fundamental, subharmonics and harmonics, should be examined (p. 81–82).
  - My reading of Fig. 10 (hole tone, S = fd/U against Re_d = Ud/ν): at L/d = 3.0, stage 1 at S ≈ 0.50 holds up to Re_d ≈ 1910 and stage 2 at S ≈ 0.65 holds down to ≈ 1420, so the loop is ≈ 500 wide in Re_d; at L/d = 2.5, stage 1 (S ≈ 0.57) holds to ≈ 2380 and stage 2 (S ≈ 0.75–0.76) down to ≈ 1800. A third branch at S ≈ 0.68 is marked L/d = 2.0 (Re_d ≈ 2200–2580).
- **The edge organises the whole shear layer** (Fig. 9, p. 81–83). With the edge, the spectra along the shear layer (Re_θ0 = 243, L/θ0 = 144; along u/U = 0.95) carry sharp peaks from x/θ0 ≈ 8 onwards, against broad humps without it, so the enhancement "is a global phenomenon extending along the entire length of the shear layer" (p. 82). Miksad (1972) saw strikingly similar spectra in a nonimpinging layer with and without sound at the fundamental, where a single forcing frequency organised all the coexisting frequencies (p. 82–83).
  - My reading of Fig. 9b: fundamental at fθ0/U ≈ 0.0155, a subharmonic near 0.008 growing from x/θ0 ≈ 71–107, and a further peak near 0.028 (not at 2f) from x/θ0 ≈ 53. Without the edge (Fig. 9a) the broad hump is centred near fθ0/U ≈ 0.014 for x/θ0 = 53–125 and moves to ≈ 0.008 at x/θ0 = 144.
  - High Re: an edge can still enhance organisation. Ethembabaoglu (1973) found well-defined wall-pressure peaks in a cavity at Re_L ~ 10⁶, and coherent oscillations may exist in regimes earlier reported as "turbulent" (Powell 1961, Bauer 1963, Sarohia 1977). Conditional sampling should be used to find them (p. 83).
- **Modes persist into neighbouring stages** (Fig. 11, p. 83–84). In Stegen & Karamcheti's (1970) band-pass-filtered transverse velocity (Re_d ≈ 700), stage 2 contains a high- and a low-frequency mode. The low-frequency one is the stage-1 frequency carried into stage 2, and its amplitude varies with x as in stage 1, so a mode persists into the next stage after the dominant frequency has jumped. The authors call understanding this "essential" for jumps and hysteresis (p. 83–84).
- **Amplitude follows the nonimpinging shear layer** (Figs 11–12, p. 84–85).
  - Stage-1 growth along the jet is "nearly exponential", so linear stability may give the growth rate (Sarohia 1975) (p. 84). My reading of the log axis: stage 1 rises from v_rms/U ≈ 0.015 at x/L = 0.1 to ≈ 0.08 at x/L ≈ 0.9 with visible saturation, while the stage-1 extension during stage 2 rises from ≈ 0.003 to ≈ 0.035, nearly straight; stage 2 rises steeply from ≈ 0.004 to ≈ 0.09.
  - At longer L amplitudes saturate and harmonics and subharmonics appear; Hussain & Zaman's (1977b) shear-tone amplitude against L resembles the nonimpinging layer (Freymuth 1966, Miksad 1972) (p. 84).
  - Powell's (1961) rms edge force from far-field pressure (30° wedge, Re_d = 346; Fig. 12) grows, saturates and falls as L increases, independent of the stage jumps (p. 84–85). My reading (log axes): F/(ρU²bd) ≈ 1.7 at L/d ≈ 3.5, a peak of ≈ 5 near L/d ≈ 6 in stage 1, ≈ 3 at the stage 2–3 jump (L/d ≈ 13) and ≈ 1 by L/d ≈ 25.
  - Evertz et al. (1976): sound intensity scaled as U⁶ for a jet–flap system and as U⁷ for the free jet, with an SPL about 20 dB higher with the flap; both the organisation and the broadband background increased with the flap (p. 85).
  - In nonimpinging layers the subharmonic and the three-halves harmonic can exceed the fundamental after vortex coalescence (Miksad 1972), so harmonics and subharmonics should be tracked with x (p. 85).
- **Onset needs a minimum length** (Fig. 13, p. 85–87).
  - Below a minimum L_m no self-sustained oscillation occurs, and L_m depends strongly on separation conditions (p. 85).
  - Sarohia (1977), axisymmetric cavity with accelerated approach flows: L_m(Re_δ0)^{1/2}/δ0 is nearly independent of cavity depth for W/δ0 ≳ 2 (p. 85–86). My reading of Fig. 13: about 300–330 for W/δ0 ≳ 2–3, rising to ≈ 500 for W/δ0 ≈ 1.
  - Karamcheti et al. (1969): a nearly uniform exit profile gives a smaller L_m than a fully developed one. Bauer (1963): L_m rises significantly below Re ≈ 800 (fully developed jet–edge). Brackenridge (1960): L_m rises with viscosity at fixed velocity (p. 86).
  - So onset is controlled by shear-layer instability. Powell (1961) used a minimum-gain criterion with several empirical constants; Woolley & Karamcheti (1974) attribute onset solely to growth: a minimum integrated amplification between separation and impingement. That criterion had not been implemented, because of near-field profile changes and streamwise gradients (p. 86–87). Approach-flow unsteadiness, and standing waves at high speed, may also matter (p. 87).
- **Resonance** (Fig. 14, p. 87–89).
  - Cavity resonance divides into shallow (longitudinal waves) and deep (transverse waves); jet–edge oscillations near pipes or cavities belong here too (Nyborg et al. 1952, 1953) (p. 87). Hole and ring tones were loudest when the upstream muffler's resonance matched the tone (Chanaud & Powell 1965) (p. 87).
  - Axisymmetric jet–plate: a jet–plate standing wave greatly enhances organisation (Wagner 1971; Ho & Nossier 1976). Wagner explained it as a downstream wave from the growing vortices plus an upstream wave from the impingement stagnation point (p. 87).
  - Underexpanded jet on a wall (Neuwerth 1973, jet pressure ratio 3.0): without the wall, vortex rings wander downstream past a stationary shock-cell pattern (Fig. 14a). With a wall 6 diameters downstream, a standing-wave pattern forms from the vortex-ring pressure field travelling downstream and acoustic waves travelling upstream. It sits "on the periphery of the jet core", unlike the subsonic case, where the waves propagate inside the core (Fig. 14b, p. 87–88).
  - Helmholtz resonance of a cylindrical cavity round a jet–hole gives strong oscillations over narrow velocity ranges (Wilson et al. 1971, Morel 1977) (p. 88–89). Flexible or spring-mounted bodies act like resonators, increasing the effectiveness of the feedback (p. 89).
- **Prediction: frequency yes, amplitude rarely** (p. 89–93).
  - Frequency has been predicted "with reasonable success" for some geometries by linear stability or semi-empirical methods; amplitude only for limited cases, and because amplitudes are nonlinear the full momentum equations will probably need numerical solution (p. 89).
  - Models with a feedback condition and models without one (maximum integrated amplification) both gave "reasonably good" frequencies, but no critical comparison exists (p. 90–91). The model list is under "Modelling side" below.
  - The authors' summary: first predict the nonimpinging layer (vortex formation, coalescence), which quasi-2-D methods can do only in the near field because 3-D effects grow downstream; then add the edge's distortion of the approaching flow and its communication with the upstream layer (p. 92). Engineers need simple criteria to identify "danger spots", so simplified models "on sound physical grounds" should be pursued (p. 92–93).

## Setup / method
- **Scope** (p. 67–68). Oscillations caused by free-shear-layer instability plus disturbance feedback. Resonant and fluid-elastic effects are discussed only briefly; their full classification is in Naudascher (1967) and Rockwell & Naudascher (1978, the companion cavity review in ASME J. Fluids Eng., "in press") (p. 67–68, 87–89). Applications listed: transonic wind tunnels, aircraft components, slotted flumes, high-head gates, velocity probes and pressure probes (p. 67).
- **Configurations** (Fig. 1, p. 68), grouped by the four kinds of shear layer:

| Planar jets | Axisymmetric jets | Planar and axisymmetric mixing layers |
|---|---|---|
| jet–edge (edge tone) | jet–ring (ring tone) | mixing-layer–edge (shear tone) |
| jet–slot | jet–hole (hole tone) | rectangular cavity |
| jet–cylinder | jet–plate | axisymmetric cavity |
| jet–surface | jet–flap | special cavity (gate with lip) |

- **Organisation.** Feedback mechanism (p. 68–78); frequency (p. 78–83); amplitude (p. 83–85); onset (p. 85–87); resonance (p. 87–89); prediction (p. 89–93). About 100 references, mostly 1950–1978.
- **Data sources in the reviewed work and what they need** (p. 80, 83–84).
  - Frequencies come from spectral analysis or filtering of hot-wire velocity, far-field microphones, hydrophones, wall-pressure transducers, or flow visualisation (p. 80). All are point measurements. Spectra and spectral peaks need time-resolved signals but are block (time) averages.
  - Amplitudes: narrow band-pass filtering of hot-wire signals at each spectral peak (Stegen & Karamcheti 1970), or far-field pressure converted to an edge force (Powell 1961) (p. 83–84).
  - Phase: time-averaged phase between points along and across the shear layer, i.e. cross-spectral phase maps (Fig. 6; Sarohia 1977). The authors note that phase relations had not been measured directly as cross-correlations between pressure and velocity at separation, along the layer and at impingement (p. 75), and call for instantaneous phase because of jitter (p. 76).
  - Visualisation: hydrogen bubbles in water (Figs 3, 7), schlieren and shadowgraph of supersonic jets (Figs 2, 4, 14), photographs of a jet–flap (Fig. 5).
  - Coherent structures at high Re: the authors recommend conditional sampling, as used for coherence in nonimpinging turbulent flows (Davies & Yule 1975, Roshko 1976) (p. 83).
- **Modelling side** (p. 89–93). No new analysis; the models reviewed, with their assumptions:
  - *Curle (1953)*: discrete vortices on both sides of the jet; L/λ = n + 1/4 deduced from Brown's (1937) smoke pictures, combined with a semi-empirical convection speed to give a frequency formula. Includes feedback, but no overall mechanism (p. 89).
  - *Nyborg (1954)*: infinitesimally thin jet coupled to a uniform outer pressure field; no jet instability, but it shows the unsteady pressure field must be included (p. 89–90).
  - *Powell (1961)*: a feedback control system whose overall transfer function combines the transverse perturbation at the nozzle exit, amplification along the jet, and the unsteady field near the edge. Self-sustained oscillation requires a transfer-function magnitude of 1 and a phase of 2πn. Two constants in the amplitude equation are unknown, and the frequency equation needs an unknown convection speed and the assumed 2π(n + 1/4) phase, so several flow details must be known in advance. It captures the central features of jet–edge oscillation "in at least a qualitative sense" (p. 90).
  - *Woolley & Karamcheti (1974; cavity version 1973)*: non-parallel stability; the frequency and higher stages follow from the integrated amplification A of a disturbance along a line of constant frequency. No feedback condition; no quantitative comparison with jet–edge data yet (p. 90).
  - *Feedback-condition cavity models*: King et al. (1958) required perturbations at separation to be π out of phase with cavity-volume fluctuations, combined with a thin-shear-layer instability analysis (porous-plate cavity). Martin et al. (1975) and Rockwell (1977b) used the same condition with global and streamwise-dependent instability parameters for a gate with extended lip and a rectangular cavity (p. 90).
  - *Sarohia (1975)*: maximum integrated amplification for an axisymmetric cavity; he and Woolley & Karamcheti (1976) note that the observed frequencies still require a prescribed phase difference between separation and impingement (p. 90–91).
  - *Linear stability in the near field* also gives phase speed, wavenumber (Hussain & Zaman 1977b) and amplitude (Sarohia 1975). The near-separation region, with strong profile change and non-parallel effects, is not described; Crighton & Gaster (1976) treat x > 1D only. Edge models need downstream boundary conditions that allow successive vortex pairing (p. 91).
  - *Semi-empirical high-speed cavity formulas*: Rossiter (1964), Heller & Bliss (1975), Block (1976). Constants for the convection speed and the phase between cavity waves and vortices are fitted; they correlate well with high-speed data (p. 91).
  - *Bilanin & Covert (1973)*: vortex-sheet shear layer, giving a Rossiter-like equation with no empirical constants; neglects coupling between the shear-layer motion and the cavity-wave structure and keeps only downstream shear-layer waves. "Could serve as a framework for future studies" (p. 91).
  - *Borland (1977)*: inviscid finite-difference solution of the whole high-speed cavity flow; no vortex coalescence (p. 91–92).
  - *Discrete-vortex models*: Hardin & Mason (1976) predicted several features of a cavity, but, as for bluff bodies (Clements & Maull 1975), impingement on a wall bounding a recirculation zone needs artificial dissipation of a large fraction of the vortices to match the mean flow (p. 92).
  - *Chan (1977)*: eddy-viscosity energy-integral model of the energy exchange between mean flow, turbulence and wave in a nonimpinging jet; it predicts wave decay in the nonlinear region and is suggested for impinging flows (p. 92).
  - *Nonimpinging building blocks*: finite-difference methods for vortex formation (Grant 1974, Ikeda 1977) and discrete-vortex methods for coalescence (Acton 1976, 1977) (p. 92).

## Key equations
The review has no numbered equations. The relations it states in words, written out:

- **Strouhal numbers** (p. 78–79): $S_{\theta_0}=f\theta_0/U$ and $S_L=fL/U$, with $Re=U\theta_0/\nu$. Fig. 10 uses $S=fd/U$ and $Re_d=Ud/\nu$; Figs 8b, 6 and 13 use δ0.
- **Feedback regime** (p. 69): acoustic when $L/\lambda_a=O(1)$ (high speed); near-field "hydrodynamic" when $L/\lambda_a\ll1$ (low-speed gases, liquids). Here λ_a is the acoustic wavelength.
- **Phase criterion** (p. 75, 89–90): phase difference of transverse velocity between separation and impingement $\Delta\phi=2\pi(n+\tfrac14)$ in early models (Curle; Powell's frequency equation), and $\Delta\phi\approx2\pi(n+\tfrac12)$ measured in mixing-layer–edge and cavity flows (Hussain & Zaman; Sarohia).
- **Curle** (p. 89): $L/\lambda=n+\tfrac14$. With $\lambda=U_c/f$ this gives $f=(U_c/L)(n+\tfrac14)$ (my rearrangement; the review gives only the ratio).
- **Powell's self-sustaining condition** (p. 90): $|T(\omega)|=1,\ \arg T(\omega)=2\pi n$, where $T$ is the loop transfer function (nozzle-exit receptivity × amplification along the jet × edge field). The symbol T is mine.
- **Onset scaling** (Fig. 13, p. 85–86): $L_m\,Re_{\delta_0}^{1/2}/\delta_0\approx\text{const}$ for $W/\delta_0\gtrsim2$ (Sarohia 1977), with $Re_{\delta_0}=U\delta_0/\nu$ presumably.
- **Integrated amplification** (p. 86, 90): oscillation requires a minimum $A=\int_0^{L}(-\alpha_i)\,dx$ along a line of constant frequency (Woolley & Karamcheti 1974). The integral form and α_i (spatial growth rate) are my notation; the review gives the criterion in words.

> Derived checks on the reviewed data (my arithmetic, from figure readings; verify before use):
> - **Jet–edge stages (Fig. 8a).** With a constant convection speed and negligible acoustic delay, $S_L=(U_c/U)(n+p)$, so stage ratios fix p. Stage 2/stage 1 ≈ 1.07/0.47 ≈ 2.3. That fits $L/\lambda=n-\tfrac14$ (ratio 1.75/0.75 = 2.33) with U_c ≈ 0.6U, which also puts stage 3 at 0.6 × 2.75 ≈ 1.65, as read. It does not fit Curle's n + 1/4 (ratio 1.8) or n + 1/2 (1.67). Stegen & Karamcheti's power-law phase means U_c is not constant, so this is only a consistency check, but it agrees with the review's statement that measured jet–edge phases differ substantially from 2π(n + 1/4) (p. 75).
> - **Hole tone (Fig. 10).** Stage 2/stage 1 = 0.65/0.50 = 1.30 at L/d = 3.0 and 0.755/0.57 = 1.32 at L/d = 2.5. If each jump adds one wavelength, n + p ≈ 3.1–3.3 for "stage 1", and with S_L = fL/U = 1.50 and 1.43, U_c ≈ 0.45–0.46U. So the published "stage 1" of the hole tone is not a one-wavelength loop.
> - **Mixing-layer–edge (Fig. 8c).** S_L rises within each stage, so fθ0/U stays at ≈ 0.011–0.015 in all three stages (e.g. 0.70/55 = 0.0127 and 0.93/85 = 0.011 in stage 1; 1.72/120 = 0.014 in stage 3). That is between the natural (0.012–0.013) and most-amplified (0.0164) values of a tanh layer ([Michalke 1984](michalke-1984-survey-jet-instability-theory.md), p. 195–196). Here the initial layer sets the frequency and L selects the stage, whereas in the jet–edge S_L is nearly constant within a stage (f ∝ U/L).
> - **Cavity phase map (Fig. 6, Sarohia 1977).** Phase lines run from φ/2π ≈ 0.1 near separation to ≈ 1.5–1.7 at the downstream corner (x/δ0 = 6), i.e. about 2π(1 + 1/2), consistent with the n + 1/2 rule. With fL/U = 0.67 this implies U_c/U ≈ 0.67/1.6 ≈ 0.42.

## Notation
- L: impingement length (separation to edge, nozzle to plate, cavity length); L_m: minimum length for onset; W: cavity depth; d: nozzle width or diameter; b: wedge height (span) in Fig. 12.
- θ0, δ0: momentum and boundary-layer thickness at separation; U: free-stream or jet exit velocity; U_c: disturbance convection speed (my symbol).
- f: oscillation frequency; S_θ0 = fθ0/U; S_L = fL/U; S = fd/U (Fig. 10); Re = Uθ0/ν; Re_d = Ud/ν; Re_δ0; Re_L.
- λ: acoustic wavelength (p. 69) or vortex spacing (Curle, p. 89); n: stage (mode) integer; φ: phase (Fig. 6, as φ/2π).
- u, v: streamwise and transverse velocity; (u′_f)²/df: velocity spectrum (Fig. 9); v_rms: filtered transverse rms (Fig. 11); F: rms transverse force on the edge (Fig. 12); A: integrated amplification.
- "Jet pressure ratio": the stagnation-to-ambient ratio, i.e. NPR (Figs 2, 4, 14).

## Relevance to this project
This is the standard early review of feedback oscillations of impinging shear layers. Later papers in the library cite it for the generality of staging ([Ho 1981](ho-1981-dynamics-impinging-jet-feedback-phenomenon.md), p. 132) and as the archetype of resonance-driven global modes ([Huerre 1990](huerre-1990-local-global-instabilities-spatially-developing-flows.md), p. 498). It is mostly about low-speed, laminar-separation flows, but almost every open question on the [impinging-jets-and-edge-tones](../Topics/impinging-jets-and-edge-tones.md) and [screech-staging-and-mode-switching](../Topics/screech-staging-and-mode-switching.md) pages is already posed here in some form. It fits the CLAUDE.md research area directly: resonance and feedback, impinging jets, staging and intermittency.

**How it fits the current threads**
- **The loop.** The four-event loop (p. 68) is the structure that [Edgington-Mitchell 2019](edgington-mitchell-2019-aeroacoustic-resonance-review.md) uses for screech and impinging tones (KH growth, sound generation, return, receptivity), and the source of Powell's (1961) two conditions: unit loop gain and a 2πn phase (p. 90), restated for screech in [Raman 1999](raman-1999-supersonic-jet-screech-half-century-powell-present.md).
- **Guided or external return.** The review records the first evidence for both sides of the present debate.
  - Subsonic impinging round jets: upstream propagation inside the core (Evertz, Neuwerth, Wagner), killed by a streamlined body in the core at the exit (p. 74). This is the in-core standing wave that [Tam & Ahuja 1990](tam-1990-theoretical-model-discrete-tone-impinging-jets.md) read as the guided upstream neutral mode (topic page), and it predates [Jordan 2018](jordan-2018-jet-flap-interaction-tones.md) and [Stavropoulos 2026](stavropoulos-2026-jet-edge-interaction.md) on guided-mode closure of jet–edge tones.
  - Supersonic jets: feedback-blocking plates show the return travels outside the jet, and "there can be no upstream propagation through the core" (p. 74). Neuwerth's standing wave with a plate at 6D sits on the periphery of the core (p. 88). This matches the external closure of the group's supersonic impinging jets ([Weightman et al. 2019](weightman-2019-nozzle-external-geometry-azimuthal-mode-impinging-jet.md)). The statement is right for plane sound in a supersonic stream but not for the jet's guided modes: [Bogey 2017](bogey-2017-feedback-loop-upwind-waves-ideally-expanded-impinging-jets.md) see upstream waves inside a supersonic core (p. 583–585), and Tam & Ahuja's supersonic modes lie "outside the jet but guided by it" (p. 79).
  - The streamlined-core-body test (p. 74) is an experimental discriminator for in-core return that the library's papers have not used; for a supersonic jet it would add its own shocks.
- **The phase lag.** The review already says the 1/4 in 2π(n + 1/4) was never justified, that jet–edge and mixing-layer flows give different values (n + 1/4 against n + 1/2), and that moving the edge across the layer changes the phase (p. 75–76). That is the present situation for supersonic impinging jets, where published p values run from +0.4 to −0.49 under different loop definitions ([Weightman 2017](weightman-2017-phase-lag-supersonic-jet-impingement.md); topic page), and where dispersive models turn p into a free end-condition phase φ ([Jaunet 2019](jaunet-2019-dynamics-round-jet-impingement.md)). My stage-ratio check (Key equations) suggests the classic jet–edge data fit n − 1/4 with constant U_c. The review's call for instantaneous phase because of jitter (p. 76) is the topic page's open question on cycle-resolved Δt.
- **Staging, switching and hysteresis.** Three points carry over to screech and impinging staging.
  - *Coexisting peaks may be switching* (Hussain & Zaman, p. 81). This is the coexistence-or-alternation question still open for impinging tones ([Ho 1981](ho-1981-dynamics-impinging-jet-feedback-phenomenon.md), p. 134–135; [Henderson 1993](henderson-1993-tones-choked-jet-impinging-flat-plates.md), p. 318) and for multimodal screech.
  - *Hysteresis depends on the flow class* (p. 81): present in jet–edge and hole tones, absent in cavities and wedges, where energy moves gradually between frequencies through a jump. In free screech, hysteresis is found only at C ↔ D, not at A1/A2 ([Powell 1992](powell-1992-oscillation-modes-choked-circular-jets.md), p. 2826, 2832), so screech staging behaves partly like each class. Why some loops are hysteretic and others not is unexplained in both literatures.
  - *Modes persist into the neighbouring stage* (Fig. 11, p. 83–84), as Powell's secondary screech tones ≈ 25 dB down and the B–D link do (Powell 1992). The authors' advice (look at the streamwise evolution of all spectral components through a jump, p. 82) translates directly into SPOD and BMD along the shear layer.
- **Onset and the gain criterion.** The minimum length L_m (p. 85–87) is the low-L cut-off seen in supersonic impinging jets (no tones below z/D ≈ 3.2 for the plug nozzle of [Powis 2016](powis-2016-impingement-tones-shock-instabilities-plug-nozzle.md)). Its two explanations, Powell's minimum gain and Woolley & Karamcheti's minimum integrated amplification, are the same split as the gain criterion that [Weightman et al. 2019](weightman-2019-nozzle-external-geometry-azimuthal-mode-impinging-jet.md) invoke but never quantify (topic page).
- **Frequency models with and without feedback.** The review's two model classes (an explicit feedback phase condition against maximum integrated amplification, p. 90–91) prefigure the debate on [screech-frequency-and-global-modes](../Topics/screech-frequency-and-global-modes.md) between the Powell loop and the absolute-instability saddle. Sarohia's and Woolley & Karamcheti's remark that even the amplification models need a prescribed separation–impingement phase (p. 90–91) is the 1979 form of the finding that the phase criterion and the saddle-point condition largely coincide. "Frequency yes, amplitude rarely" (p. 89) is still the consensus for screech (Raman 1998).
- **Models.** Bilanin & Covert's vortex sheet with downstream waves only (p. 91) is the ancestor of the vortex-sheet phase models that add the upstream guided wave (Jordan 2018; Jaunet 2019). Chan's eddy-viscosity energy model (p. 92) foreshadows eddy-viscosity linear operators. The prediction that full numerical solution would be needed for amplitude (p. 89) has been met by LES of impinging tones (Bogey 2017).
- **Supersonic jets without a plate.** Poldervaart's planar jet radiates where "a vortex interacts with a shock wave" (p. 69–70), an early picture of what is now shock leakage, and the feedback changes the visible shock-cell count (p. 70–74), consistent with the reflector effects reviewed in [Raman 1998](raman-1998-supersonic-jet-screech-review.md).

**Methods and data for the lab workflow (CLAUDE.md)**
- Every result reviewed comes from time-averaged point spectra, band-pass amplitudes, time-averaged phase maps and flow visualisation. None of them separates coexistence from switching, gives cycle-resolved phase or follows energy transfer between harmonics in space and time, and the authors ask for all three (p. 76, 81–82).
- **Time-resolved data (TR schlieren, microphones, TR-PIV):** SPOD at the tone and at the subharmonic gives the Fig. 6 phase map as a full field, and its k_x sign separates downstream and upstream waves. BMD along the shear layer is the modern form of the Fig. 9 analysis (fundamental–subharmonic–harmonic transfer, here with a third, non-harmonic peak). SPOD → reconstruction → Hilbert POD gives what the authors ask for: instantaneous phase between separation and impingement, the cycle-to-cycle jitter of Fig. 3, and whether two peaks at a stage jump coexist or alternate. Band-passed Hilbert envelopes of long microphone records are the cheapest test of switching.
- **Non-time-resolved data (PIV):** POD-pair phase gives phase-line maps like Fig. 6 and the spatial growth envelope of Fig. 11, but not jitter, dwell times or hysteresis dynamics.
- **Hysteresis needs sweep direction.** Fig. 10's loops appear only in up-and-down sweeps; a hysteresis claim needs both directions and dwell times at each set point.

**Modelling side and the PyFR staging LES**
- The A1/A2 continuation in `Simulations/PyFR_Staging_Continuation/` is exactly the experiment the review asks for at a stage jump. Record the lip-line spectra along x at each NPR step to see whether energy moves between the loops gradually (cavity and wedge type) or abruptly (jet–edge and hole-tone type). Track the subdominant loop's amplitude after the switch, as in Fig. 11, as a sign of the other branch. Step both up and down in NPR to test for a hysteresis loop like Fig. 10's.
- The phase-map test (Fig. 6) transfers directly to the LES: the phase of the A1 and A2 SPOD modes between the lip and the source shock gives each loop's N + p without assuming U_c.

**Rocket noise.** The jet–flap result, U⁶ against U⁷ scaling and about 20 dB more sound with the surface (p. 85), is an early measure of how impingement changes the source, relevant to pad and deflector noise models that treat impingement only by ending the potential core (topic page). The authors' expectation that an edge can organise even turbulent, high-Re flows (p. 83) sits against the absence of tones at launch-vehicle scale ([Lubert 2022](lubert-2022-supersonic-jet-noise-launch-vehicles-review.md)).
