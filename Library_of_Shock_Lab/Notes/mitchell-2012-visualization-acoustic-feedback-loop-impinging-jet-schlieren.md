## Summary
A short flow-visualisation paper that gives the first time-resolved images of the feedback loop of a supersonic impinging jet (p. 333–335). A 15 mm converging nozzle at NPR 3.2 fires at a flat plate 4d away. It is imaged with a Z-type schlieren system, using a PCO Dimax (2016 × 2016 px, 1 kfps, independent images) for spatial detail and a Shimadzu HPV-1 (312 × 260 px, 250–500 kfps) for sequences (p. 335). A composite of independent images shows the four accepted stages: an upstream wave reaching the lip, a shear-layer disturbance, a vortex ring, and new upstream waves (Fig. 3, p. 336–337). The time-resolved sequences show that the wave's arrival at the lip produces a short-wavelength (λ ≈ 0.05d), sinusoid-like train of density-gradient bands. The bands convect downstream and die out by x/d ≈ 0.3, and the embryonic vortex ring appears at their trailing edge at x/d ≈ 0.25 (p. 337–339). No slow shear-layer undulation at the loop period, which Ho & Nosseir's "collective interaction" requires, is seen. The authors favour Tam's view that the wave excites the shear layer's intrinsic instability, and they suggest that the ring's linear growth is hidden under the larger high-frequency transient (p. 339–340). There is no model, simulation, acoustic measurement or quantitative image analysis beyond normalised intensity profiles.

## Key findings
- **First time-resolved view of the impinging-jet loop** (p. 333–335, 337). Earlier work relied on mean or phase-locked ensemble data (Henderson et al. 2005), so the loop's legs had been inferred rather than seen (p. 334). Sequences at up to 1 Mfps allow individual structures to be tracked (p. 334–335).
- **The four stages** (Fig. 3, p. 336–337; NPR 3.2, z/d 4). The stages are: (a) an acoustic wave about to reach the nozzle exit, shown in both dρ/dr and dρ/dx; (b) the shear layer perturbed just after arrival, a sinusoid-like disturbance at the lip in dρ/dr (inset); (c) the perturbation grown into a large vortex ring; (d) new upstream acoustic waves.
  - The four images are **not from one time-resolved sequence**. They are representative independent images (p. 337), so the figure assumes a repeating cycle, as phase-locked imaging does.
  - In Fig. 3c the coherent structures sit at matching x on both sides of the jet (my reading), consistent with the authors' "vortex ring" (axisymmetric). No azimuthal measurement was made.
  - In Fig. 3d the labelled new fronts lie outside the jet boundary about two-thirds of the way to the plate (my reading). The paper does not locate where they are generated; it adopts the Henderson et al. (2005) chain of structure passage → shock motion → wall-jet fluctuation → upstream wave (p. 337).
- **Flange reflections are visible but assumed harmless** (p. 337). The nozzle's mounting flange reflects waves, which appear in the sequences, but "it is not believed" they affect the loop. This was not tested.
- **Vortex genesis at 250 kfps** (Fig. 4, p. 337–338). The sequence starts when a wave reaches the lip and shows every fifth frame (20 µs apart). The structure grows rapidly over a short axial distance and penetrates both the ambient fluid and the jet core as it convects.
  - The initial shear-layer perturbation (the dark region just ahead of the vortex) convects ahead of the rotating packet. So the vortex forms from the **tail** of the wave-induced perturbation, not its leading edge (p. 337).
- **No collective interaction at the loop period** (p. 337). The HPV-1 images cannot resolve the small initial vortices, so merging itself cannot be seen. But the shear-layer oscillation at the period of the upstream waves that Ho & Nosseir's theory predicts is absent. Instead, a sinusoid-like disturbance with a period "significantly shorter" than the acoustic waves' appears near the exit (p. 337, referring to "Fig. 2b", evidently Fig. 3b).
- **High-magnification sequence at 500 kfps** (Fig. 5, p. 337, 339). Every fifth frame is shown, 10 µs apart (frames A–H), with a 0.5d scale bar.
  - (A) The wave is about 12 µs from the lip, with the shear layer unperturbed. (B) The front is about to reach the lip. (C) Alternating bands of positive and negative density gradient appear along the shear layer.
  - From the stated 12 µs and 10 µs steps, the bands are already well developed about 8 µs after arrival (my derivation). The paper gives no tighter bound on the receptivity delay.
  - The bands convect downstream and have damped out by **x/d ≈ 0.3** (p. 337).
  - (E) A small dark spot, the precursor of the vortex ring, appears at the trailing edge of the initial instability, first visible at **x/d ≈ 0.25** (p. 337; also abstract, p. 333, and conclusions, p. 340). By G–H it is a large structure.
- **Intensity profiles along the shear-layer edge** (Fig. 6, p. 337–340; high-resolution dρ/dx images; caption says NPR 3). Intensity is sampled along a line on the outer edge of the shear layer, where the path-integration effect is smallest, from L/D = 0 at the lip to L/D = 1. It is normalised by the mean intensity.
  - (a) Before any wave arrives: random fluctuation about 1 (≈ 0.8–1.2, read from figure), with no structure.
  - (b) About 10 µs after a wave arrives: a strongly sinusoidal disturbance of **λ ≈ 0.05d** (p. 339). The first peak reaches ≈ 2.5 at L/D ≈ 0.03 and the oscillation fades by L/D ≈ 0.3 (read from figure).
  - (c) Later: the sinusoid is replaced by a single peak (≈ 2.2 at L/D ≈ 0.68–0.70, read from figure), the vortex ring.
  - In no image did the sinusoidal variation persist past **l/d ≈ 0.3**, matching Fig. 5 (p. 339).
- **Interpretation** (p. 339–340).
  - The wave arrival makes a "highly spatially transient" shear-layer disturbance, at whose trailing edge a large coherent structure appears (p. 339).
  - The slow sinusoidal shear-layer oscillation that collective interaction needs is not seen. The optics cannot say whether vortex merging or "collectivisation" happens at all (p. 339).
  - The authors lean on Tam (1995): the wave excites the intrinsic instability of the thin, most receptive shear layer at the lip, and the most unstable wavelength then grows. They also cite Manning (1999), whose supersonic shear-layer simulations show linear exponential growth followed by nonlinear saturation as vortices form (p. 339).
  - The high-frequency transient dies out at about the distance where the incipient ring appears. The authors' reading is that the ring's linear growth is obscured by the larger high-frequency perturbation and that the ring becomes visible as viscous forces damp the transient. They say a role for the transient in vortex genesis "cannot be categorically ruled out" (p. 339).
  - The conclusions say the images support the feedback model of Henderson et al. (2005) and that a more quantitative analysis was under way (p. 339–340).

## Setup / method
- **Facility** (Fig. 2, p. 335; dimensions read from a 300 dpi render). Continuous compressed air through a settling chamber with a honeycomb and several screens, then a converging nozzle. NPR is measured by a pressure transducer in the plenum (p. 335). Supply temperature, transducer type and accuracy are not stated.
- **Nozzle** (Fig. 2, Detail A). Exit diameter d = 15 mm. The contraction contour is drawn dashed with R 62 mm. The outside is conical, rising 50 mm from the flange to the exit face. The exit face's outer diameter reads 20 mm, i.e. a 2.5 mm lip, t/d ≈ 0.17 (my reading of the labels "20" and "15"; check). Contraction ratio, exit boundary layer and surface finish are not given.
  - This differs from the group's later 15 mm nozzles: t/D 0.33 with a 67.15 mm contraction radius in [Edgington-Mitchell 2014](edgington-mitchell-2014-coherent-structure-helical-screech-mode.md) (p. 825), and t/D 0.1, 0.33 and 7 in [Weightman et al. 2019](weightman-2019-nozzle-external-geometry-azimuthal-mode-impinging-jet.md). Do not assume it is the same nozzle.
- **Plate.** Aluminium, normal to the jet, 300 mm wide in Fig. 2 (D_plate/d = 20, a large plate in Henderson & Powell's sense; my ratio). Stand-off z/d = 4 for all results (p. 335, 337).
- **Conditions.** NPR 3.2, z/d 4 (Figs 1, 3–5); the Fig. 6 caption says NPR 3, which may be a typo or a second condition. M_j, U_j, Re and the tone frequency are not stated, and there are no acoustic measurements.
  - My isentropic estimates: M_j ≈ 1.40 at NPR 3.2 (1.36 at 3.0); with T₀ ≈ 293 K, U_j ≈ 407 m s⁻¹.
- **Schlieren** (p. 335). Toepler Z-type, mirrors of 2032 mm effective focal length. The knife edge at the second mirror's focus is set for either dρ/dr or dρ/dx; both are shown.
  - *High spatial resolution:* PCO Dimax, 2016 × 2016 px at 1 kfps, lit by a Phlatlight CBT-120 green LED. At this rate the images are independent (p. 335). Pulse length or exposure, and the number of images, are not stated.
  - *Time-resolved:* Shimadzu HPV-1, 312 × 260 px, up to 1 Mfps, lit by a Metz Mecablitz photographic flash (p. 335). The sequences shown are at 250 kfps (Fig. 4) and 500 kfps (Fig. 5). Exposure, sequence length and number of sequences are not stated. The HPV-1 holds at most 102 frames per sequence ([Weightman 2019](weightman-2019-sound-production-impinging-supersonic-jets.md), from that thesis, not this paper).
- **Analysis.** Entirely qualitative inspection of single frames and short sequences, plus one quantitative device: intensity along a line on the outer shear-layer edge, normalised by the average intensity (Fig. 6). The paper does not say whether the average is over the line, the image or the ensemble. No statistics, spectra, POD, correlation, phase averaging or convection-speed measurement are reported.
  - *Data dependence:* the stage composite (Fig. 3) and the line profiles (Fig. 6) use independent images and so need no time resolution; the sequence (bands → ring) needs ≥ 250 kfps bursts; the arrival-to-response timing needs the 500 kfps sequence.
- **Modelling side.** None. Ho & Nosseir's collective interaction (Ho & Huang's forced mixing layer, applied to impinging jets), Tam's 1995 lip-receptivity picture and Manning's 1999 supersonic shear-layer simulations (linear growth, then nonlinear saturation into vortices) are cited only in words (p. 337, 339). No stability calculation, base flow or simulation is made.

## Key equations
The paper has no equations. For context, my own relations (not in the paper):
- Isentropic fully expanded Mach number: $M_j=\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\gamma-1}\big(\mathrm{NPR}^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma}-1\big)}$, γ = 1.4, which gives 1.40 at NPR 3.2 and 1.36 at 3.0.
- Strouhal number of the lip bands if they are convected waves: $St_j=\dfrac{fd}{U_j}=\dfrac{U_c/U_j}{\lambda/d}$. With λ ≈ 0.05d (p. 339) and an assumed U_c ≈ 0.5–0.6U_j this gives St_j ≈ 10–12. That is about 25–50 times the group's impinging tones at St ≈ 0.25–0.39 (Weightman et al. 2019), in line with the paper's "significantly shorter" period (p. 337). U_c near the lip is not measured here.

## Relevance to this project
This is the earliest Shock Lab group paper in the library on impinging-jet resonance and the first group use of ultra-high-speed schlieren, so it starts the thread that runs through [Weightman et al. 2017](weightman-2017-phase-lag-supersonic-jet-impingement.md), the user's thesis ([Weightman 2019](weightman-2019-sound-production-impinging-supersonic-jets.md)), [Weightman et al. 2019](weightman-2019-nozzle-external-geometry-azimuthal-mode-impinging-jet.md), [Karami 2020](karami-2020-acoustic-hydrodynamic-waves-underexpanded-impinging-jets.md) and [Edgington-Mitchell 2025](edgington-mitchell-2025-vortices-shocks-nonlinear-waves-impinging-jet-resonance.md). Its 2032 mm Z-type system is the one later group papers cite ([Edgington-Mitchell 2014](edgington-mitchell-2014-coherent-structure-helical-screech-mode.md), p. 826; [Edgington-Mitchell 2021](edgington-mitchell-2021-generation-screech-tones-shock-leakage.md)). It bears directly on the CLAUDE.md research area of impinging supersonic jets and feedback, studied with time-resolved schlieren.

**How it fits and what has changed since**
- **Receptivity delay.** The later papers rely on this one for a negligible lip delay: "at most 2 µs", the shear-layer perturbation following the wave's arrival "in the next frame" ([Weightman 2017](weightman-2017-phase-lag-supersonic-jet-impingement.md), p. 2; [Weightman 2019](weightman-2019-sound-production-impinging-supersonic-jets.md), p. 126). The printed paper shows frames 10 µs apart and states no delay. From its own numbers the bands are present within about 8 µs of arrival (Fig. 5, p. 337, my derivation). The 2 µs figure is the 500 kfps frame interval and presumably comes from the full sequence or Movie 2. Either bound is small against a loop period of ≈ 90–150 µs (St 0.25–0.4 with my U_j), so the conclusion that the phase lag belongs to the source survives. But the topic page's "within about 2 µs" should be attributed to Weightman 2017's reading, not to the printed figures.
- **Collective interaction.** This is the first experimental evidence against [Ho 1981](ho-1981-dynamics-impinging-jet-feedback-phenomenon.md)'s collective interaction in supersonic impinging jets: there is no slow undulation at the loop period (p. 337, 339). [Karami 2020](karami-2020-acoustic-hydrodynamic-waves-underexpanded-impinging-jets.md) reaches the same conclusion from LES of the thesis case (p. 3, 12–13, 21) and adds a mechanism: spatial growth on a rapidly evolving mean flow, with the local most-unstable St falling from ≈ 4 at x = 0.1d to ≈ 0.5 by 0.9d (p. 21–23), and little energy upstream of x ≈ 0.3–0.4d in the LES energy-norm spectra (p. 24). That fits this paper's sequence (high-frequency bands to x/d ≈ 0.3, ring from 0.25d) closely. Karami's companion receptivity LES (as cited in Edgington-Mitchell 2025, p. 2) found that near-impulsive lip forcing gives a broadband high-frequency response that becomes one large vortex within 1d, which is a numerical version of Figs 5–6.
  - *My reading:* the paper attributes the bands' disappearance to viscous damping (p. 339). A more natural explanation is that the bands are the thin lip layer's own high-St response to an impulsive front, and they stop growing as the layer thickens and its unstable band moves to lower St (Karami's Fig. 18 trend). The upstream fronts are weak shocks at birth ([Edgington-Mitchell 2021](edgington-mitchell-2021-generation-screech-tones-shock-leakage.md), p. 3–4, 18–19; Edgington-Mitchell 2025, p. 10–13), so broadband forcing is expected. On this picture the loop-frequency component is present from the start but buried, as the authors suggest.
  - *A scale check (my estimate):* λ ≈ 0.05d at U_c ≈ 0.5–0.6U_j means St_j ≈ 10–12, above the unstable band of Karami's LES mean at x = 0.1d (up to St ≈ 7–7.5, Karami note), whose inflow layer is δ_in = 0.04d. Either the real lip layer is much thinner than that LES inflow (as the thesis assumes for the high-contraction nozzles, Weightman 2019, p. 89), or the bands convect more slowly. Both would matter for LES inflow choices.
- **Vortex triggered downstream of the lip.** The ring precursor first appears at x/d ≈ 0.25 (p. 337). [Mercier 2017](mercier-2017-screech-feedback-loop-underexpanded-round-jets.md) uses this to explain the positive excess in its free-jet screech loop phase (that note, p. 224). That carries an impinging-jet observation over to screech; it rests on one impinging condition here.
- **Flange reflections.** The paper assumes the reflections off the mounting flange do not affect the loop (p. 337). [Weightman et al. 2019](weightman-2019-nozzle-external-geometry-azimuthal-mode-impinging-jet.md) later found that, in the same group's rig, reflection off the nozzle's outer base is *necessary* for the thin-lip helical tone (p. 434–441). That outcome contradicts the assumption in general, though not necessarily for this nozzle and condition.
- **Sources.** The paper takes the source from Henderson et al. 2005 (wall-jet fluctuation driven by shock motion; [Henderson 2002](henderson-2002-flow-structure-supersonic-impinging-jets.md) is that work's conference precursor). It does not image the source itself. The later group papers identify stand-off-shock leakage, the wall-jet shocklet and vortex–wall sound (Weightman 2017; Weightman 2019; Edgington-Mitchell 2025).
- **Elliptical jets.** The same HPV-1 approach, sequences of a lip-triggered vortex, reappears in [Edgington-Mitchell 2015](edgington-mitchell-2015-multimodal-instability-weakly-underexpanded-elliptic-jet.md) (Fig. 5, p. 2742) for a free elliptical jet.

**Methods and data for the lab workflow (CLAUDE.md)**
- **Short bursts suit visualisation, not decomposition.** 102-frame HPV-1 sequences show single events but cannot support SPOD, BMD or HPOD; frequency, convection speed and azimuthal mode are not measured at all here. The long-record SA-Z data of the later group papers (100–200 kfps, 10⁴–10⁵ frames) can, but their Nyquist limit (St_j ≈ 2.8 at 150 kfps for d = 15 mm and U_j ≈ 407 m s⁻¹, my arithmetic) is far below the St_j ≈ 10 lip bands. The lip transient therefore stays a burst-camera problem.
- **A receptivity impulse response from bursts** (my suggestion). Detect each upstream front's arrival at the lip (from near-lip intensity, or a microphone flush with the nozzle face). Then ensemble-average the following frames conditioned on arrival time, and on the front's strength or shape (sharp or diffuse; Edgington-Mitchell 2025, p. 10–13). The result would be an empirical receptivity transfer function, i.e. the band amplitude and the ring's emergence point against front strength. That is the quantity the Weightman 2017 phase-lag argument and any loop-gain model need.
- **Independent high-resolution images** (PCO Dimax) suit statistics, two-point correlation and POD as in the group's later work. Here they serve only for the Fig. 3 composite and the Fig. 6 profiles. The edge-line profile is a useful practice: sample where the line-of-sight integration is weakest (p. 337).
- **Long records for the loop itself.** SPOD at the tone with the k_x sign separating the up- and downstream waves, then reconstruction and Hilbert POD, would give cycle-to-cycle amplitude and phase of the loop near the lip. This could test whether the ring's strength depends on the arriving front, which this paper's single sequences cannot. Intermittency and mode switching are not addressed (one condition only).

**Modelling side and the PyFR staging LES**
- Any LES of a screeching or impinging jet must resolve the lip response. Here it is a λ ≈ 0.05d (≈ 0.75 mm) transient over the first 0.3d, set by the exit boundary layer. For the PyFR A1/A2 staging runs (`Simulations/PyFR_Staging_Continuation/`), check that the near-lip resolution and the inflow layer thickness can carry such a transient, or show that the loop gain does not depend on it. Karami 2020 needed fine lip-edge resolution to capture receptivity (p. 6).
- The paper's open question, whether the high-frequency transient feeds vortex genesis or only masks it (p. 339), is best settled numerically. Impulsive lip forcing of a non-parallel linear model (PSE/OWNS, or a linearised Navier–Stokes impulse response) on a measured near-lip mean flow would show whether the loop-frequency wavepacket is present from the first diameter.

**Rocket noise.** Only indirect. The lab loop depends on a thin, receptive lip shear layer and a normal plate. Neither is present for full-scale plumes on inclined deflectors, where no tones are found ([Lubert 2022](lubert-2022-supersonic-jet-noise-launch-vehicles-review.md), p. 16–20).
