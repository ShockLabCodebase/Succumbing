# Impinging jets and edge tones

_Last updated: 2026-10-20 · 8 papers_

## Scope
Self-sustained tones from jets that meet a surface: supersonic jets on plates, cylinders and hemispheres; subsonic jets grazing a plate edge (jet–edge or jet–flap tones); and how the nozzle exterior and other reflecting surfaces close such loops. It also covers impingement at launch-vehicle scale, where the question is why the laboratory tones disappear. Free-jet screech is on [screech](screech.md); vortex-sheet dispersion relations, saddle points and guided-jet-wave theory on [linear-models](linear-models.md); broadband pad and deflector noise on [rocket-noise-models](rocket-noise-models.md) and [rocket-noise-measurements](rocket-noise-measurements.md); POD-pair practice on [modal-decomposition](modal-decomposition.md). Tone frequencies are collected in `data/impinging_tones.csv` (see [data/README](../data/README.md)).

## Current understanding
1. **Every tone here is a Powell-type feedback loop**: a KH wavepacket goes downstream, the surface region launches an upstream wave, and receptivity at the lip closes the loop. A phase condition sets the frequency, either Powell's timing equation ([Henderson 2002](../notes/henderson-2002-flow-structure-supersonic-impinging-jets.md), eq. 1, p. 5; [Weightman 2019](../notes/weightman-2019-sound-production-impinging-supersonic-jets.md), eq. 1.1, p. 5) or its dispersive form Δk_r L + φ = 2nπ ([Jordan 2018](../notes/jordan-2018-jet-flap-interaction-tones.md), p. 7–9; [Stavropoulos 2026](../notes/stavropoulos-2026-jet-edge-interaction.md), p. 5).
2. **What carries the upstream wave depends on where it is born.** Sound generated inside the core can only travel upstream as a guided-jet (or duct-like) mode, within that mode's band; sound generated outside travels as a free-stream wave ([Nogueira 2024](../notes/nogueira-2024-guided-jet-waves.md), p. 31–33; [Edgington-Mitchell 2025](../notes/edgington-mitchell-2025-vortices-shocks-nonlinear-waves-impinging-jet-resonance.md), p. 3–4, 15–17). Subsonic jet–edge tones are closed by guided modes (Jordan 2018, p. 4–10; Stavropoulos 2026, p. 8–18); the group's supersonic impinging jets are often closed by external waves (Weightman 2019, p. 75, 81, 89).
3. **Supersonic impinging jets have several sources, all driven by the same KH vortex**: stand-off-shock leakage, a transient wall-jet shocklet (29–40 µs delay), vortex–wall sound in the core, and possibly the oscillating wall-jet boundary. Two can act within one cycle (Edgington-Mitchell 2025, p. 14–24; Weightman 2019, p. 118–134; Henderson 2002, p. 4–6). Leakage works as in free-jet screech, at a saddle point between vortices once the vortex has passed, with vortex strength setting where ([Edgington-Mitchell 2021](../notes/edgington-mitchell-2021-generation-screech-tones-shock-leakage.md), p. 23–30; Edgington-Mitchell 2025, p. 18).
4. **The upstream waves are weak shocks at birth** (M_s ≈ 1.07 from a PIV velocity jump), as free-jet leakage fronts are (Edgington-Mitchell 2021, p. 3–4, 18–19); sharp and diffuse forms alternate from cycle to cycle, and pairs can merge (Edgington-Mitchell 2025, p. 10–13).
5. **The nozzle exterior is a boundary condition of the loop**: changing only the lip switches an NPR 3.4, z/D 5 jet from helical to toroidal, and foam on the outer base removes the helical tone (Weightman 2019, p. 70–81).
6. **The band in which the upstream wave exists bounds the tones** of subsonic jet–edge flows, including their high-St cut-offs and switches between loops (Jordan 2018, p. 10–14; Stavropoulos 2026, p. 15–18), using the waves of [Towne 2017](../notes/towne-2017-acoustic-resonance-potential-core-subsonic-jets.md) (p. 31–33).
7. **Frequency selection can be linear or nonlinear**: several inharmonic, uncoupled tones can give way abruptly to one saturated oscillator (Jordan 2018, p. 5; Stavropoulos 2026, p. 8–15).
8. **No impingement tones are seen at launch-vehicle scale**; pad noise is broadband, dominated by ground-reflected Mach waves and flame-duct sources ([Lubert 2022](../notes/lubert-2022-supersonic-jet-noise-launch-vehicles-review.md), p. 16–20; [Tsutsumi 2015](../notes/tsutsumi-2015-epsilon-liftoff-acoustics.md), p. 13–16; [Panda 2013](../notes/panda-2013-noise-sources-rocket-test-firings-launch-phased-array.md), p. 17).

## The feedback equation and the phase lag
Henderson et al. built the cycle of an NPR 4.03 jet on a large plate (h/d = 2.02, axisymmetric L1 tone ≈ 10 kHz) from microphone-locked shadowgraphs and phase-binned 10 Hz PIV (p. 2–5). A three-leg Powell criterion gave N = 4, p ≈ +0.4, but the first leg's convection Mach number (0.7–0.75) was borrowed because disturbances upstream of the Mach disk could not be tracked (p. 5–6).

Weightman's thesis shows how loosely the timing equation constrains the loop. With p = 0 and measured U_c/U_j = 0.44–0.48, the direct, inner-base and outer-base paths each reproduce both tone families (St ≈ 0.25 and 0.39) for some integer N, and moving the reflection point across the outer base shifts St from 0.34 to 0.42 (p. 82–85). The gain criterion that could choose a path (p. 6) is never quantified. Chapter 7 then measured every term for a cylinder case (NPR 3.6, z/D 3.5): N = 4, u_c(x) from the POD phase average, U_s = 331 m/s after an entrainment correction, and a delay Δt = 29.4 µs between shocklet inception and emission (p ≈ −0.24). The prediction is 8230 Hz against 8300 Hz measured (−0.8%), against +5.4% with Δt = 0; on a hemisphere Δt = 40 µs, p = −0.49 (p. 129–134).

This support for the shocklet is weaker than it looks: one phase-averaged case cannot give the cycle-to-cycle spread of Δt (p. 133). The no-delay alternative is rejected because it needs a mean u_c ≈ 0.43 U_j, yet the measured local u_c spans 0.42–0.52 U_j and is valid only over 0.75 < x/D < 2.6 (p. 107, 131–134), so a 10% bias in u_c would close the gap without a delay. And the cylinder's normalised values use U_j = 460.8 m/s (= M_j U_E) rather than the isentropic ≈ 420 m/s (thesis note, verify item 3). Published phase lags, +0.4 (Henderson), 0 (Gojon et al. 2016), −0.4 (Krothapalli et al. 1999; both as cited in Weightman 2019, p. 126) and −0.49, use different loop definitions, so p alone is not comparable. Nor can a fitted p yet show which source is active: the thesis times leakage (p ≈ 0) from vortex arrival (p. 122) and the shocklet from its inception (p. 129–134), but leakage begins only after the vortex has passed (Edgington-Mitchell 2025, p. 18), once the saddle point behind it reaches the shock (Edgington-Mitchell 2021, p. 26). Timed from the vortex core, that wait is of order half a cycle, comparable to the shocklet's delay (my estimate).

For subsonic edges the Pprime group replaced Powell's constant speeds with vortex-sheet wavenumbers: R₁R₂e^{iΔkL} = 1, in phase only, with φ = 0 or π (Jordan 2018, p. 7–9). The dispersive upstream wave captures the "frequency squeezing" that the classical model (KH down, free-stream sound up) misses (p. 4–5, 9). Keeping |R₁R₂| (0.002–0.008) bounds the tone-free low-St region but is fragile near saddle points (p. 15–16, 20–24). The same criterion is the screech model of [Mancinelli 2019](../notes/mancinelli-2019-screech-prediction-upstream-modes.md), which notes that a plate would simply replace the shock cell as the downstream reflector (p. 2).

## What carries the upstream wave
**Guided-mode closure.** For an M 0.6–1.0 jet grazing an edge at L/D = 2–4, KH paired with the upstream jet modes k_TH⁻ (M ≤ 0.82) and k_p⁻ (0.82 < M < 1) reproduces the tone spacing and Mach trend that free-stream closure cannot (Jordan 2018, p. 4–10). These are the waves trapped in the potential core of a free M 0.9 jet, where the vortex-sheet model allows resonance only for 0.82 < M < 1 (Towne 2017, p. 14–19, 31–33). The cut-offs are physical: above St ≈ 1 at M ≤ 0.82 the k_TH⁻ wave is confined to the core and transmitted into the nozzle rather than reflected, and above 0.82 the k_p⁻ branch ends at the S2 saddle; the two lines bound the observed tones (Jordan 2018, p. 10–14).

**External closure.** In Weightman's jets (NPR 3.4, z/D 5, D = 15 mm) foam over the outer base removes the thin-lip helical tone, foam over the inner base leaves it, and an infinite foam cap kills the resonance (p. 74–82). The thesis concludes that the loop closes only by reflection off the outer base, by freely propagating external waves, in contrast to the guided-mode closure of Gojon and Bogey's LES (p. 75, 81, 89). Jordan et al. cite this work as evidence that free-stream sound can close impinging loops (Jordan 2018, p. 2).

**Reconciliation.** Nogueira et al. treat the guided mode as a scattering problem: a plane wave arriving from outside and travelling directly upstream is cancelled at the shear layer, while sound generated inside is totally reflected over a band (Nogueira 2024, p. 14–22). Screech always needs the mode because its KH–shock source lies inside the sonic line; impingement needs it only when the source is in the core (p. 31–33). Edgington-Mitchell et al. (2025) supply the experiments: vortex–wall sound peaks on the axis, so it is an in-core source and must pass the guided-mode filter, which explains why guided-mode models work for subsonic and some supersonic impinging jets; the shocklet and stand-off-shock leakage sit outside the core (p. 8, 14–17). In some Bogey and Gojon LES cases the pressure inside and outside the jet is almost uncorrelated, which rules out the guided mode there (p. 8). The "two schools" were describing different mechanisms, which can coexist within one cycle (p. 25).

Two caveats remain. Nogueira's argument concerns whether an external wave can *create* a guided mode, whereas external closure works by direct receptivity at the lip, so it constrains in-core sources without proving external closure. And the foam tests show the external path is *necessary* for the thin-lip tone, but the thesis PIV has never been split by streamwise wavenumber (as in [Edgington-Mitchell 2018](../notes/edgington-mitchell-2018-upstream-acoustic-modes-screech-closure.md)) to look for a guided component too.

## Where the upstream wave is generated
Henderson et al. describe an axially oscillating Mach disk that moves recirculation zones between stand-off shock and plate, an annular shock that moves and collapses, and a wall jet oscillating strongly at r ≈ 2.6R (≈ 1.3d), proposed as an annular simple source (p. 3–6). At h/d = 3.00, in a zone of silence, a second stand-off shock forms and the recirculation survives without a tone, so recirculation alone does not make tones (p. 4).

Weightman added two mechanisms. A streamwise–temporal Fourier split of 500 kfps schlieren shows an upstream wave emitted at the stand-off shock, and ray tracing on POD phase-reconstructed PIV puts leakage there with no delay, matching the schlieren in location and timing (p. 109–122). The ray tracing is qualitative (scale separation violated at convective Mach ≈ 1, c = 340 m/s, no directivity or amplitude), and leakage depends strongly on vortex amplitude (none at Ã = 0.2, sideline at 0.7), which the phase reconstruction smooths (p. 112–117, 162). The second mechanism is the shocklet, a transient wall-jet shock that forms as a vortex passes, tilts and launches the returning wave at (x/D, y/D) = (3.78, 1.43), close to Henderson's 1.3D source (p. 129–136).

Edgington-Mitchell et al. (2021) published the leakage method for free jets. Their k < 0-filtered 1 Mfps schlieren of screeching A2, B and twin jets shows the shock tilting upstream as a vortex arrives and a small arc of its tip separating as one sharp front (p. 13–19). Ray tracing (planar; constant c, as temperature modelled from PIV worsened shock artefacts; p. 11–13) puts the first leak at the 3rd or 4th cell, at a saddle point rather than the vorticity peak: a ray meeting a vortex is turned back into the core (p. 19–28). Rescaling the reconstruction to r̄ ± 2σ of the POD-pair amplitude moves the first leak at NPR 2.25 from about the 3rd cell (strong vortices) to the 6th (weak), spanning the 3rd–5th seen in schlieren (p. 28–30), a measured-field counterpart of the thesis's Stuart-vortex sweep. The impinging version is still unpublished (Edgington-Mitchell 2025, p. 17).

Edgington-Mitchell et al. (2025) widened the evidence to four nozzles (round and AR 2 rectangular, converging and C-D), three surface shapes and NPR 2.5–6 (p. 5–7). The shocklet appears to close the loop consistently when NPR ≥ 4.5 and z/D ≤ 4.0, and is not visible for NPR ≤ 3.0 in most datasets, perhaps because the wall jet hides it (p. 15–17). Leakage shows a split front, not seen in screeching jets, as the stronger vortices turn part of it back into the core (p. 18); Henderson's source is hypothesised to produce diffuse waves that coalesce (p. 23–24). When one vortex makes two strong waves, usually only one reaches the lip with useful strength, so some measured tonal components are by-products (p. 24–25). No clean map of mechanism against NPR, z/D or azimuthal mode exists (p. 12–13, 25).

The same paper weakens two thesis inferences. An in-core shock from vortex–wall impact would displace the stand-off shock and look like leakage in schlieren, so the thin-lip leakage identification is plausible but not unique (p. 18). And the thesis's quantitative shocklet test was at NPR 3.6, below the regime where the shocklet is found to close the loop consistently.

At rocket scale, LES of a cold M_e 1.8–2.0 jet on a 45° plate shows five wave types: free-jet and wall-jet Mach waves, vortex–plate-shock waves, BBSAN-like tail-shock waves and separation-shock waves (Lubert 2022, p. 17–18, not tagged). The last three are inclined-plate relatives of leakage and the shocklet, but none is a tone.

## Mode selection: lip, surface and azimuthal mode
At NPR 3.4, z/D 5 the mean flow barely changes with the lip, but the tones do: the thin lip (t/D 0.1) gives one helical tone at St 0.255; the infinite lip (t/D 7) a toroidal tone at 0.385, a helical one at 0.241 and their difference at 0.144 (Weightman 2019, p. 68–72). The intermediate lip (t/D 0.33) should entrain like the thin lip but behaves like the infinite one (0.383, 0.253), so entrainment is rejected in favour of the acoustic path (p. 73–79), though the relevant entrainment may lie closer to the exit than the PIV resolves (p. 74). Nearly every configuration gives St ≈ 0.25 or 0.39, assumed but not shown to be the base flow's most unstable frequencies (p. 81, 86). The switch is not universal: at NPR 3.0, z/D 3.5 both lips give a toroidal pair, and at NPR 4.2, z/D 2 neither gives a dominant mode (p. 147–155).

Free-jet screech responds differently, with lip thickness changing staging and the Mach range of screech, but Ponton's proposed mechanism, upstream sound reflecting off the larger exit face, is the same acoustic-path idea ([Ponton 1992](../notes/ponton-1992-nozzle-exit-lip-thickness-plume-resonance.md), p. 536–548, not tagged). Upstream reflectors detune screech ([Raman 1998](../notes/raman-1998-supersonic-jet-screech-review.md), p. 75–78, not tagged), and an upstream plate on a Monash rig altered free-jet staging ([Edgington-Mitchell 2022](../notes/edgington-mitchell-2022-unifying-theory-jet-screech.md), p. 6, 16, not tagged).

Surface shape matters too. A convex cylinder fixes the flapping axis, so repeated PIV catches the same instability (St 0.29, 42% of the energy in POD modes 1–2; Weightman 2019, p. 29–30); a hemisphere shows the shocklet best; every phenomenon also appears, less clearly, on flat plates (Edgington-Mitchell 2025, p. 6–7). Henderson's axisymmetric L1 tones come from a 0.5d lip (p. 2–3), consistent with thick lips favouring toroidal modes.

One coincidence has not been followed up: the thin-lip impinging tone (St 0.255 at NPR 3.4) is close to free-jet helical C-mode screech at NPR 3.40 in the same group's D = 15 mm nozzle family (St 0.26, [Edgington-Mitchell 2020](../notes/edgington-mitchell-2020-waves-in-screeching-jets.md), not tagged, but 0.30 for the same PIV case in Edgington-Mitchell 2021, p. 6). Whether the helical impinging tone is a plate-modified screech loop has not been tested.

## Subsonic jet–edge tones: linear and nonlinear selection
Jordan et al. saw weak multi-peak resonance and, in some Mach ranges, a single high-Q oscillator with its harmonic and suppressed broadband (e.g. St ≈ 1.25 at M ≈ 0.78, L = 2D), with wavelets showing dominance, slow or fast competition, or coexistence of neighbouring cycles (p. 5, 16–17). Stavropoulos et al. mapped this in steps of 0.01 in M_j and R/D, using bicoherence with a threshold (b > 0.35) set from a tone-free case (p. 2–4). Linear selection (several inharmonic tones, zero bicoherence) gives way to nonlinear selection (one saturated tone with harmonics and passive triads) only for R/D ≲ 0.57 and 0.56 ≲ M_j ≲ 0.87 (p. 8–12). The jump at M_j 0.87–0.88 is two orders of magnitude within ΔM_j = 0.01, without hysteresis, and the nonlinear fundamental lies on the same model line as a linear tone, so the oscillator is an amplified linear loop (p. 9, 13–15). Between M_j 0.82 and 0.86 the dominant tone switches from a k⁻_d loop (St ≈ 0.52) to a k⁻_p loop (St ≈ 0.46); a duct-model reflection ratio and k⁻_p's wider radial support are offered for why k⁻_p wins, though the authors say a non-parallel model is needed (p. 15–18, 23–25).

## Impingement at launch-vehicle scale
An Epsilon boom microphone with the nozzle 8.7 D_e above the pad shows no tone, and Lubert et al. offer only masking by mixing noise and pad geometry as reasons (Lubert 2022, p. 16–17, not tagged). The 1/42 Epsilon campaign (hot, overexpanded M_e 3.7, 14° deflector) found the worst fairing level (149.1 dB at H/D_e = 14) came from free-jet Mach waves reflected off the ground in the open flame path, with impingement waves hard to see in the LES (Tsutsumi 2015, p. 11–16). At Wallops the water-quenched flame-duct exit dominated an Antares static fire, without tones (Panda 2013, p. 17, not tagged). Vega pad CAA shows reflected waves reaching the nozzle lip, so feedback is possible but untested ([Casalino 2009](../notes/casalino-2009-hybrid-empirical-caa-rocket-noise.md), p. 14–15, not tagged). Empirical models treat impingement only by ending the potential core at the deflector, which helps against Saturn V ([Haynes 2009](../notes/haynes-2009-sp-8072-dsm-ii-ares-i-lift-off.md), p. 10–12, not tagged) but overpredicts Ares I ([Ranow 2021](../notes/ranow-2021-acoustic-prediction-rocket-flame-deflector.md), p. 41–44, not tagged). Impingement on terrain removes high-frequency content and skewness ([Gee 2012](../notes/gee-2012-reusable-srm-noise-spectral-statistical.md), p. 10–11, not tagged).

## Disagreements and open questions
**Guided or free-stream closure in a given supersonic impinging jet.** Settled in principle by source location, but never tested on one dataset. A streamwise-wavenumber split of the thesis POD pairs, the coherence and phase continuity of the tone across the shear layer (Edgington-Mitchell 2025, p. 8), and placing each tone against the guided-mode band (Nogueira 2024, eqs. 4.7–4.8, or a finite-thickness model) would decide it.

**Leakage or vortex–wall impact at the stand-off shock.** Cases with the stand-off shock well clear of the wall, ray tracing with a measured sound-speed field and amplitude-preserving reconstructions (r̄ ± 2σ rescaling is a first step; Edgington-Mitchell 2021, p. 28–30), or LES with surface-source attribution would separate them, with free-jet leakage (p. 13–19) as the reference signature.

**What triggers leakage.** A sufficiently positive sonic-line angle "rather than the saddle point" (Weightman 2019, p. 163), or the saddle point (Edgington-Mitchell 2021, p. 26–27)? The thesis takes the sonic-line tilt at the saddle (p. 114–115), so these may be one geometry; neither gives a general criterion combining incidence angle and local vorticity (Edgington-Mitchell 2021, p. 26–28).

**The phase lag.** Values from +0.4 to −0.49 use different loop definitions and reference events, and the shocklet's 0.8% agreement rests on one case. A common loop definition and reference event (vortex core, saddle point or shocklet inception), cycle-resolved Δt from time-resolved imaging, and a sensitivity analysis of the predicted f to u_c would let p separate leakage from the shocklet.

**Where the shocklet matters.** It closes the loop consistently at NPR ≥ 4.5, z/D ≤ 4.0, while the thesis delay test is at NPR 3.6. High-frame-rate imaging on a hemisphere at NPR 3.4–3.6 would show whether it also closes the loop there or the agreement is partly coincidental.

**What fixes the recurring St ≈ 0.25 and 0.39.** Stability or resolvent analysis of the PIV mean flows, as done for free jets in Edgington-Mitchell 2020, and free and impinging spectra at the same NPR and lip would test the "most unstable frequency" assumption and the link to C-mode screech.

**Which upstream wave closes jet–edge loops, and with what phase.** Jordan 2018 found that k_d⁻ loops "did not match the data" and needed φ = 0 for some cases and π for others (p. 9). Stavropoulos 2026, with the same facility, nozzle and overlapping geometry (L/D = 2, R/D = 0.5) but a thicker chamfered plate, closes higher tones at M_j 0.82–0.89 with k⁻_d (p = 4–9) and uses φ = 0 almost everywhere (p. 13–18). With integer p up to 9 and φ free, the criterion can fit almost any tone, and the m = 1 tones are unexplained. An azimuthal array and a non-parallel model with nozzle and edge, giving R₁ and R₂, would settle both points.

**Coexistence or switching of toroidal and helical modes** for the thick lips cannot be told from PIV POD or averaged spectra (Weightman 2019, p. 33, 70, 119). Long-record time-resolved schlieren with Hilbert POD, and BMD of 0.144 = 0.385 − 0.241, would.

**Why full-scale pads show no tones.** Masking is a conjecture. The laboratory work suggests more specific candidates: inclined deflectors have no normal stand-off shock, nozzle heights of 8 D_e or more, the shocklet's need for a transonic wall jet, and the dependence of the guided-mode band on M_j and temperature. Hot, overexpanded subscale jets on normal and inclined plates at 2–10 D_e, with time-resolved schlieren and bicoherence on near-pad microphones, would discriminate.

## Methods and data
- **Point acoustics (time-resolved):** long records (20–30 s at 200 kHz) over fine Mach sweeps, analysed with PSD maps, thresholded bicoherence and bump-wavelet scalograms (μ = 5, σ = 0.6), are cheap and decisive for mapping regimes, but cannot resolve azimuthal mode (Jordan 2018, p. 3; Stavropoulos 2026, p. 3–4).
- **Non-time-resolved PIV:** phase from a microphone reference (Henderson 2002, p. 2, 5) or from a POD pair's phase portrait (Weightman 2019, p. 27–28). About 500 snapshots converge the leading pair in resonant flows and about 2500 are needed when no mode dominates (p. 56–57). Phase averages keep shocks and acoustic fronts, phase reconstructions lose them (p. 102–105), and fronts fall into the random part of a POD triple decomposition (p. 92–93). Single snapshots give shock strength via Rankine–Hugoniot, smeared by particle lag (Edgington-Mitchell 2025, p. 10).
- **Time-resolved schlieren:** the thesis camera gave at most 102 frames, enough for visualisation and a streamwise–temporal Fourier split but not spectral decomposition (p. 24–26, 109–110); the 2025 paper tracked waves frame by frame at 50–200 kfps with correlation-based phase averages whose templates are not stated (p. 7, 18, 20). **No paper in this thread uses SPOD, BMD or HPOD.**
- **Models:** Powell's equation (under-determined), the vortex-sheet phase criterion with a duct nozzle-reflection model, complex-mode resonance (fragile), geometrical-acoustics ray tracing (qualitative) and the scattering theory of Nogueira 2024. There is no stability or resolvent analysis of an impinging mean flow in the library; impinging-jet LES (Gojon, Bogey, Varé) appears only as citations.

## Relevance to our work
- **Thesis PIV:** a streamwise-wavenumber split of the thin- and infinite-lip POD pairs tests for guided-mode content; Appendix A's eight-condition matrix, built for an LES comparison that never appeared, is a ready validation set with non-resonant cases (p. 145).
- **New time-resolved schlieren:** long records of thin- and infinite-lip jets on cylinder and hemisphere would allow SPOD at the tone with the k_x sign separating upstream waves, phase continuity across the shear layer, BMD of the difference tone and the shock-like fronts' harmonics, and the SPOD → reconstruction → HPOD pipeline for cycle-to-cycle Δt and mode or wave-form switching. Fine NPR and z/D microphone sweeps with bicoherence should come first.
- **PyFR LES:** nozzle exterior geometry is part of the boundary condition and must be modelled in any LES, free or impinging (Weightman 2019, p. 89).
- **Rocket noise:** impinging-jet waves are shocks at birth, linking to crackle; the open question is why the loop does not survive in hot, overexpanded plumes on inclined deflectors.
