# Impinging jets and edge tones

_Last updated: 2026-10-22 · 18 papers_

## Scope
Self-sustained tones from jets that meet a surface: subsonic and supersonic jets on plates, cylinders and hemispheres; subsonic jets grazing a plate edge (jet–edge or jet–flap tones); and how the nozzle exterior and other reflecting surfaces close such loops. It also covers impingement at launch-vehicle scale, where the question is why the laboratory tones disappear. Free-jet screech is on [screech](screech.md) and its staging on [screech-staging-and-mode-switching](screech-staging-and-mode-switching.md); vortex-sheet dispersion relations, saddle points and guided-jet-wave theory on [linear-models](linear-models.md); LES numerics on [simulation-methods](simulation-methods.md); broadband pad and deflector noise on [rocket-noise-models](rocket-noise-models.md) and [rocket-noise-measurements](rocket-noise-measurements.md); POD-pair practice on [modal-decomposition](modal-decomposition.md). Tone frequencies are collected in `Data/impinging_tones.csv` (see [Data/README](../Data/README.md)).

## Current understanding
1. **Every tone here is a Powell-type feedback loop**: a KH wavepacket goes downstream, the surface region launches an upstream wave, and receptivity at the lip closes the loop. The two legs were first measured in a subsonic jet, in phase at the plate and an integer number of cycles apart at the lip ([Ho 1981](../Notes/ho-1981-dynamics-impinging-jet-feedback-phenomenon.md), p. 124–133); LES shows the loop as a standing wave whose cell count equals Powell's N ([Bogey 2017](../Notes/bogey-2017-feedback-loop-upwind-waves-ideally-expanded-impinging-jets.md), p. 573–576). A phase condition sets the frequency, either Powell's timing equation ([Henderson 2002](../Notes/henderson-2002-flow-structure-supersonic-impinging-jets.md), eq. 1, p. 5; [Weightman 2017](../Notes/weightman-2017-phase-lag-supersonic-jet-impingement.md), eq. 1.1, p. 2) or its dispersive form Δk_r L + φ = 2nπ ([Jordan 2018](../Notes/jordan-2018-jet-flap-interaction-tones.md), p. 7–9; [Jaunet 2019](../Notes/jaunet-2019-dynamics-round-jet-impingement.md), p. 3; [Stavropoulos 2026](../Notes/stavropoulos-2026-jet-edge-interaction.md), p. 5). The constant-speed form counts stages and can be tuned to any one tone; only the dispersive form follows the trends (Jaunet 2019, p. 7; Jordan 2018, p. 4–5, 9).
2. **What carries the upstream wave depends on where it is born.** The guided-mode school starts with [Tam 1990](../Notes/tam-1990-theoretical-model-discrete-tone-impinging-jets.md): the return is an upstream neutral mode of the jet, and it must fall in the KH band (p. 72–75, 81–82). Sound generated inside the core can only travel upstream as a guided-jet (or duct-like) mode, within that mode's band; sound generated outside travels as a free-stream wave ([Nogueira 2024](../Notes/nogueira-2024-guided-jet-waves.md), p. 31–33; [Edgington-Mitchell 2025](../Notes/edgington-mitchell-2025-vortices-shocks-nonlinear-waves-impinging-jet-resonance.md), p. 3–4, 15–17). Subsonic jet–edge tones are closed by guided modes (Jordan 2018, p. 4–10; Stavropoulos 2026, p. 8–18), and so, on band-limit and LES evidence, are low-St axisymmetric impinging tones (Jaunet 2019, p. 7; Bogey 2017, p. 579–585). The group's supersonic impinging jets are often closed by external waves ([Weightman et al. 2019](../Notes/weightman-2019-nozzle-external-geometry-azimuthal-mode-impinging-jet.md), p. 440–441; [Weightman 2019](../Notes/weightman-2019-sound-production-impinging-supersonic-jets.md), p. 89).
3. **Supersonic impinging jets have several sources, all driven by the same KH vortex**: stand-off-shock leakage, a transient wall-jet shocklet (29–40 µs delay), vortex–wall sound in the core, and possibly the oscillating wall-jet boundary. Two can act within one cycle (Edgington-Mitchell 2025, p. 14–24; Weightman 2017, p. 4–10; Weightman 2019, p. 109–122; Henderson 2002, p. 4–6). An LES of the thesis infinite-lip case puts the strongest source where vortices hit the wall, 0.8–1.1d off axis, with leakage-like waves weaker ([Karami 2020](../Notes/karami-2020-acoustic-hydrodynamic-waves-underexpanded-impinging-jets.md), p. 9–12). Leakage works as in free-jet screech, at a saddle point between vortices once the vortex has passed, with vortex strength setting where ([Edgington-Mitchell 2021](../Notes/edgington-mitchell-2021-generation-screech-tones-shock-leakage.md), p. 23–30; Edgington-Mitchell 2025, p. 18).
4. **The upstream waves are weak shocks at birth** (M_s ≈ 1.07 from a PIV velocity jump), as free-jet leakage fronts are (Edgington-Mitchell 2021, p. 3–4, 18–19); sharp and diffuse forms alternate from cycle to cycle, and pairs can merge (Edgington-Mitchell 2025, p. 10–13). LES near-field pressure at the tone has the broad wavenumber content of such sharp fronts (Karami 2020, p. 15).
5. **The nozzle exterior is a boundary condition of the loop**: changing only the lip switches an NPR 3.4, z/D 5 jet from helical to toroidal, and foam on the outer base removes the helical tone (Weightman et al. 2019, p. 428–441). A reflector placed a set fraction of a wavelength behind the exit suppresses or switches tones of both large- and small-plate classes ([Henderson 1993](../Notes/henderson-1993-tones-choked-jet-impinging-flat-plates.md), p. 318–319). Both show that the external field at the lip is necessary; neither shows the return travels only outside (notes on both papers).
6. **The band in which the upstream wave exists bounds the tones.** Round impinging tones die at the upper limit S(0,1) of the m = 0 guided mode (Jaunet 2019, p. 7); jet–edge tones end at the trapping and saddle cut-offs (Jordan 2018, p. 10–14; Stavropoulos 2026, p. 15–18) of the waves of [Towne 2017](../Notes/towne-2017-acoustic-resonance-potential-core-subsonic-jets.md) (p. 31–33). The KH band limit explains the low-Mach cut-off and the axisymmetry of subsonic impinging tones (Tam 1990, p. 81–82, 85), and the bands order the alternating axisymmetric and helical tones of an LES (Bogey 2017, p. 577–582).
7. **Staging, switching and nonlinear selection are generic.** Integer-N stages with intermittent switching at stage boundaries occur in subsonic (Ho 1981, p. 132–135) and supersonic impinging jets, where multiple tones are "the rule rather than the exception" (Henderson 1993, p. 312, 318). In jet–edge flows several inharmonic, uncoupled tones can give way abruptly to one saturated oscillator (Jordan 2018, p. 5; Stavropoulos 2026, p. 8–15).
8. **No impingement tones are seen at launch-vehicle scale**; pad noise is broadband, dominated by ground-reflected Mach waves and flame-duct sources ([Lubert 2022](../Notes/lubert-2022-supersonic-jet-noise-launch-vehicles-review.md), p. 16–20; [Tsutsumi 2015](../Notes/tsutsumi-2015-epsilon-liftoff-acoustics.md), p. 13–16; [Panda 2013](../Notes/panda-2013-noise-sources-rocket-test-firings-launch-phased-array.md), p. 17).

## The feedback equation and the phase lag
Ho and Nosseir separated the legs of the loop in a cold M 0.3–0.9 jet on a large plate (resonance for M > 0.7, x0/d < 7.5; p. 119) with pre-whitened correlations and cross-spectral phase. Structures convect at 0.62U, and non-dispersive upstream waves travel at a∞ with conical fronts whose angle falls from about 37° to 22° as x0/d goes from 3 to 7 (p. 124–129). The legs are in phase at the plate and 5 × 2π apart at the lip (M 0.8, x0/d 4; p. 132–133), so p = 0; this is the form the thesis uses (Weightman 2019, eq. 1.3, p. 6). Integer N gives the M 0.9 staircase (N = 2–6), and with St_min = 0.33 eq. 7b places the jumps at x0/d = 1.25N against 2.40–7.50 measured (Table 1, p. 134–135). But constant speeds give integer N only at stage ends: mid-stage the count is 0.4–0.7 low, and the jumps are 11–19% instead of (N + 1)/N (note's reading of Fig. 14a). That is the "frequency squeezing" Jordan 2018 attributes to a dispersive upstream wave (p. 4–5, 9), now seen in an impinging jet. Henderson and Powell found the same looseness at supersonic speed: Powell's constants (p = 0, M_con,u = 0.73, h″/d = 0.8) count N = 2–8 near integers (p. 314), yet at fixed N cannot give the small-plate tones' log–log slopes above 1 (note's check), which supports the authors' view that those loops scale differently (p. 324).

Henderson et al. built the cycle of an NPR 4.03 jet on a large plate (h/d = 2.02, axisymmetric L1 tone ≈ 10 kHz) from microphone-locked shadowgraphs and phase-binned 10 Hz PIV (p. 2–5). A three-leg Powell criterion gave N = 4, p ≈ +0.4, but the first leg's convection Mach number (0.7–0.75) was borrowed from Powell (1988) and Henderson 1993 because disturbances upstream of the Mach disk could not be tracked (p. 5–6). In Bogey and Gojon's LES the p = 0 equation gets the dominant tones within 0.7–8%, with every tone at N to N + 0.5 (note's check), consistent with the p = 0 of Gojon et al. 2016.

Weightman et al. (2019) show how loosely the timing equation constrains the loop. With p = 0 and measured U_c/U_j = 0.44–0.48, the direct, inner-base and outer-base paths each reproduce both tone families (St ≈ 0.25 and 0.39) for some integer N, and moving the reflection point across the outer base shifts St from 0.34 to 0.42 (p. 442–445). The allowed tones are only ΔSt = 0.034–0.065 apart, so matches within 0.02 cannot pick a path (note's arithmetic). The gain criterion that could (p. 423) is never quantified. Weightman et al. (2017) then measured every term for a cylinder case (NPR 3.6, z/D 3.5): N = 4, u_c(x) from the POD phase average, U_s = 331 m/s after an entrainment correction, and a delay Δt = 29.4 µs between shocklet inception and emission (p ≈ −0.24). The prediction is 8230 Hz against 8300 Hz measured (−0.8%), against +5.4% with Δt = 0; on a hemisphere at NPR 5.0 the schlieren gives Δt = 40 µs directly, p = −0.49 (p. 5, 7–10).

This support for the shocklet is weaker than it looks. The 0.8% agreement is largely built in: Δt is the phase residual that closes the loop with the same u_c and path that enter the integral, so the equation returns the measured f up to the gap between the straight acoustic leg and the traced wave (Weightman 2017 note, Key equations). The independent evidence is the 0.24-cycle deficit at Δt = 0 and the 40 µs seen on the hemisphere. The deficit would vanish with a mean u_c of 0.43U_j, which the paper rejects as implausible (p. 10). Yet the measured local u_c spans 0.42–0.52U_j and is valid only over 0.75 < x/D < 2.6 (p. 8), so a 9% bias would close the gap without a delay, and a phase average cannot give the cycle-to-cycle spread of Δt (p. 9). The convection speed is itself uncertain. LES of the thesis infinite-lip case gives 0.77a₀ ≈ 0.63U_j from x–t diagrams (Karami 2020, p. 13; note's conversion), close to Bogey 2017 (0.58–0.62u_j, p. 571) and Ho 1981 (0.62U, p. 128–129), against 0.44–0.48U_j from the thesis PIV at the same NPR and z/D (Weightman et al. 2019, p. 432–433). And the cylinder values are normalised by U_j = 460.8 m/s (= M_j U_E) rather than the isentropic ≈ 420 m/s, on which basis the rejected value is 0.47U_j (Weightman 2017 note).

Published phase lags, +0.4 (Henderson), 0 (Gojon et al. 2016), −0.4 (Krothapalli et al. 1999; both as cited in Weightman 2017, p. 2) and −0.49, use different loop definitions: under the −1 ≤ p ≤ 0 reading of Weightman 2017 (p. 10), Henderson's +0.4 would be a negative delay. From Karami's LES numbers, N + p ≈ 4.2 and 5.2 at h = 2d and 5d, i.e. p ≈ +0.2 (note's estimate). In the dispersive models the lag becomes the end-condition phase φ, still a free parameter until the plate and nozzle reflection coefficients are known (Jaunet 2019, p. 5, 10). Nor can a fitted p yet show which source is active: the thesis times leakage (p ≈ 0) from vortex arrival (Weightman 2019, p. 122) and the shocklet from its inception (Weightman 2017, p. 9), but leakage begins only after the vortex has passed (Edgington-Mitchell 2025, p. 18), once the saddle point behind it reaches the shock (Edgington-Mitchell 2021, p. 26). Timed from the vortex core, that wait is of order half a cycle, comparable to the shocklet's delay (my estimate).

**Dispersive models.** For subsonic edges the Pprime group replaced Powell's constant speeds with vortex-sheet wavenumbers: R₁R₂e^{iΔkL} = 1, in phase only, with φ = 0 or π (Jordan 2018, p. 7–9). The dispersive upstream wave captures the frequency squeezing that the classical model misses (p. 4–5, 9). Keeping |R₁R₂| (0.002–0.008) bounds the tone-free low-St region but is fragile near saddle points (p. 15–16, 20–24). The same criterion is the screech model of [Mancinelli 2019](../Notes/mancinelli-2019-screech-prediction-upstream-modes.md), which notes that a plate would simply replace the shock cell as the downstream reflector (p. 2). Tam and Ahuja had met the same effect from another side: Neuwerth's impinging tones stay near one average St whatever L/D, which they predict as the St of the least-dispersive (0, 1) wave, d²ω/dk² = 0 (eq. 25, p. 82–83). The curve falls from 0.73 at M_j 0.70 to 0.36 at 0.95 and matches five data sets, Ho's included, over M_j 0.7–0.95 (Fig. 16, p. 83), though the four M_j 0.9 points sit 5–15% below it (note's reading). The note's recalculation shows the inflection point is where Towne's neutral saddles are born (M_j ≈ 0.81 cold, against 0.82 isothermal in Towne 2017, p. 31–32), and that the flat (0, 1) branch in the dispersive phase condition gives a plateau at St ≈ 0.54–0.56 over L/D 2.5–5 at M_j 0.8. So the L-independent average St and frequency squeezing are one effect. Jaunet et al. carried the phase-only model to round impinging jets at M_j 0.7–1.5: free-stream closure can be tuned to any single peak but never fits all of them and predicts tones where none occur, while KH plus the k_p⁻ guided wave follows the trends across M_j and H/D, although no predicted line lands exactly on a peak (p. 7). At H/D 8 the φ = 0 and π lines are only about 0.03 apart in St, so the evidence lies in the slopes and the extinction limit (note's estimate). At supersonic M_j frequencies barely separate the two closures: on k = −ω/a₀ the combined model of Bogey and Gojon (eq. 3.6) reduces exactly to the classical one, and at M_j 1.5 the upstream branches travel at 0.93–1.00a₀ (note's checks; the authors note that the tones sit where the waves move at about a₀, p. 582).

## What carries the upstream wave
**Guided-mode closure.** Tam and Ahuja proposed that the upstream leg of impinging tones in cold, high-Re round jets is an upstream neutral mode of the vortex-sheet jet (Tam & Hu 1989), with a radial and azimuthal shape fixed by the mean flow (p. 72–75). Only modes in the KH band, St < 0.6–0.7, can take part. In cold subsonic jets that leaves the axisymmetric (0, 1) mode, so subsonic resonance must be axisymmetric; at supersonic M_j the helical (1, 1) mode qualifies too (p. 81–82), and their M_j 1.4 photographs show axisymmetric resonance at L/D 6 and helical at L/D 10 (p. 70–71). The least-dispersive St leaving the KH band is offered as the low-Mach cut-off (p. 85); the note's reproduction puts the crossing at M_j ≈ 0.72, not the printed 0.65. External closure was rejected because the (0, 1) eigenfunction is confined to the jet and a coflow test showed "little frequency shift" (p. 84–85). The note finds both grounds weak: external closure predicts only about an 8% shift, and Ho's 5 × 2π lip count is also met by the guided wave with n = 6–8.

For an M 0.6–1.0 jet grazing an edge at L/D = 2–4, KH paired with the upstream jet modes k_TH⁻ (M ≤ 0.82; Tam and Ahuja's (0, 1) branch) and k_p⁻ (0.82 < M < 1) reproduces the tone spacing and Mach trend that free-stream closure cannot (Jordan 2018, p. 4–10). These are the waves trapped in the potential core of a free M 0.9 jet, where the vortex-sheet model allows resonance only for 0.82 < M < 1 (Towne 2017, p. 14–19, 31–33). The cut-offs are physical: above St ≈ 1 at M ≤ 0.82 the k_TH⁻ wave is confined to the core and transmitted into the nozzle rather than reflected, and above 0.82 the k_p⁻ branch ends at the S2 saddle; the two lines bound the observed tones (Jordan 2018, p. 10–14). This reverses Tam and Ahuja's reading, for whom confinement in the core was the mark of in-jet feedback (p. 84). Both can hold if at St ≈ 0.5–0.7 the wave keeps enough amplitude at the lip, but neither paper has a receptivity model (Tam 1990 note).

Jaunet et al. (cold, thin-lipped jet; M_j 0.7–1.5, H/D 2–8; one exit-plane microphone) found that from H/D 4 the tones form ordered families, each dying along a curve that matches the upper limit S(0,1) of the m = 0 guided mode "almost perfect[ly]" (p. 2–3, 7). This extends the band cut-offs of Jordan 2018 (p. 12–14) and Mancinelli 2019 (p. 6) to impinging jets. Two qualifications come from the note. The plotted S(0,1) is reproduced by an isothermal vortex sheet while the jet is cold, whose band edge is 4–12% higher at M_j 1.0–1.5, so the match needs rechecking. And many strong supersonic tones lie above S(0,1), e.g. a B-like line at H/D 6–8 (read from Fig. 2), which the model cannot produce and the paper does not discuss. The paper also discards duct-like upstream waves as having too little support outside the jet (p. 6–7), yet such waves are seen, strongly nonlinear, in M 0.86–0.90 impinging jets (Edgington-Mitchell 2025, p. 8), and k⁻_d loops close jet–edge tones (Stavropoulos 2026, p. 15–18).

Bogey and Gojon supply the LES evidence: four LES of a cold, ideally expanded M_j 1.5 jet (Re 6 × 10⁴, laminar exit, lip 0.1r₀) on a plate at L/D 3–6 reproduce the tones and staging paths of Krothapalli et al. (1999) (p. 572–577). The tones alternate in St bands, axisymmetric below 0.225, helical to 0.355, axisymmetric to 0.5 and helical above. The classical model cannot explain this; the vortex-sheet upstream bands order it (p. 577–582), and the LES amplitude has the A2 eigenfunction's node near r ≈ 0.5r₀ (p. 583). (A1, A2 there are radial orders of the guided mode, not screech stages.) Pressure snapshots and k–ω spectra of the L/D 6 jet show waves travelling upstream inside the supersonic core and on the lip line at about a₀ (p. 583–585).

**External closure.** In Weightman et al.'s jets (NPR 3.4, z/D 5, D = 15 mm), foam over the outer base removes the thin-lip helical tone (a weak St 0.472 appears instead), foam over the inner base leaves it, and an infinite foam cap kills the resonance (Weightman et al. 2019, p. 434–441). The paper concludes that, "for this facility", the loop closes by reflection off the outer base, a path not reported before (p. 435, 441); the thesis adds the contrast between freely propagating external waves and the guided modes of Gojon and Bogey's LES (Weightman 2019, p. 89). The foam shows the outer-base path is necessary, not that the direct path contributes nothing: interference of the Henderson 1993 kind is excluded only by where the surfaces sit (p. 441), and two foam cases gave different spectra in the uncaged schlieren rig (p. 426, 442). Jordan et al. cite this work as evidence that free-stream sound can close impinging loops (Jordan 2018, p. 2). The classical external school is no more decisive. Ho and Nosseir measured only outside the jet (r/d > 2.5) and set aside Wagner's in-core standing wave (p. 120), which Tam and Ahuja read as KH plus the upstream neutral wave (Tam 1990, p. 85) and which resembles the duct-like waves imaged by Edgington-Mitchell 2025 (p. 8); their data cannot tell sound from a guided wave near sonic speed (Ho 1981 note). Karami et al.'s LES shows shear-layer upstream waves at ≈ −a₀, but the authors say free-stream and guided waves cannot be told apart there, and they do not look inside the core (p. 14–16); their remark that a guided mode in a supersonic core is "unexpected" (p. 3) is contradicted by Tam 1990 (p. 77–79), [Edgington-Mitchell 2018](../Notes/edgington-mitchell-2018-upstream-acoustic-modes-screech-closure.md) and Nogueira 2024.

**Reconciliation.** Nogueira et al. treat the guided mode as a scattering problem: a plane wave arriving from outside and travelling directly upstream is cancelled at the shear layer, while sound generated inside is totally reflected over a band (Nogueira 2024, p. 14–22). Screech always needs the mode because its KH–shock source lies inside the sonic line; impingement needs it only when the source is in the core (p. 31–33). Edgington-Mitchell et al. (2025) supply the experiments: vortex–wall sound peaks on the axis, so it is an in-core source and must pass the guided-mode filter, which explains why guided-mode models work for subsonic and some supersonic impinging jets; the shocklet and stand-off-shock leakage sit outside the core (p. 8, 14–17). In some Bogey and Gojon LES cases the pressure inside and outside the jet is almost uncorrelated, which rules out the guided mode there (p. 8). The "two schools" were describing different mechanisms, which can coexist within one cycle (p. 25). The classical papers fit this: Tam and Ahuja's subsonic loop is driven by structures hitting the wall inside the core, and their supersonic modes lie "outside the jet but guided by it" (Tam 1990, p. 79). Bogey and Gojon's ideally expanded jets have no strong stand-off shock, so leakage and the shocklet may simply be absent; the paper says only that the waves "seem to be generated in the region of jet impingement" (p. 566–567), and Nogueira's in-core reading of it (p. 33) is an interpretation (Bogey 2017 note).

**Band test.** The notes now place both schools' tones against cold vortex-sheet bands. At M_j 1.42 the bands are (0, 1) St 0–0.22, (0, 2) 0.42–0.53 and (1, 1) 0.369–0.371: the thesis helical tones (0.241–0.255) lie 31–35% below the (1, 1) cut-off and the toroidal ones (0.383–0.385) 9–10% below (0, 2) (Tam 1990 note), and both lie above S(0,1) ≈ 0.19–0.21 (Jaunet 2019 note). That supports external closure robustly for the helical tone and only weakly for the toroidal one, since a thick shear layer lowers the bands and shocks are ignored. In the LES every axisymmetric tone lies inside A1 or A2, or within 6.4% below A2, but six of seven helical tones lie 11–26% below H1 or H2; the paper's plotted H1 range is wider than its own eq. 3.5 gives (Bogey 2017 note). The LES tones at L/D 5–6 (helical 0.255–0.29, axisymmetric 0.375–0.44) closely resemble the thesis tones. On band evidence, then, guided closure is plausible for the axisymmetric tones and unproven for the helical ones in both, and the schools may agree more than the framing suggests (Bogey 2017 note's synthesis). The LES's helical k–ω evidence is at r = r₀, where grazing external sound at a₀ would look the same within its ΔkD ≈ 1.3 (Bogey 2017 note).

Two caveats remain. Nogueira's argument concerns whether an external wave can *create* a guided mode, whereas external closure works by direct receptivity at the lip, so it constrains in-core sources without proving external closure; Ho's upstream waves also arrive obliquely (θ_a ≈ 25–35°), not straight upstream (Ho 1981 note). And the foam and reflector tests show the external path is *necessary*, but the thesis PIV has never been split by streamwise wavenumber (as in Edgington-Mitchell 2018) to look for a guided component too.

## Where the upstream wave is generated
Henderson et al. describe an axially oscillating Mach disk that moves recirculation zones between stand-off shock and plate, an annular shock that moves and collapses, and a wall jet oscillating strongly at r ≈ 2.6R (≈ 1.3d), proposed as an annular simple source (p. 3–6). At h/d = 3.00, in a zone of silence, a second stand-off shock forms and the recirculation survives without a tone, so recirculation alone does not make tones (p. 4). Henderson and Powell's single-spark schlieren had put the fronts in the wall jet only at very small spacing, and "in the impingement region" at larger spacings (Henderson 1993, p. 321–322), in mild tension with that wall-jet source. On small plates (D/d ≲ 2–4) a second class of tones appears from NPR 3.04 with steeper slopes; there the stand-off shock oscillates streamwise by up to d/3, with only symmetric disturbances and no link to passing ones, and the proposed loop runs through that shock (p. 316–318, 322–324).

Weightman added two mechanisms. A streamwise–temporal Fourier split of 500 kfps schlieren shows an upstream wave emitted at the stand-off shock, and ray tracing on POD phase-reconstructed PIV puts leakage there with no delay, matching the schlieren in location and timing (Weightman 2019, p. 109–122); the published paper rests that source and p = 0 on the schlieren alone (Weightman et al. 2019, p. 443). The ray tracing is qualitative (scale separation violated at convective Mach ≈ 1, c = 340 m/s, no directivity or amplitude), and leakage depends strongly on vortex amplitude (none at Ã = 0.2, sideline at 0.7), which the phase reconstruction smooths (Weightman 2019, p. 112–117, 162). The second mechanism is the shocklet. On a hemisphere at NPR 5.0 a wall-jet shock forms as a vortex passes, rotates towards normal to the wall, creeps upstream and releases the returning wave shortly before the next vortex arrives (Weightman 2017, p. 4–6); it was also seen, though not shown, at other NPR and on flat plates (p. 5). On the cylinder it sits at (x/D, y/D) = (3.78, 1.43) (p. 8), close to Henderson's 1.3D source. The location agrees but the mechanism differs: the article says the shocklet "appears to differ" from Henderson's source (p. 5), the thesis calls it the first mechanistic description of it (Weightman 2019, p. 136), and Edgington-Mitchell 2025 keeps them separate (p. 23–24).

Karami et al.'s LES of the thesis infinite-lip case (NPR 3.4, a hole in a flat wall, plate at h = 2d and 5d) puts the strongest upstream waves where coherent structures hit the wall, 0.8d and 1.1d from the axis, located by tracing cylindrical fronts back (p. 9, 12). That lies between the on-axis vortex–wall source of Edgington-Mitchell 2025 (p. 14–15) and the wall-jet sources at 1.3d (Henderson) and 1.43D (shocklet). Waves from vortex–oblique-shock and stand-off-shock interaction, resembling leakage, are present but weaker (p. 12). The source is placed by eye, and the LES fields were not used for source attribution (Karami 2020 note). In Bogey and Gojon's LES, circular waves centred near z ≈ 3r₀, made where upstream waves meet shear-layer structures (p. 567), might be a leakage-like escape of the trapped wave (Bogey 2017 note, speculation).

Edgington-Mitchell et al. (2021) published the leakage method for free jets. Their k < 0-filtered 1 Mfps schlieren of screeching A2, B and twin jets shows the shock tilting upstream as a vortex arrives and a small arc of its tip separating as one sharp front (p. 13–19). Ray tracing (planar; constant c, as temperature modelled from PIV worsened shock artefacts; p. 11–13) puts the first leak at the 3rd or 4th cell, at a saddle point rather than the vorticity peak: a ray meeting a vortex is turned back into the core (p. 19–28). Rescaling the reconstruction to r̄ ± 2σ of the POD-pair amplitude moves the first leak at NPR 2.25 from about the 3rd cell (strong vortices) to the 6th (weak), spanning the 3rd–5th seen in schlieren (p. 28–30), a measured-field counterpart of the thesis's Stuart-vortex sweep. The impinging version is still unpublished (Edgington-Mitchell 2025, p. 17).

Edgington-Mitchell et al. (2025) widened the evidence to four nozzles (round and AR 2 rectangular, converging and C-D), three surface shapes and NPR 2.5–6 (p. 5–7). The shocklet appears to close the loop consistently when NPR ≥ 4.5 and z/D ≤ 4.0, and is not visible for NPR ≤ 3.0 in most datasets, perhaps because the wall jet hides it (p. 15–17). Leakage shows a split front, not seen in screeching jets, as the stronger vortices turn part of it back into the core (p. 18); Henderson's source is hypothesised to produce diffuse waves that coalesce (p. 23–24). When one vortex makes two strong waves, usually only one reaches the lip with useful strength, so some measured tonal components are by-products (p. 24–25), a mechanism-level form of Henderson and Powell's worry that some tones are sum and difference products (p. 324). No clean map of mechanism against NPR, z/D or azimuthal mode exists (p. 12–13, 25).

The same paper weakens two thesis inferences. An in-core shock from vortex–wall impact would displace the stand-off shock and look like leakage in schlieren, so the thin-lip leakage identification is plausible but not unique (p. 18). And the quantitative shocklet test was at NPR 3.6 (Weightman 2017, p. 7–10), below the regime where the shocklet is found to close the loop consistently; the directly timed hemisphere case (NPR 5.0, stand-off not stated) sits inside it in NPR.

At rocket scale, LES of a cold M_e 1.8–2.0 jet on a 45° plate shows five wave types: free-jet and wall-jet Mach waves, vortex–plate-shock waves, BBSAN-like tail-shock waves and separation-shock waves (Lubert 2022, p. 17–18). The last three are inclined-plate relatives of leakage and the shocklet, but none is a tone.

## Mode selection: lip, surface and azimuthal mode
At NPR 3.4, z/D 5 the mean flow barely changes with the lip, but the tones do: the thin lip (t/D 0.1) gives one helical tone at St 0.255; the infinite lip (t/D 7) a toroidal tone at 0.385, a helical one at 0.241 and their difference at 0.144 (Weightman et al. 2019, p. 428–433). The intermediate lip (t/D 0.33) should entrain like the thin lip but behaves like the infinite one (0.383, 0.253), so entrainment is rejected in favour of the acoustic path (p. 433–439), though the relevant entrainment may lie closer to the exit than the PIV resolves (p. 439). Nearly every configuration gives St ≈ 0.25 or 0.39, assumed but not shown to be the base flow's most unstable frequencies (p. 441, 446). The switch is not universal: at NPR 3.0, z/D 3.5 both lips give a toroidal pair, and at NPR 4.2, z/D 2 neither gives a dominant mode (Weightman 2019, p. 147–155). An LES of the infinite-lip case reproduces the toroidal tone (St_j 0.39 against 0.385, with m = 0 holding ≈ 65% of the shear-layer energy; Karami 2020, p. 18, 28), but shows no spectrum and does not address the helical or difference tones; its h = 2d case has one clean tone, where the nearest thesis case (z/D 2, thick lip) had no dominant pair (Weightman 2019, p. 151; Karami 2020 note).

Henderson and Powell's choked nozzle (t/d ≈ 0.28) shows the same mix: cross-spectra make the large-plate L1 line symmetrical and L2/L3 helical (p. 322–323), as Tam and Ahuja predicted for supersonic M_j (Tam 1990, p. 82), while Henderson's 0.5d lip gave only L1 (Henderson 2002, p. 2–3). With the thesis's intermediate lip, these nozzles form a lip-thickness sequence consistent with item 5 (Henderson 1993 note), though Henderson and Powell's phases never reached the expected values and changed with small changes in h/d (p. 323). Tam and Ahuja's axisymmetry rule for subsonic jets holds cleanly only below M_j ≈ 0.85, since the (1, 1) cut-off is already St 0.69 at M_j 0.9 (Tam 1990 note). They also predict that subsonic twin impinging jets should not couple, because flapping needs the (1, 1) mode (p. 70–71); this is untested (see [twin-jet-coupling](twin-jet-coupling.md)).

Free-jet screech responds differently, with lip thickness changing staging and the Mach range of screech, but Ponton's proposed mechanism, upstream sound reflecting off the larger exit face, is the same acoustic-path idea ([Ponton 1992](../Notes/ponton-1992-nozzle-exit-lip-thickness-plume-resonance.md), p. 536–548, not tagged). Upstream reflectors detune screech ([Raman 1998](../Notes/raman-1998-supersonic-jet-screech-review.md), p. 75–78, not tagged), and an upstream plate on a Monash rig altered free-jet staging ([Edgington-Mitchell 2022](../Notes/edgington-mitchell-2022-unifying-theory-jet-screech.md), p. 6, 16, not tagged). The plate changes the selection too: the same t/D 0.33 lip gives a helical C-mode free jet at NPR 3.4 (St 0.30; [Edgington-Mitchell 2014](../Notes/edgington-mitchell-2014-coherent-structure-helical-screech-mode.md), not tagged) but a toroidal-dominant jet with a plate at 5D (Weightman et al. 2019, p. 434, 437).

Surface shape matters as well. A convex cylinder fixes the flapping axis, so repeated PIV catches the same instability (St 0.29, 42% of the energy in POD modes 1–2; Weightman 2019, p. 29–30); a hemisphere shows the shocklet best; every phenomenon also appears, less clearly, on flat plates (Edgington-Mitchell 2025, p. 6–7). Plate size matters: small plates give their own tone class and intermediate ones (D/d 1–2) often greatly reduce tonal activity (Henderson 1993, p. 314–317).

One coincidence has not been followed up: the thin-lip impinging tone (St 0.255 at NPR 3.4) is close to free-jet helical C-mode screech at NPR 3.40 in the same group's D = 15 mm nozzle family (St 0.26, [Edgington-Mitchell 2020](../Notes/edgington-mitchell-2020-waves-in-screeching-jets.md), not tagged, but 0.30 for the same PIV case in Edgington-Mitchell 2021, p. 6). Whether the helical impinging tone is a plate-modified screech loop has not been tested.

## Receptivity at the lip
Ho and Nosseir found that near the lip the signal carries the initial instability (St 3–5, ∝ M^{1/2}) on top of the tone, and is almost purely the tone by x ≈ 1.3d. They proposed "collective interaction", forced multiple vortex merging, as the receptivity mechanism (p. 135–139), and explained the high-M onset of resonance by the forcing amplitude it needs (p. 138). Their claim that the resonance is then "independent of the initial conditions" (p. 140) was never tested and is contradicted, for supersonic jets, by the lip results above (Weightman et al. 2019, p. 428–441). In the supersonic thesis case Karami et al. see no collective interaction and judge vortex pairing (once, occasionally twice) too weak for a tenfold drop; the structures settle at the loop frequency about 1d from the lip by spatial growth on a rapidly evolving mean flow (p. 12–13, 21–25). Their local m = 0 stability analysis of the LES mean puts the most unstable St near 4 at x = 0.1d, falling to about 0.5 by 0.9d, identically for h = 2d and 5d, whose tones differ by a factor of two (p. 21–23); so a local most-unstable frequency near the lip cannot select the tone (Karami 2020 note). The lip responds within about 2 µs of a wave's arrival, so the receptivity delay is negligible (Mitchell et al. 2012, as cited in Weightman 2017, p. 2), and the thesis attributes a high receptivity to the high-contraction nozzle's thin initial shear layer (Weightman 2019, p. 89). Tam and Ahuja's low-Mach cut-off (frequency mismatch with the KH band) and Ho and Nosseir's (forcing amplitude) are compatible, and neither has been tested on the same data (Tam 1990 note).

## Staging, switching and frequency selection
Ho and Nosseir gave the first switching evidence in this thread: at M 0.9, x0/d 2.35 a 2.4 s spectrum holds the N = 2 and 3 tones (≈ 4.4 and 5.1 kHz), while 0.061 s spectra usually hold one, so the jet alternates between stages. They call it hysteresis but give no sweep direction or dwell times (p. 134–135). Henderson and Powell extend this to supersonic, multi-azimuthal-mode jets. At small and intermediate spacings three parallel lines L1–L3 stage with h/d, and they merge into screech at h/d ≳ 5 (p. 311–316). At NPR 3.04, D/d 4, h/d 3.8, two-average spectra switch at random between L1 and L2 (20 and 14.25 kHz) near an L1 jump, where the jet looked most receptive, and whether long-average multiple tones coexist or alternate "was not investigated" (p. 316, 318). Jaunet et al. likewise see screech only with the plate at H/D 8, and quiet bands at H/D 2 that they link to Henderson et al.'s zones of silence (p. 3). Bogey and Gojon's LES holds four tones in every record, with a sum tone of a helical and an axisymmetric tone at L/D 3 (p. 573); in the thesis infinite-lip spectrum the difference tone (0.144) is louder than its 0.241 parent (Weightman et al. 2019, Fig. 5, p. 430, read in the note), which argues for at least partial coexistence of the two loops.

For jet–edge tones, Jordan et al. saw weak multi-peak resonance and, in some Mach ranges, a single high-Q oscillator with its harmonic and suppressed broadband (e.g. St ≈ 1.25 at M ≈ 0.78, L = 2D), with wavelets showing dominance, slow or fast competition, or coexistence of neighbouring cycles (p. 5, 16–17). Stavropoulos et al. mapped this in steps of 0.01 in M_j and R/D, using bicoherence with a threshold (b > 0.35) set from a tone-free case (p. 2–4). Linear selection (several inharmonic tones, zero bicoherence) gives way to nonlinear selection (one saturated tone with harmonics and passive triads) only for R/D ≲ 0.57 and 0.56 ≲ M_j ≲ 0.87 (p. 8–12). The jump at M_j 0.87–0.88 is two orders of magnitude within ΔM_j = 0.01, without hysteresis, and the nonlinear fundamental lies on the same model line as a linear tone, so the oscillator is an amplified linear loop (p. 9, 13–15). Between M_j 0.82 and 0.86 the dominant tone switches from a k⁻_d loop (St ≈ 0.52) to a k⁻_p loop (St ≈ 0.46); a duct-model reflection ratio and k⁻_p's wider radial support are offered for why k⁻_p wins, though the authors say a non-parallel model is needed (p. 15–18, 23–25). Tam and Ahuja's least-dispersive St at M_j 0.85 (0.477) lies between the two (Tam 1990 note).

## Impingement at launch-vehicle scale
An Epsilon boom microphone with the nozzle 8.7 D_e above the pad shows no tone, and Lubert et al. offer only masking by mixing noise and pad geometry as reasons (Lubert 2022, p. 16–17). The 1/42 Epsilon campaign (hot, overexpanded M_e 3.7, 14° deflector) found the worst fairing level (149.1 dB at H/D_e = 14) came from free-jet Mach waves reflected off the ground in the open flame path, with impingement waves hard to see in the LES (Tsutsumi 2015, p. 11–16). At Wallops the water-quenched flame-duct exit dominated an Antares static fire, without tones (Panda 2013, p. 17, not tagged). Vega pad CAA shows reflected waves reaching the nozzle lip, so feedback is possible but untested ([Casalino 2009](../Notes/casalino-2009-hybrid-empirical-caa-rocket-noise.md), p. 14–15, not tagged). Empirical models treat impingement only by ending the potential core at the deflector, which helps against Saturn V ([Haynes 2009](../Notes/haynes-2009-sp-8072-dsm-ii-ares-i-lift-off.md), p. 10–12, not tagged) but overpredicts Ares I ([Ranow 2021](../Notes/ranow-2021-acoustic-prediction-rocket-flame-deflector.md), p. 41–44, not tagged). Impingement on terrain removes high-frequency content and skewness ([Gee 2012](../Notes/gee-2012-reusable-srm-noise-spectral-statistical.md), p. 10–11, not tagged).

## Disagreements and open questions
**Guided or free-stream closure in a given supersonic impinging jet.** Settled in principle by source location, but never tested on one dataset. The vortex-sheet band test has now been run on both schools' tones (Tam 1990, Jaunet 2019 and Bogey 2017 notes): it favours guided closure for the axisymmetric LES tones and external closure for the thesis helical tone, but frequencies alone barely separate the two at supersonic M_j, and Jaunet's tones above S(0,1) are unexplained. The bands also need the right temperature ratio and shear-layer thickness: thickening lowers the (0, 2) limits in [Mancinelli 2021](../Notes/mancinelli-2021-complex-valued-resonance-model-screech.md) (p. 16–17, not tagged), whereas Bogey and Gojon's temporal mixing layers put thick-layer bands slightly above the vortex sheet (p. 586–588). A streamwise-wavenumber split inside the core (as in Bogey 2017, p. 583–585; not done in the infinite-lip LES, Karami 2020, p. 16), the coherence and phase continuity of the tone across the shear layer (Edgington-Mitchell 2025, p. 8), and placing each tone against cold, finite-thickness bands (Nogueira 2024, eqs. 4.7–4.8) would decide it.

**Leakage or vortex–wall impact at the stand-off shock.** The infinite-lip LES puts the main source at the wall 0.8–1.1d off axis and leakage-like waves weaker, but by eye (Karami 2020, p. 9–12). Cases with the stand-off shock well clear of the wall, ray tracing with a measured sound-speed field and amplitude-preserving reconstructions (r̄ ± 2σ rescaling is a first step; Edgington-Mitchell 2021, p. 28–30), or LES with surface-source attribution would separate them, with free-jet leakage (p. 13–19) as the reference signature.

**What triggers leakage.** A sufficiently positive sonic-line angle "rather than the saddle point" (Weightman 2019, p. 163), or the saddle point (Edgington-Mitchell 2021, p. 26–27)? The thesis takes the sonic-line tilt at the saddle (p. 114–115), so these may be one geometry; neither gives a general criterion combining incidence angle and local vorticity (Edgington-Mitchell 2021, p. 26–28).

**The phase lag.** Values from +0.4 to −0.49 use different loop definitions and reference events, and the shocklet's 0.8% agreement is largely built into the method, leaving a 0.24-cycle deficit that a 9% lower u_c would also explain (Weightman 2017 note). Convection speeds for the same NPR 3.4 case differ by 30–40% between LES x–t diagrams and PIV (Karami 2020, p. 13; Weightman et al. 2019, p. 432–433). A common loop definition and reference event (vortex core, saddle point or shocklet inception), u_c measured along the whole path including the turn into the wall jet, and cycle-resolved Δt would let p separate leakage from the shocklet. Since df/f ≈ −0.2% per µs of Δt, ±10 µs of jitter should appear as about ±2% instantaneous-frequency wander, testable with a Hilbert analysis of long microphone records (Weightman 2017 note).

**Where the shocklet matters.** It closes the loop consistently at NPR ≥ 4.5, z/D ≤ 4.0 (Edgington-Mitchell 2025, p. 15–17); the direct 40 µs delay is at NPR 5.0, but the quantitative delay test is at NPR 3.6 (Weightman 2017, p. 5, 7–10). High-frame-rate imaging on a hemisphere at NPR 3.4–3.6 would show whether it also closes the loop there or the agreement is partly coincidental.

**What fixes the recurring St ≈ 0.25 and 0.39.** A near-lip most-unstable frequency is unlikely: it falls from about 4 to 0.5 within 1d, identically for two spacings whose tones differ twofold (Karami 2020, p. 21–23). Similar families appear in an ideally expanded, thin-lip LES at L/D 5–6 (helical 0.255–0.29, axisymmetric 0.375–0.44; Bogey 2017, p. 573, 576). Global stability or resolvent analysis of the PIV mean flows, as done for free jets in Edgington-Mitchell 2020, and free and impinging spectra at the same NPR and lip would test the "most unstable frequency" assumption and the link to C-mode screech.

**Which upstream wave closes jet–edge loops, and with what phase.** Jordan 2018 found that k_d⁻ loops "did not match the data" and needed φ = 0 for some cases and π for others (p. 9). Stavropoulos 2026, with the same facility, nozzle and overlapping geometry (L/D = 2, R/D = 0.5) but a thicker chamfered plate, closes higher tones at M_j 0.82–0.89 with k⁻_d (p = 4–9) and uses φ = 0 almost everywhere (p. 13–18). With integer p up to 9 and φ free, the criterion can fit almost any tone, and the m = 1 tones are unexplained. Jaunet 2019 excludes the duct-like waves from its impinging model (p. 6–7), which may cost it tones in 0.82 < M_j < 1 (Jaunet 2019 note). An azimuthal array and a non-parallel model with nozzle and edge, giving R₁ and R₂, would settle these points.

**Coexistence or switching of tones.** Switching between neighbouring stages or lines has been seen on short spectral windows in subsonic and supersonic impinging jets, without dwell times (Ho 1981, p. 134–135; Henderson 1993, p. 318), and the four coexisting LES tones were never tested (Bogey 2017, p. 573). For the thick lips, PIV POD and averaged spectra cannot tell toroidal–helical coexistence from switching (Weightman 2019, p. 33, 70, 119), though the loud difference tone argues for partial coexistence (Weightman et al. 2019 note). Band-pass Hilbert envelopes and bicoherence of the existing 2.5 s, 200 kHz microphone records (Weightman et al. 2019, p. 426) are a cheap first test; long-record time-resolved schlieren with Hilbert POD, and BMD of 0.144 = 0.385 − 0.241, would locate the interaction.

**Why full-scale pads show no tones.** Masking is a conjecture. The laboratory work suggests more specific candidates: inclined deflectors have no normal stand-off shock, nozzle heights of 8 D_e or more, the shocklet's need for a transonic wall jet, and the dependence of the guided-mode band on M_j and temperature, which has not been computed for hot plumes (Jaunet 2019 note). That a reflector or foam can kill a tone (Henderson 1993, p. 318–319; Weightman et al. 2019, p. 434–441) adds the acoustic environment near the nozzle (vehicle base, launch mount) as a candidate (Henderson 1993 note). Hot, overexpanded subscale jets on normal and inclined plates at 2–10 D_e, with time-resolved schlieren and bicoherence on near-pad microphones, would discriminate.

## Methods and data
- **Point acoustics (time-resolved):** long records (20–30 s at 200 kHz) over fine Mach sweeps, analysed with PSD maps, thresholded bicoherence and bump-wavelet scalograms (μ = 5, σ = 0.6), are cheap and decisive for mapping regimes and extinction curves, but one microphone cannot resolve azimuthal mode (Jordan 2018, p. 3; Stavropoulos 2026, p. 3–4; Jaunet 2019, p. 2). Pre-whitening (notching the tone before correlating) separates the two legs, and the side peaks of one sensor's autocorrelation give the round-trip time (Ho 1981, p. 123–131). Block-averaged spectra cannot tell coexistence from switching (Henderson 1993, p. 318).
- **Non-time-resolved PIV:** phase from a microphone reference (Henderson 2002, p. 2, 5) or from a POD pair's phase portrait (Weightman et al. 2019, p. 430–432). Phase-binned averages of total velocity then time emission, convection and arrival at the lip to within the bin (5° sectors; Weightman 2017, p. 8–9) and keep shocks and acoustic fronts, which phase reconstructions lose (Weightman 2019, p. 102–105); fronts fall into the random part of a POD triple decomposition (p. 92–93). About 500 snapshots converge the leading pair in resonant flows and about 2500 are needed when no mode dominates (p. 56–57). Single snapshots give shock strength via Rankine–Hugoniot, smeared by particle lag (Edgington-Mitchell 2025, p. 10).
- **Time-resolved schlieren:** the thesis camera gave at most 102 frames, enough for visualisation and a streamwise–temporal Fourier split but not spectral decomposition (Weightman 2019, p. 24–26, 109–110); the 2025 paper tracked waves frame by frame at 50–200 kfps with correlation-based phase averages whose templates are not stated (p. 7, 18, 20). **No paper in this thread uses SPOD, BMD or HPOD.**
- **LES:** two impinging-jet LES are now in the library. Bogey and Gojon (relaxation-filter LES of an ideally expanded jet) use single-frequency DFT maps for standing-wave cells and azimuthal phase, and axis and lip-line k–ω spectra (p. 573–585). Karami et al. (the thesis infinite-lip case at Re 5 × 10⁴) use x–t diagrams, St–k_x spectra and zero-lag correlation maps (p. 13–21). Both reproduce measured tones with laminar exit layers. Other impinging LES (Gojon, Varé) appear only as citations.
- **Models:** Powell's equation (under-determined), the vortex-sheet phase criterion with a duct nozzle-reflection model, complex-mode resonance (fragile), the vortex-sheet bands of Tam and Ahuja (reproduced to three digits in the Tam 1990 note, for any temperature ratio), geometrical-acoustics ray tracing (qualitative) and the scattering theory of Nogueira 2024. The only stability analysis of an impinging mean flow is Karami's local, parallel, m = 0 analysis for x ≤ 0.9d, which gets the trend of the most unstable St but not its values (p. 21–25).

## Relevance to our work
- **Thesis data:** a streamwise-wavenumber split of the thin- and infinite-lip POD pairs tests for guided-mode content, and zero-lag correlation maps of the same PIV would give the shear-layer wavelength to cross-check u_c (Karami 2020 note). Band-passed Hilbert envelopes and bicoherence of the 2.5 s microphone records test coexistence (Weightman et al. 2019 note). Appendix A's eight-condition matrix, built for an LES comparison that never appeared, is a ready validation set with non-resonant cases (Weightman 2019, p. 145).
- **New time-resolved schlieren:** long records of thin- and infinite-lip jets on cylinder and hemisphere would allow SPOD at the tone with the k_x sign separating upstream waves, phase continuity across the shear layer, BMD of the difference tone and the shock-like fronts' harmonics, and the SPOD → reconstruction → HPOD pipeline for cycle-to-cycle Δt and mode or wave-form switching. Fine NPR and z/D microphone sweeps with bicoherence should come first. Ho's M 0.9 stage table and Jaunet's maps are ready validation sets for a dispersive phase model (Ho 1981 and Jaunet 2019 notes).
- **PyFR LES:** nozzle exterior geometry is part of the boundary condition and must be modelled in any LES, free or impinging (Weightman 2019, p. 89). Reduced-Re, laminar-exit LES reproduce measured impinging tones (Bogey 2017, p. 572–577; Karami 2020, p. 18), indirect support for the PyFR set-up; Karami et al. needed fine resolution at the lip edge to capture receptivity (p. 6), and axis k–ω spectra as in Bogey 2017 would show the (0, 2) guided mode in the A1/A2 runs (Bogey 2017 note).
- **Rocket noise:** impinging-jet waves are shocks at birth, linking to crackle; the open question is why the loop does not survive in hot, overexpanded plumes on inclined deflectors, for which the guided-mode bands can now be computed with the Tam 1990 machinery.
