# Impinging jets and edge tones

_Last updated: 2026-10-24 · 28 papers_

## Scope
Self-sustained tones from jets that meet a surface: subsonic and supersonic round, plug and rectangular jets on plates, cylinders and hemispheres; subsonic jets grazing a plate edge (jet–edge or jet–flap tones); and how the nozzle exterior and other reflecting surfaces close such loops. Edge, hole and cavity tones appear only as the classical background ([Rockwell 1979](../Notes/rockwell-1979-self-sustained-oscillations-impinging-shear-layers.md)). It also covers impingement at launch-vehicle scale, where the question is why the laboratory tones disappear. Free-jet screech is on [screech](screech.md) and its staging on [screech-staging-and-mode-switching](screech-staging-and-mode-switching.md); vortex-sheet dispersion relations, saddle points and guided-jet-wave theory on [linear-models](linear-models.md); LES numerics on [simulation-methods](simulation-methods.md); broadband pad and deflector noise on [rocket-noise-models](rocket-noise-models.md) and [rocket-noise-measurements](rocket-noise-measurements.md); POD-pair practice on [modal-decomposition](modal-decomposition.md). Tone frequencies are collected in `Data/impinging_tones.csv` (see [Data/README](../Data/README.md)).

## Current understanding
1. **Every tone here is a Powell-type feedback loop**: a KH wavepacket goes downstream, the surface region launches an upstream wave, and receptivity at the lip closes the loop, the four-event loop common to all impinging shear layers (Rockwell 1979, p. 68–69; [Edgington-Mitchell 2019](../Notes/edgington-mitchell-2019-aeroacoustic-resonance-review.md), p. 120–121). The legs were first measured in a subsonic jet, in phase at the plate and an integer number of cycles apart at the lip ([Ho 1981](../Notes/ho-1981-dynamics-impinging-jet-feedback-phenomenon.md), p. 124–133); LES shows the loop as a standing wave whose cell count equals Powell's N ([Bogey 2017](../Notes/bogey-2017-feedback-loop-upwind-waves-ideally-expanded-impinging-jets.md), p. 573–576). A phase condition sets the frequency, either Powell's timing equation ([Henderson 2002](../Notes/henderson-2002-flow-structure-supersonic-impinging-jets.md), eq. 1, p. 5; [Weightman 2017](../Notes/weightman-2017-phase-lag-supersonic-jet-impingement.md), eq. 1.1, p. 2) or its dispersive form Δk_r L + φ = 2nπ ([Jordan 2018](../Notes/jordan-2018-jet-flap-interaction-tones.md), p. 7–9; [Jaunet 2019](../Notes/jaunet-2019-dynamics-round-jet-impingement.md), p. 3; [Stavropoulos 2026](../Notes/stavropoulos-2026-jet-edge-interaction.md), p. 5); with one dominant source, screech obeys the same condition as an edge tone with h = (N + p)s ([Raman 1999](../Notes/raman-1999-supersonic-jet-screech-half-century-powell-present.md), p. 555). The constant-speed form counts stages and can be tuned to any one tone; only the dispersive form follows the trends (Jaunet 2019, p. 7; Jordan 2018, p. 4–5, 9).
2. **What carries the upstream wave depends on where it is born.** The guided-mode school starts with [Tam 1990](../Notes/tam-1990-theoretical-model-discrete-tone-impinging-jets.md): the return is an upstream neutral mode of the jet, and it must fall in the KH band (p. 72–75, 81–82). Sound generated inside the core can only travel upstream as a guided-jet (or duct-like) mode, within that mode's band; sound generated outside travels as a free-stream wave ([Nogueira 2024](../Notes/nogueira-2024-guided-jet-waves.md), p. 31–33; [Edgington-Mitchell 2025](../Notes/edgington-mitchell-2025-vortices-shocks-nonlinear-waves-impinging-jet-resonance.md), p. 3–4, 15–17). Subsonic jet–edge tones are closed by guided modes (Jordan 2018, p. 4–10; Stavropoulos 2026, p. 8–18), and so, on band-limit and LES evidence, are low-St axisymmetric impinging tones (Jaunet 2019, p. 7; Bogey 2017, p. 579–585). The group's supersonic impinging jets are often closed by external waves ([Weightman et al. 2019](../Notes/weightman-2019-nozzle-external-geometry-azimuthal-mode-impinging-jet.md), p. 440–441; [Weightman 2019](../Notes/weightman-2019-sound-production-impinging-supersonic-jets.md), p. 89; Edgington-Mitchell 2019, p. 172). Both paths were reported by 1979: inside the core in subsonic impinging jets, outside it in underexpanded ones (Rockwell 1979, p. 73–74, 87–88).
3. **Supersonic impinging jets have several sources, all driven by the same KH vortex**: stand-off-shock leakage, a transient wall-jet shocklet (29–40 µs delay), vortex–wall sound in the core, and possibly the oscillating wall-jet boundary. Two can act within one cycle (Edgington-Mitchell 2025, p. 14–24; Weightman 2017, p. 4–10; Weightman 2019, p. 109–122; Henderson 2002, p. 4–6), and no one source is universal (Edgington-Mitchell 2019, p. 166–167). The stand-off shock oscillates at the tone frequency and can split ([Powis 2016](../Notes/powis-2016-impingement-tones-shock-instabilities-plug-nozzle.md), p. 6–7), yet an ideally expanded jet resonates without one ([Krothapalli 1999](../Notes/krothapalli-1999-flow-field-noise-supersonic-impinging-jet.md), p. 177). An LES of the thesis infinite-lip case puts the strongest source where vortices hit the wall, 0.8–1.1d off axis ([Karami 2020](../Notes/karami-2020-acoustic-hydrodynamic-waves-underexpanded-impinging-jets.md), p. 9–12). Leakage works as in free-jet screech, at a saddle point between vortices once the vortex has passed, with vortex strength setting where ([Edgington-Mitchell 2021](../Notes/edgington-mitchell-2021-generation-screech-tones-shock-leakage.md), p. 23–30; Edgington-Mitchell 2025, p. 18).
4. **The upstream waves are weak shocks at birth** (M_s ≈ 1.07 from a PIV velocity jump), as free-jet leakage fronts are (Edgington-Mitchell 2021, p. 3–4, 18–19); sharp and diffuse forms alternate from cycle to cycle, and pairs can merge (Edgington-Mitchell 2025, p. 10–13). LES near-field pressure at the tone has the broad wavenumber content of such sharp fronts (Karami 2020, p. 15).
5. **Geometry at either end of the loop decides whether a tone exists and which mode it takes.** Changing only the lip switches an NPR 3.4, z/D 5 jet from helical to toroidal, and foam on the outer base removes the helical tone (Weightman et al. 2019, p. 428–441); a reflector a set fraction of a wavelength behind the exit suppresses or switches tones ([Henderson 1993](../Notes/henderson-1993-tones-choked-jet-impinging-flat-plates.md), p. 318–319); a plug nozzle gives no tones for z/D < 3.2, where round nozzles resonate (Powis 2016, p. 5); and at the plate end a convex cylinder locks the flapping plane while a concave one silences the jet for NPR 2.9–3.9 ([Mason-Smith 2015](../Notes/mason-smith-2015-underexpanded-jet-impinging-cylindrical-sections.md), p. 614, 617). These tests show which surfaces are necessary; none shows whether receptivity, the path or the source was changed (notes on all four).
6. **The band in which the upstream wave exists bounds the tones.** Round impinging tones die at the upper limit S(0,1) of the m = 0 guided mode (Jaunet 2019, p. 7); jet–edge tones end at the trapping and saddle cut-offs (Jordan 2018, p. 10–14; Stavropoulos 2026, p. 15–18) of the waves of [Towne 2017](../Notes/towne-2017-acoustic-resonance-potential-core-subsonic-jets.md) (p. 31–33). The KH band limit explains the low-Mach cut-off and the axisymmetry of subsonic impinging tones (Tam 1990, p. 81–82, 85), the bands order the alternating axisymmetric and helical tones of an LES (Bogey 2017, p. 577–582), and planar bands bound the varicose and flapping tones of rectangular jets ([Tam 1992](../Notes/tam-1992-impingement-tones-large-aspect-ratio-rectangular-jets.md), p. 310). As a test of closure path the bands are weak (see "Band test").
7. **Staging, switching and nonlinear selection are generic.** Stages and jumps collapse across edge, hole, cavity and jet–plate flows (Rockwell 1979, p. 78–80). Integer-N stages with intermittent switching at stage boundaries occur in subsonic (Ho 1981, p. 132–135) and supersonic impinging jets, where multiple tones are "the rule rather than the exception" (Henderson 1993, p. 312, 318). Two loops can run at once and fill the spectrum with combination tones (Tam 1992, p. 305–308). In jet–edge flows several inharmonic, uncoupled tones can give way abruptly to one saturated oscillator (Jordan 2018, p. 5; Stavropoulos 2026, p. 8–15).
8. **Receptivity at the lip is fast and local.** The arriving wave makes a short-wavelength transient (λ ≈ 0.05d) that dies out by x/d ≈ 0.3, the new vortex appears at its trailing edge, and there is no low-frequency "collective interaction" ([Mitchell 2012](../Notes/mitchell-2012-visualization-acoustic-feedback-loop-impinging-jet-schlieren.md), p. 337–340; Karami 2020, p. 12–13, 21–25; Edgington-Mitchell 2019, p. 174).
9. **No impingement tones are seen at launch-vehicle scale**; pad noise is broadband, dominated by ground-reflected Mach waves and flame-duct sources ([Lubert 2022](../Notes/lubert-2022-supersonic-jet-noise-launch-vehicles-review.md), p. 16–20; [Tsutsumi 2015](../Notes/tsutsumi-2015-epsilon-liftoff-acoustics.md), p. 13–16; [Panda 2013](../Notes/panda-2013-noise-sources-rocket-test-firings-launch-phased-array.md), p. 17), and LES of a supersonic jet on a 45° plate is broadband too ([Nonomura 2011](../Notes/nonomura-2011-supersonic-jet-impinging-inclined-plate-acoustic-waves.md), p. 403, 418).

## The feedback equation and the phase lag
Rockwell and Naudascher already doubted the phase term: the 1/4 in the classical 2π(n + 1/4) "has never been rigorously justified", measured jet–edge phases differ substantially from it, mixing-layer–edge and cavity flows give about 2π(n + 1/2), moving the edge across the layer changes it, and cycle-to-cycle jitter calls for instantaneous phase measurements (p. 75–76). The classic jet–edge stage ratios fit n − 1/4 with U_c ≈ 0.6U (Rockwell 1979 note). Raman's edge-tone analogy carries the condition to screech: with one dominant shock source the amplitude criterion drops out and the nozzle-to-edge distance becomes h = (N + p)s, which reproduces Powell's screech formula for any N + p (Raman 1999, p. 555; note's check). Obstacles beside an on-design rectangular jet set up such a supersonic edge tone ("induced screech"), raising downstream mass flux by about 30% at a thrust penalty near 20%, and Euler simulations matched its frequency, amplitude and thrust loss (p. 562–563).

Ho and Nosseir separated the legs in a cold M 0.3–0.9 jet on a large plate: structures convect at 0.62U and upstream waves travel at a∞ (p. 124–129), and the legs are in phase at the plate and 5 × 2π apart at the lip (M 0.8, x0/d 4; p. 132–133), so p = 0, the form the thesis uses (Weightman 2019, eq. 1.3, p. 6). Integer N gives the M 0.9 staircase (N = 2–6; Table 1, p. 134–135), but constant speeds give integer N only at stage ends: mid-stage the count is 0.4–0.7 low (note's reading of Fig. 14a), the "frequency squeezing" Jordan 2018 attributes to a dispersive upstream wave (p. 4–5, 9). Henderson and Powell's constants (p = 0, M_con,u = 0.73, h″/d = 0.8) count N = 2–8 near integers (p. 314) but cannot give the small-plate tones' log–log slopes (note's check), and Mason-Smith et al.'s tone branches miss integers by up to about 0.5 with these constants or with u_con = 0.55U_j (Mason-Smith 2015 note).

Supersonic loops seem to need a lag. Krothapalli et al. fired an ideally expanded M 1.5 jet (NPR 3.7) through a lift plate ten exit diameters across onto a ground plane. Single-snapshot PIV gives structure speeds C_i/U_clm of 0.52 at h/d = 4, rising linearly to 0.56 at h/d = 10 (0.60 for the free jet), and 0.56–0.59 with shock cells at NPR 2.5 and 5.0 (Fig. 10, p. 167). With these speeds p = 0 misses the staged tones and p = −0.4 fits them at NPR 3.7 and 5.0 (Figs 20–21, p. 176–178). The jet has no stand-off shock (smooth centreline, p. 171–172), so the authors reject Henderson and Powell's shock source and blame receptivity at a lip that is also a reflecting plate, while conceding that p is a best fit that cannot be separated from convection-speed error (p. 177–178). In the note's reading of 33 dominant tones the fractional part of N + p has a circular mean of −0.42 with about ±0.2-stage scatter, and N + p creeps upwards within each stage, as in Ho's jet; a C_i 14% lower would make p = 0 work, and Bogey and Gojon's LES fits the same tones with p = 0, u_c 0.58–0.62u_j and different N (Krothapalli 1999 note; Bogey 2017, p. 571, 575–577). Henderson et al.'s NPR 4.03 cycle gave N = 4, p ≈ +0.4, with a first-leg convection Mach number borrowed from Powell (1988) and Henderson 1993 (Henderson 2002, p. 2–6). Powis et al.'s plug-nozzle stages follow Powell's equation with θ = 2–6 and p = 0.85 (Fig. 8, p. 4–5), i.e. N = 3–7 with p = −0.15 in the −1 ≤ p ≤ 0 convention; no u_c is given, and the note's back-calculation gives 0.65–0.74 of the isentropic U_j (Powis 2016 note).

Weightman et al. (2019) show how loosely the equation constrains a loop. With p = 0 and measured U_c/U_j = 0.44–0.48, the direct, inner-base and outer-base paths each reproduce both tone families (St ≈ 0.25 and 0.39) for some integer N, and the allowed tones are only ΔSt = 0.034–0.065 apart, so matches within 0.02 cannot pick a path (p. 442–445; note's arithmetic); the gain criterion that could (p. 423) is never quantified. Weightman et al. (2017) then measured every term for a cylinder case (NPR 3.6, z/D 3.5): N = 4, u_c(x) from the POD phase average, U_s = 331 m/s, and a delay Δt = 29.4 µs between shocklet inception and emission (p ≈ −0.24), predicting 8230 Hz against 8300 Hz measured (+5.4% with Δt = 0); on a hemisphere at NPR 5.0 the schlieren gives Δt = 40 µs directly, p = −0.49 (p. 5, 7–10). The 0.8% agreement is largely built in, since Δt is the residual that closes the loop with the same u_c and path (Weightman 2017 note). The independent evidence is the 0.24-cycle deficit at Δt = 0, which a mean u_c of 0.43U_j would remove. The paper rejects that value (p. 10), but the measured local u_c spans 0.42–0.52U_j and is valid only over 0.75 < x/D < 2.6 (p. 8), and on the isentropic U_j (≈ 420 rather than 460.8 m/s) the rejected value is 0.47U_j (note). Krothapalli's lag, read as a delay, is 70–175 µs, 2–6 times the shocklet's, in a jet with neither stand-off shock nor shocklet, so a negative p is not by itself evidence for the shocklet (Krothapalli 1999 note).

The convection speed is the weak link. For the thesis infinite-lip case, LES x–t diagrams give 0.77a₀ ≈ 0.63U_j (Karami 2020, p. 13; note's conversion), close to Bogey 2017 (0.58–0.62u_j, p. 571) and Ho 1981 (0.62U, p. 128–129), against 0.44–0.48U_j from the thesis PIV (Weightman et al. 2019, p. 432–433). Published lags, +0.4 (Henderson), 0 (Gojon et al. 2016, as cited in Weightman 2017, p. 2), −0.15 (Powis), −0.4 (Krothapalli) and −0.24 to −0.49 (Weightman 2017), use different loop definitions; Karami's numbers give N + p ≈ 4.2 and 5.2 at h = 2d and 5d, i.e. p ≈ +0.2 (note's estimate). In the dispersive models the lag becomes the end-condition phase φ, still free until the plate and nozzle reflection coefficients are known (Jaunet 2019, p. 5, 10). Nor can a fitted p yet show which source is active: the thesis times leakage (p ≈ 0) from vortex arrival (Weightman 2019, p. 122) and the shocklet from its inception (Weightman 2017, p. 9), but leakage begins only once the saddle point behind the vortex reaches the shock (Edgington-Mitchell 2025, p. 18; Edgington-Mitchell 2021, p. 26), of order half a cycle after the vortex core (my estimate).

**Dispersive models.** For subsonic edges the Pprime group replaced Powell's constant speeds with vortex-sheet wavenumbers: R₁R₂e^{iΔkL} = 1, in phase only, with φ = 0 or π (Jordan 2018, p. 7–9). The dispersive upstream wave captures frequency squeezing (p. 4–5, 9), and keeping |R₁R₂| (0.002–0.008) bounds the tone-free low-St region but is fragile near saddle points (p. 15–16, 20–24). The same criterion is the screech model of [Mancinelli 2019](../Notes/mancinelli-2019-screech-prediction-upstream-modes.md), in which a plate would simply replace the shock cell as the downstream reflector (p. 2). Tam and Ahuja met the effect from another side: Neuwerth's impinging tones stay near one average St whatever L/D, which they predict as the St of the least-dispersive (0, 1) wave (eq. 25, p. 82–83), falling from 0.73 at M_j 0.70 to 0.36 at 0.95 and matching five data sets, Ho's included (Fig. 16, p. 83). The note shows that this inflection point is where Towne's neutral saddles are born (M_j ≈ 0.81 cold, against 0.82 isothermal in Towne 2017, p. 31–32), so the L-independent average St and frequency squeezing are one effect. Jaunet et al. carried the phase-only model to round impinging jets at M_j 0.7–1.5: free-stream closure can be tuned to any single peak but never fits all of them and predicts tones where none occur, while KH plus the k_p⁻ guided wave follows the trends across M_j and H/D, although no predicted line lands exactly on a peak (p. 7). At supersonic M_j frequencies barely separate the two closures: on k = −ω/a₀ Bogey and Gojon's combined model (eq. 3.6) reduces exactly to the classical one, and at M_j 1.5 the upstream branches travel at 0.93–1.00a₀ (note's checks; the authors note that the tones sit where the waves move at about a₀, p. 582).

## What carries the upstream wave
**Classical evidence.** Rockwell and Naudascher record both closures. In subsonic impinging round jets the feedback travels inside the core, and a streamlined body placed in the core at the exit attenuates it (Evertz, Neuwerth and Wagner; p. 74), the in-core standing wave that Tam and Ahuja read as KH plus the upstream neutral wave (Tam 1990, p. 85). In underexpanded jets, plates that block the outside path stop the feedback, and Neuwerth's NPR 3.0 standing wave with a plate at 6D lies "on the periphery of the jet core" (p. 73–74, 87–88). Their statement that a supersonic core allows no upstream propagation (p. 74) holds for plane sound but not for guided modes (Bogey 2017, p. 583–585), and the core-body test has not been used since (Rockwell 1979 note).

**Guided-mode closure.** Tam and Ahuja proposed that the upstream leg of impinging tones in cold, high-Re round jets is an upstream neutral mode of the vortex-sheet jet (Tam & Hu 1989), with a shape fixed by the mean flow (p. 72–75). Only modes in the KH band, St < 0.6–0.7, can take part: in cold subsonic jets that leaves the axisymmetric (0, 1) mode, while at supersonic M_j the helical (1, 1) mode qualifies too (p. 81–82), and their M_j 1.4 photographs show axisymmetric resonance at L/D 6 and helical at L/D 10 (p. 70–71). The note's reproduction puts the low-Mach cut-off at M_j ≈ 0.72, not the printed 0.65, and finds both grounds for rejecting external closure (p. 84–85) weak: external closure predicts only about an 8% coflow shift, and Ho's 5 × 2π lip count is also met by the guided wave with n = 6–8. Tam and Norum carried the model to Norum's AR 4.24 rectangular jets on a wall (M_j 1.16–1.70): a varicose and a higher flapping basic tone fall "within (or almost within)" the first symmetric and antisymmetric planar bands, whose order explains why the flapping tone is the higher (Tam 1992, p. 308–310). The note finds the test weaker than it looks. The first symmetric band runs from zero, so only its upper limit constrains, and from M_j 1.49 the symmetric tones press against or just above it; two first-band tones need a/s > ≈ 1.4, which one of the paper's own spectra (a/s 1.26) violates; and the flapping tones sit near the branch point, where the upstream phase speed is −a∞, so frequencies cannot separate guided from free-stream return (Tam 1992 note).

For an M 0.6–1.0 jet grazing an edge at L/D = 2–4, KH paired with the upstream jet modes k_TH⁻ (M ≤ 0.82; Tam and Ahuja's (0, 1) branch) and k_p⁻ (0.82 < M < 1) reproduces the tone spacing and Mach trend that free-stream closure cannot (Jordan 2018, p. 4–10). These are the waves trapped in the potential core of a free M 0.9 jet (Towne 2017, p. 14–19, 31–33). The cut-offs are physical: above St ≈ 1 at M ≤ 0.82 the k_TH⁻ wave is confined to the core and transmitted into the nozzle rather than reflected, and above 0.82 the k_p⁻ branch ends at the S2 saddle (Jordan 2018, p. 10–14). That reverses Tam and Ahuja's reading, for whom confinement in the core was the mark of in-jet feedback (p. 84); both can hold if at St ≈ 0.5–0.7 the wave keeps enough amplitude at the lip, but neither paper has a receptivity model (Tam 1990 note).

Jaunet et al. (cold, thin-lipped jet; M_j 0.7–1.5, H/D 2–8; one exit-plane microphone) found that from H/D 4 the tones form ordered families, each dying along a curve that matches the upper limit S(0,1) of the m = 0 guided mode "almost perfect[ly]" (p. 2–3, 7), extending the band cut-offs of Jordan 2018 (p. 12–14) and Mancinelli 2019 (p. 6) to impinging jets. The note adds three qualifications: the plotted S(0,1) is for an isothermal vortex sheet while the jet is cold (the cold band edge is 4–12% higher at M_j 1.0–1.5); many strong supersonic tones lie above S(0,1), e.g. a B-like line at H/D 6–8, which the model cannot produce; and the duct-like upstream waves the paper discards (p. 6–7) are seen, strongly nonlinear, in M 0.86–0.90 impinging jets (Edgington-Mitchell 2025, p. 8) and close jet–edge loops (Stavropoulos 2026, p. 15–18).

Bogey and Gojon supply the LES evidence: four LES of a cold, ideally expanded M_j 1.5 jet (Re 6 × 10⁴, laminar exit, lip 0.1r₀) on a plate at L/D 3–6 reproduce the tones and staging of Krothapalli et al. (p. 572–577). The tones alternate in St bands, axisymmetric below 0.225, helical to 0.355, axisymmetric to 0.5 and helical above, which the classical model cannot explain and the vortex-sheet upstream bands order (p. 577–582); the LES amplitude has the A2 eigenfunction's node near r ≈ 0.5r₀ (p. 583), A1 and A2 there being radial orders of the guided mode, not screech stages. Pressure snapshots and k–ω spectra of the L/D 6 jet show waves travelling upstream inside the supersonic core and on the lip line at about a₀ (p. 583–585). The group's review called this and Gojon et al.'s earlier LES "the strongest evidence to date" for guided closure (Edgington-Mitchell 2019, p. 171–172).

**External closure.** In Weightman et al.'s jets (NPR 3.4, z/D 5, D = 15 mm), foam over the outer base removes the thin-lip helical tone (a weak St 0.472 appears instead), foam over the inner base leaves it, and an infinite foam cap kills the resonance (p. 434–441). The paper concludes that, "for this facility", the loop closes by reflection off the outer base (p. 435, 441); the thesis contrasts these freely propagating waves with the guided modes of Gojon and Bogey's LES (Weightman 2019, p. 89). The foam shows the outer-base path is necessary, not that the direct path contributes nothing: interference of the Henderson 1993 kind is excluded only by where the surfaces sit (p. 441), and two foam cases gave different spectra in the uncaged schlieren rig (p. 426, 442). The group's first impinging paper had assumed that flange reflections do not affect the loop (Mitchell 2012, p. 337), which these tests contradict for the group's rig. A sequence first published in the review (NPR 3.1, z/D 2.66) shows a free-stream wave passing right through the jet, hitting the lip and starting a vortex (Edgington-Mitchell 2019, Fig. 38, p. 172). The classical external school is no more decisive. Ho and Nosseir measured only outside the jet (r/d > 2.5) and set aside Wagner's in-core standing wave (p. 120), and their data cannot tell sound from a guided wave near sonic speed (Ho 1981 note). Karami et al.'s LES shows shear-layer upstream waves at ≈ −a₀, but the authors say free-stream and guided waves cannot be told apart there, and they do not look inside the core (p. 14–16); their remark that a guided mode in a supersonic core is "unexpected" (p. 3) is contradicted by Tam 1990 (p. 77–79), [Edgington-Mitchell 2018](../Notes/edgington-mitchell-2018-upstream-acoustic-modes-screech-closure.md) and Nogueira 2024.

**Reconciliation.** Nogueira et al. treat the guided mode as a scattering problem: a plane wave arriving from outside and travelling directly upstream is cancelled at the shear layer, while sound generated inside is totally reflected over a band (Nogueira 2024, p. 14–22). Screech always needs the mode because its KH–shock source lies inside the sonic line; impingement needs it only when the source is in the core (p. 31–33). Edgington-Mitchell et al. (2025) supply the experiments: vortex–wall sound peaks on the axis, so it is an in-core source that must pass the guided-mode filter, while the shocklet and stand-off-shock leakage sit outside the core (p. 8, 14–17); in some Bogey and Gojon LES cases the pressure inside and outside the jet is almost uncorrelated, which rules out the guided mode there (p. 8). The "two schools" were describing different mechanisms, which can coexist within one cycle (p. 25), as the 2019 review had argued: acoustic jet modes "are not always the closure mechanism" (Edgington-Mitchell 2019, p. 176). The classical papers fit this: Tam and Ahuja's subsonic loop is driven by structures hitting the wall inside the core, and their supersonic modes lie "outside the jet but guided by it" (Tam 1990, p. 79). Bogey and Gojon's ideally expanded jets have no strong stand-off shock, so leakage and the shocklet may simply be absent; the paper says only that the waves "seem to be generated in the region of jet impingement" (p. 566–567), and Nogueira's in-core reading of it (p. 33) is an interpretation (Bogey 2017 note).

**Band test.** At M_j 1.42 the cold vortex-sheet bands are (0, 1) St 0–0.22, (0, 2) 0.42–0.53 and (1, 1) 0.369–0.371: the thesis helical tones (0.241–0.255) lie 31–35% below the (1, 1) cut-off and the toroidal ones (0.383–0.385) 9–10% below (0, 2) (Tam 1990 note), and both lie above S(0,1) ≈ 0.19–0.21 (Jaunet 2019 note). Mason-Smith et al.'s jets repeat the pattern: the flat-plate axisymmetric tone at NPR 3.0 (St_j ≈ 0.46) lies inside (0, 2) and the flapping tones at NPR 3.4 (0.32–0.34) 9–13% below (1, 1) (Mason-Smith 2015 note). In the LES every axisymmetric tone lies inside A1 or A2, or within 6.4% below A2, but six of seven helical tones lie 11–26% below H1 or H2 (Bogey 2017 note). On band evidence, then, guided closure is plausible for the axisymmetric tones and unproven for the helical ones in both schools' data, though a thick shear layer lowers the bands and shocks are ignored; the LES's helical k–ω evidence is at r = r₀, where grazing external sound at a₀ would look the same within its ΔkD ≈ 1.3 (Bogey 2017 note).

Two caveats remain. Nogueira's argument concerns whether an external wave can *create* a guided mode, whereas external closure works by direct receptivity at the lip, so it constrains in-core sources without proving external closure; Ho's upstream waves also arrive obliquely (θ_a ≈ 25–35°), not straight upstream (Ho 1981 note). And the foam and reflector tests show the external path is *necessary*, but the thesis PIV has never been split by streamwise wavenumber (as in Edgington-Mitchell 2018) to look for a guided component too.

## Where the upstream wave is generated
Powell (1988) separated small plates, where tones occur only when the plate sits in an expansion and the oscillating stand-off shock is the source (a Hartmann–Sprenger analogy), from large plates with the source at the plate (Edgington-Mitchell 2019, p. 141–142). Henderson and Powell's small-plate tones (D/d ≲ 2–4, from NPR 3.04) have steeper slopes, a stand-off shock oscillating streamwise by up to d/3 and only symmetric disturbances (Henderson 1993, p. 316–318, 322–324); their 1996 sequel found these tones insensitive to an upstream reflector, so the loop runs between plate and stand-off shock, as in the entropy-wave linear models of Mørch and of Kuo and Dowling (1996) (Edgington-Mitchell 2019, p. 143). On a large plate Henderson et al. describe an axially oscillating Mach disk, an annular shock that moves and collapses, and a wall jet oscillating strongly at r ≈ 2.6R (≈ 1.3d), proposed as an annular simple source (p. 3–6). At h/d = 3.00, in a zone of silence, a second stand-off shock forms and the recirculation survives without a tone (p. 4), and the journal version found that recirculation does not correlate with tones (Edgington-Mitchell 2019, p. 144). Henderson and Powell's single-spark schlieren had put the fronts in the wall jet only at very small spacing, and "in the impingement region" at larger ones (Henderson 1993, p. 321–322).

Powis et al. tracked the stand-off shock itself, by edge detection on 500 kfps schlieren of an overexpanded plug-nozzle jet at NPR 3.4. In toroidal cases (z/D 3.56–5.0) it moves normal to the plate at the tone frequency, within the 10–15% scatter of 20 two-cycle bursts; helical cases move it sideways and tilt it (p. 5–6). Its mean standoff (s/D 0.43–0.55) follows the equivalent free jet's shock cells, supporting Henderson et al.'s 2005 hypothesis (p. 6). Within one cell a larger standoff goes with a smaller amplitude (A/D 0.03–0.14), which the authors attribute, untested, to a local Hartmann-type shock–plate loop (p. 6–7); but at equal s/D 0.47 the amplitude doubles from z/D 3.64 to 4.80, so standoff is not the controlling variable (Powis 2016 note). At z/D 4.80 the shock splits into a staircase of weaker shocks and briefly vanishes each cycle (Fig. 14, p. 7), which the review equates with free-jet shock splitting (Edgington-Mitchell 2019, p. 145, 147). That is image-level support for the multi-front picture at the stand-off shock (leakage and split fronts, below), though no outgoing wave is tracked, and the panel timings of the paper's 250 kfps loop sequence (p. 5) imply a returning wave at about 3a∞ and need checking (note). Krothapalli et al.'s jet is the opposite case: no stand-off shock at NPR 3.7, yet strong tones, with front curvature placing the source in the stagnation region (Fig. 4a, p. 164, 177), consistent with an in-core vortex–wall source (Edgington-Mitchell 2025, p. 14–15).

Weightman added two mechanisms. A streamwise–temporal Fourier split of 500 kfps schlieren shows an upstream wave emitted at the stand-off shock, and ray tracing on POD phase-reconstructed PIV puts leakage there with no delay, matching the schlieren in location and timing (Weightman 2019, p. 109–122); the published paper rests that source and p = 0 on the schlieren alone (Weightman et al. 2019, p. 443). The ray tracing is qualitative (scale separation violated at convective Mach ≈ 1, c = 340 m/s, no directivity or amplitude), and leakage depends strongly on vortex amplitude (none at Ã = 0.2, sideline at 0.7), which the phase reconstruction smooths (Weightman 2019, p. 112–117, 162). In the same thin-lip case, snapshots with a strong helical mode also have a thicker shear layer from the first shock reflection (x/D ≈ 1.4) on, with a 27% spread of vorticity thickness between quintiles at x/D 3.6 and large vorticity packets only in strong snapshots ([Weightman 2018](../Notes/weightman-2018-signatures-shear-layer-unsteadiness-pod.md), p. 8–9), so strong and weak cycles differ in mean flow as well as amplitude. The second mechanism is the shocklet. On a hemisphere at NPR 5.0 a wall-jet shock forms as a vortex passes, rotates towards normal to the wall, creeps upstream and releases the returning wave shortly before the next vortex arrives (Weightman 2017, p. 4–6); it was also seen, though not shown, at other NPR and on flat plates (p. 5). On the cylinder it sits at (x/D, y/D) = (3.78, 1.43) (p. 8), close to Henderson's 1.3D source. The article says the shocklet "appears to differ" from that source (p. 5), the thesis calls it the first mechanistic description of it (Weightman 2019, p. 136), and Edgington-Mitchell 2025 keeps them separate (p. 23–24). The 2019 review cites stand-off-shock-edge sources in other cases, and Gojon et al.'s impingement-region source that needs no lag, as evidence for "at least two different" mechanisms (Edgington-Mitchell 2019, p. 166–167).

Karami et al.'s LES of the thesis infinite-lip case (NPR 3.4, a hole in a flat wall, plate at h = 2d and 5d) puts the strongest upstream waves where coherent structures hit the wall, 0.8d and 1.1d from the axis, located by tracing cylindrical fronts back (p. 9, 12). That lies between the on-axis vortex–wall source of Edgington-Mitchell 2025 (p. 14–15) and the wall-jet sources at 1.3d (Henderson) and 1.43D (shocklet). Waves from vortex–oblique-shock and stand-off-shock interaction, resembling leakage, are present but weaker (p. 12); the source is placed by eye (Karami 2020 note). In Bogey and Gojon's LES, circular waves centred near z ≈ 3r₀, made where upstream waves meet shear-layer structures (p. 567), might be a leakage-like escape of the trapped wave (Bogey 2017 note, speculation).

Edgington-Mitchell et al. (2021) published the leakage method for free jets. Their k < 0-filtered 1 Mfps schlieren of screeching A2, B and twin jets shows the shock tilting upstream as a vortex arrives and a small arc of its tip separating as one sharp front (p. 13–19). Ray tracing puts the first leak at the 3rd or 4th cell, at a saddle point rather than the vorticity peak (p. 19–28), and rescaling the reconstruction to r̄ ± 2σ of the POD-pair amplitude moves the first leak at NPR 2.25 from about the 3rd cell (strong vortices) to the 6th (weak) (p. 28–30). The impinging version is still unpublished (Edgington-Mitchell 2025, p. 17).

Edgington-Mitchell et al. (2025) widened the evidence to four nozzles (round and AR 2 rectangular, converging and C-D), three surface shapes and NPR 2.5–6 (p. 5–7). The shocklet appears to close the loop consistently when NPR ≥ 4.5 and z/D ≤ 4.0, and is not visible for NPR ≤ 3.0 in most datasets, perhaps because the wall jet hides it (p. 15–17). Leakage shows a split front, not seen in screeching jets, as the stronger vortices turn part of it back into the core (p. 18); Henderson's source is hypothesised to produce diffuse waves that coalesce (p. 23–24). When one vortex makes two strong waves, usually only one reaches the lip with useful strength, so some measured tonal components are by-products (p. 24–25), a mechanism-level form of Henderson and Powell's worry about sum and difference tones (p. 324). No clean map of mechanism against NPR, z/D or azimuthal mode exists (p. 12–13, 25). The same paper weakens two thesis inferences. An in-core shock from vortex–wall impact would displace the stand-off shock and look like leakage in schlieren, so the thin-lip leakage identification is plausible but not unique (p. 18). And the quantitative shocklet test was at NPR 3.6 (Weightman 2017, p. 7–10), below the regime where the shocklet is found to close the loop consistently; the directly timed hemisphere case (NPR 5.0) sits inside it.

## Mode selection: nozzle, surface and azimuthal mode
At NPR 3.4, z/D 5 the mean flow barely changes with the lip, but the tones do: the thin lip (t/D 0.1) gives one helical tone at St 0.255; the infinite lip (t/D 7) a toroidal tone at 0.385, a helical one at 0.241 and their difference at 0.144 (Weightman et al. 2019, p. 428–433). The intermediate lip (t/D 0.33) should entrain like the thin lip but behaves like the infinite one (0.383, 0.253), so entrainment is rejected in favour of the acoustic path (p. 433–439), though the relevant entrainment may lie closer to the exit than the PIV resolves (p. 439). Nearly every configuration gives St ≈ 0.25 or 0.39, assumed but not shown to be the base flow's most unstable frequencies (p. 441, 446). The thin-lip tone is one helical mode whose amplitude varies, not two modes alternating: one POD pair holds 33.7% of the energy, no symmetric pair appears in 20 modes, the spectrum has one peak and a spectrogram shows a nearly constant tone (Weightman 2018, p. 6, 9), although one plane cannot strictly separate a helix from precessing flapping (Weightman 2018 note). The lip switch is not universal: at NPR 3.0, z/D 3.5 both lips give a toroidal pair, and at NPR 4.2, z/D 2 neither gives a dominant mode (Weightman 2019, p. 147–155). An LES of the infinite-lip case reproduces the toroidal tone (St_j 0.39 against 0.385, with m = 0 holding ≈ 65% of the shear-layer energy; Karami 2020, p. 18, 28), but shows no spectrum and does not address the helical or difference tones (Karami 2020 note).

Henderson and Powell's choked nozzle (t/d ≈ 0.28) makes the large-plate L1 line symmetrical and L2/L3 helical (p. 322–323), as Tam and Ahuja predicted for supersonic M_j (Tam 1990, p. 82), while Henderson's 0.5d lip gave only L1 (Henderson 2002, p. 2–3). Krothapalli et al.'s jet issues through its lift plate, in effect an infinite lip, and is helical at h/d ≈ 8 and 3 but axisymmetric at h/d ≈ 4–6 (p. 163); its dominant tones cluster at St ≈ 0.35–0.38 at the axisymmetric heights, 0.25–0.30 at the helical ones and 0.14–0.20 in between (note's conversion), matching Bogey and Gojon's alternating bands and the thesis's 0.25 and 0.385 families. Tam and Ahuja's axisymmetry rule for subsonic jets holds cleanly only below M_j ≈ 0.85 (Tam 1990 note), and their prediction that subsonic twin impinging jets should not couple (p. 70–71) is untested (see [twin-jet-coupling](twin-jet-coupling.md)).

The nozzle type matters beyond the lip. A truncated plug nozzle (annular throat, M_d 1.94, 1 mm lip) run overexpanded at NPR 3.4 gives staged, mostly toroidal tones (flapping never appears) but **none for z/D < 3.2**, where round convergent (Powell 1988, NPR 3.38, z/D ≤ 1.7) and C-D nozzles (Krothapalli 1999, NPR 3.7, z/D ≤ 2.0) resonate (Powis 2016, p. 4–5). The authors blame the annular near field, with its inner shear layer and plug recirculation, without testing it (p. 5); with a thin conventional lip, receptivity is an unlikely cause, so the source or path must differ (Powis 2016 note). Free-jet screech also responds to the lip, and Ponton's proposed mechanism, upstream sound reflecting off the larger exit face, is the same acoustic-path idea ([Ponton 1992](../Notes/ponton-1992-nozzle-exit-lip-thickness-plume-resonance.md), p. 536–548, not tagged). Upstream reflectors detune screech ([Raman 1998](../Notes/raman-1998-supersonic-jet-screech-review.md), p. 75–78, not tagged) or cancel it at odd multiples of λ/4 from the exit (Raman 1999, p. 557), as Henderson and Powell's reflectors do for impingement tones, and an upstream plate on a Monash rig altered free-jet staging ([Edgington-Mitchell 2022](../Notes/edgington-mitchell-2022-unifying-theory-jet-screech.md), p. 6, 16, not tagged). The plate changes the selection too: the same t/D 0.33 lip gives a helical C-mode free jet at NPR 3.4 (St 0.30; [Edgington-Mitchell 2014](../Notes/edgington-mitchell-2014-coherent-structure-helical-screech-mode.md), not tagged) but a toroidal-dominant jet with a plate at 5D (Weightman et al. 2019, p. 434, 437).

The impingement surface matters as much. On a convex half-cylinder (radius 2.5d, h/d 3.5) the flapping jet at NPR 3.4 stays in the plane normal to the cylinder axis, as two-view two-point correlations of schlieren show, whereas on a flat plate the flapping plane precesses (Mason-Smith 2015, p. 617). That is why the group's later PIV used cylinders (Weightman 2019, p. 29–30; Edgington-Mitchell 2025, p. 6–7); Weightman 2017's stationary POD pair at NPR 3.6 confirms the locking (p. 7–8), and the two papers' tones agree to 1.3% (Mason-Smith 2015 note). A **concave** half-cylinder at the same h gives no tone for NPR 2.9–3.9 while flat and convex plates resonate, so this zone of silence does not depend on where the plate sits in the free-jet shock train, unlike Henderson's (p. 614, 618). The authors favour lower shear-layer receptivity caused by the wall jet turned back into the entrainment field, citing a coefficient of variation of entrainment-region schlieren intensity of 0.2 against 0.06 (Table 1, p. 620–621). That comparison is at another condition (h/d 4.5, NPR 4.0) and measures no thickness, and the paper's own images show a steady stand-off structure with fluctuations confined to the shocks (p. 614), like Henderson's silent case (Henderson 2002, p. 4), so a source-side explanation fits as well (Mason-Smith 2015 note). A hemisphere shows the shocklet best, and every phenomenon also appears, less clearly, on flat plates (Edgington-Mitchell 2025, p. 6–7). Small plates give their own tone class and intermediate ones (D/d 1–2) often greatly reduce tonal activity (Henderson 1993, p. 314–317); inclined plates offer many feedback path lengths, and steady microjets have more authority over helical than toroidal impinging instabilities (Edgington-Mitchell 2019, p. 147, 152).

One coincidence has not been followed up: the thin-lip impinging tone (St 0.255 at NPR 3.4) is close to free-jet helical C-mode screech at NPR 3.40 in the same group's D = 15 mm nozzle family (St 0.26, [Edgington-Mitchell 2020](../Notes/edgington-mitchell-2020-waves-in-screeching-jets.md), not tagged; 0.30 for the same PIV case in Edgington-Mitchell 2021, p. 6). Tam and Norum's flapping tone (St = fH/u_j ≈ 0.20–0.21 at M_j 1.29) likewise lies where free-jet flapping screech of that nozzle would be (Tam 1992 note), and Henderson and Powell remark that some of Norum's stages sit near screech (Henderson 1993, p. 312). Whether helical or flapping impinging tones are plate-modified screech loops has not been tested.

## Receptivity at the lip
Ho and Nosseir found that near the lip the signal carries the initial instability (St 3–5, ∝ M^{1/2}) on top of the tone, and is almost purely the tone by x ≈ 1.3d. They proposed "collective interaction", forced multiple vortex merging, as the receptivity mechanism, explained the high-M onset of resonance by the forcing amplitude it needs (p. 135–139), and claimed the resonance is then "independent of the initial conditions" (p. 140), which the lip results above contradict for supersonic jets. Mitchell et al. gave the first time-resolved images of a supersonic loop (NPR 3.2, z/d 4; schlieren at 250–500 kfps; p. 335, 337). The arriving wave makes a short-wavelength (λ ≈ 0.05d), sinusoid-like train of bands along the shear layer that convects and dies out by x/d ≈ 0.3, and the vortex-ring precursor appears at their trailing edge at x/d ≈ 0.25 (Figs 5–6, p. 337–340). No slow undulation at the loop period appears, the first experimental evidence against collective interaction in a supersonic impinging jet (p. 337, 339). Group schlieren over many conditions has found none since, and its one reported observation (Diebold & Elliott 2014) has not been reproduced (Edgington-Mitchell 2019, p. 174). LES of the thesis case agrees: no collective interaction, pairing (once, occasionally twice) too weak for a tenfold frequency drop, and structures that settle at the loop frequency about 1d from the lip by spatial growth on a rapidly evolving mean flow (Karami 2020, p. 12–13, 21–25). Karami's local m = 0 stability analysis of the LES mean puts the most unstable St near 4 at x = 0.1d, falling to about 0.5 by 0.9d, identically for h = 2d and 5d, whose tones differ twofold (p. 21–23), so a near-lip most-unstable frequency cannot select the tone (Karami 2020 note). The bands' λ ≈ 0.05d (St_j ≈ 10–12 if convected at 0.5–0.6U_j) lies above that LES's unstable range at 0.1d, so either the real lip layer is thinner than the LES inflow or the bands convect more slowly (Mitchell 2012 note).

The receptivity delay is small, though not as small as often quoted. Later group papers cite Mitchell et al. for a delay of at most 2 µs (Weightman 2017, p. 2), but that is Weightman's reading: the printed frames are 10 µs apart and put the bands within about 8 µs of the wave's arrival (Fig. 5, p. 337; Mitchell 2012 note). Either bound is small against a 90–150 µs loop, so the phase lag sits at the source. The thesis attributes high receptivity to the high-contraction nozzle's thin initial shear layer (Weightman 2019, p. 89), consistent with vortex-sheet theory, which puts receptivity highest at the thinnest part of the layer, and with Barone and Lele's adjoint finding that the edge thickness matters (Edgington-Mitchell 2019, p. 173–174). Onset needs a minimum loop length, explained classically by a minimum loop gain or a minimum integrated amplification (Rockwell 1979, p. 85–87); Tam and Ahuja's low-Mach cut-off (frequency mismatch with the KH band) and Ho and Nosseir's (forcing amplitude) are compatible, and neither has been tested on the same data (Tam 1990 note).

## Staging, switching and frequency selection
Rockwell and Naudascher had shown that stages collapse across jet–edge, hole, cavity and mixing-layer–edge flows (p. 78–80), that two peaks at a jump may be intermittent switching rather than coexistence (p. 81), that hysteresis appears in jet–edge and hole tones but not in cavities or wedges (p. 81–82), and that a mode can persist into the next stage after the dominant frequency has jumped (p. 83–84). Ho and Nosseir gave the first switching evidence for an impinging jet: at M 0.9, x0/d 2.35 a 2.4 s spectrum holds the N = 2 and 3 tones (≈ 4.4 and 5.1 kHz), while 0.061 s spectra usually hold one; they call it hysteresis but give no sweep direction or dwell times (p. 134–135). Henderson and Powell extend this to supersonic, multi-azimuthal-mode jets. At small and intermediate spacings three parallel lines L1–L3 stage with h/d, and they merge into screech at h/d ≳ 5 (p. 311–316). At NPR 3.04, D/d 4, h/d 3.8, two-average spectra switch at random between L1 and L2 near an L1 jump, and whether long-average multiple tones coexist or alternate "was not investigated" (p. 316, 318). Jaunet et al. see screech only with the plate at H/D 8, and quiet bands at H/D 2 that they link to zones of silence (p. 3); Krothapalli et al.'s ideally expanded jet has tones at every height from h/d_j 1.8 to 9.2 (Fig. 20, p. 176), consistent with zones of silence being absent in such jets (Edgington-Mitchell 2019, p. 143–144).

Coexisting loops and their products are clearest in rectangular jets. Tam and Norum showed that the 20–28 tones in each spectrum of Norum's AR 4.24 jets are sums and differences of two basic tones, the varicose s and the flapping a, plus sidebands of a 0.6 kHz facility tone, and strobe-locked schlieren shows both loops in the same jet at the same plate distance (p. 305–308; e.g. s = 2.5 kHz and a = 6.4 kHz, both ≈ 154 dB, at M_j 1.29, L/H 11.05). That is the earliest evidence in the library for the mechanism behind the thesis difference tone 0.144 = 0.385 − 0.241, louder than its 0.241 parent (Weightman et al. 2019, Fig. 5, p. 430, 433), and behind the sum tone of a helical and an axisymmetric tone in Bogey and Gojon's LES, every record of which holds four tones (p. 573). Shock-containing impinging LES also shows helical and axisymmetric modes coexisting with strongly intermittent tone strength (Gojon & Bogey 2017, as cited in Edgington-Mitchell 2019, p. 147). Mason-Smith et al.'s tone map at fixed h jumps with NPR between branches at St (f d/u_e) ≈ 0.28, 0.40–0.43 and 0.55–0.65, and the axisymmetric-to-flapping change coincides with a jump (Fig. 6, p. 614, 617). The convex 0.278 branch is half the flat-plate 0.56 branch to within 1%, the NPR 3.0 correlation maps carry the band spacing of 0.56, and sidebands n × 0.401 + 0.265 appear at NPR 3.4, which points to a subharmonic or quadratically interacting component (Mason-Smith 2015 note). Powis et al. found up to four tones at once, multimodal at z/D 3.9–4.1 and 5.2, but restarted the jet at every z to avoid hysteresis, so their map cannot show it (p. 4–5).

For jet–edge tones, Jordan et al. saw weak multi-peak resonance and, in some Mach ranges, a single high-Q oscillator with its harmonic and suppressed broadband (e.g. St ≈ 1.25 at M ≈ 0.78, L = 2D), with wavelets showing dominance, slow or fast competition, or coexistence of neighbouring cycles (p. 5, 16–17). Stavropoulos et al. mapped this in steps of 0.01 in M_j and R/D, using bicoherence with a threshold (b > 0.35) set from a tone-free case (p. 2–4). Linear selection (several inharmonic tones, zero bicoherence) gives way to nonlinear selection (one saturated tone with harmonics and passive triads) only for R/D ≲ 0.57 and 0.56 ≲ M_j ≲ 0.87 (p. 8–12). The jump at M_j 0.87–0.88 is two orders of magnitude within ΔM_j = 0.01, without hysteresis, and the nonlinear fundamental lies on the same model line as a linear tone, so the oscillator is an amplified linear loop (p. 9, 13–15). Between M_j 0.82 and 0.86 the dominant tone switches from a k⁻_d loop (St ≈ 0.52) to a k⁻_p loop (St ≈ 0.46); a duct-model reflection ratio and k⁻_p's wider radial support are offered for why k⁻_p wins, though the authors say a non-parallel model is needed (p. 15–18, 23–25). Tam and Ahuja's least-dispersive St at M_j 0.85 (0.477) lies between the two (Tam 1990 note).

## Impingement at launch-vehicle scale
An Epsilon boom microphone with the nozzle 8.7 D_e above the pad shows no tone, and Lubert et al. offer only masking by mixing noise and pad geometry as reasons (Lubert 2022, p. 16–17). The 1/42 Epsilon campaign (hot, overexpanded M_e 3.7, 14° deflector) found the worst fairing level (149.1 dB at H/D_e = 14) came from free-jet Mach waves reflected off the ground in the open flame path, with impingement waves hard to see in the LES (Tsutsumi 2015, p. 11–16). LES of a cold M_e 1.8–2.0 jet on a 45° plate shows five wave types: free-jet and wall-jet Mach waves, vortex–plate-shock waves, BBSAN-like tail-shock waves and separation-shock waves (Lubert 2022, p. 17–18); the last three are inclined-plate relatives of leakage and the shocklet, but none is a tone. Nonomura et al.'s implicit LES of an ideally expanded M_j 2.0 jet on a 45° plate (L/D 5 and 10, cold and hot) is likewise broadband, which the authors contrast with the feedback tones of normal impingement (p. 403, 418). Its plate shock is an oblique crescent, with separation and reattachment only when the plate is inside the potential core (Fig. 7, p. 409, 412), and there is no recirculating stand-off pocket; the impingement waves radiate 40–60° off the upstream jet axis, and the arcs stop short of the nozzle direction, so the level returning to the lip is not shown (Nonomura 2011 note). The run is at Re 10⁵ with a thin exit layer (θ ≈ 0.006D), conditions under which normal-plate LES does give tones (Bogey 2017, p. 572–577), but M_j, L/D and inflow differ and there is no normal-plate control. Its candidate sources (impingement, the standing plate shock, reattachment, reflected Mach waves; p. 418) rest on an OASPL iso-surface alone (Fig. 15). Surfaces raise the noise even without tones: a flap makes a subsonic jet about 20 dB louder (Evertz et al., as cited in Rockwell 1979, p. 85), and a ground plane adds about 8 dB to a laboratory jet's near-field OASPL (Krothapalli 1999, p. 178). At Wallops the water-quenched flame-duct exit dominated an Antares static fire, without tones (Panda 2013, p. 17, not tagged). Vega pad CAA shows reflected waves reaching the nozzle lip, so feedback is possible but untested ([Casalino 2009](../Notes/casalino-2009-hybrid-empirical-caa-rocket-noise.md), p. 14–15, not tagged). Empirical models treat impingement only by ending the potential core at the deflector, which helps against Saturn V ([Haynes 2009](../Notes/haynes-2009-sp-8072-dsm-ii-ares-i-lift-off.md), p. 10–12, not tagged) but overpredicts Ares I ([Ranow 2021](../Notes/ranow-2021-acoustic-prediction-rocket-flame-deflector.md), p. 41–44, not tagged). Impingement on terrain removes high-frequency content and skewness ([Gee 2012](../Notes/gee-2012-reusable-srm-noise-spectral-statistical.md), p. 10–11, not tagged).

## Disagreements and open questions
**Guided or free-stream closure in a given supersonic impinging jet.** Settled in principle by source location (Edgington-Mitchell 2019, p. 172, 176; Edgington-Mitchell 2025; Nogueira 2024), but never tested on one dataset. The vortex-sheet band test has now been run on round and rectangular tones (Tam 1990, Tam 1992, Jaunet 2019, Bogey 2017 and Mason-Smith 2015 notes): it favours guided closure for axisymmetric tones and external closure for helical or flapping ones, but frequencies barely separate the two at supersonic M_j, tones near the branch point are ambiguous, and tones above S(0,1) or between the planar bands are unexplained. The bands also need the right temperature ratio and shear-layer thickness: thickening lowers the (0, 2) limits in [Mancinelli 2021](../Notes/mancinelli-2021-complex-valued-resonance-model-screech.md) (p. 16–17, not tagged), whereas Bogey and Gojon's temporal mixing layers put thick-layer bands slightly above the vortex sheet (p. 586–588). A streamwise-wavenumber split inside the core (as in Bogey 2017, p. 583–585; not done in the infinite-lip LES, Karami 2020, p. 16), the coherence and phase continuity of the tone across the shear layer (Edgington-Mitchell 2025, p. 8), cold finite-thickness bands (Nogueira 2024, eqs. 4.7–4.8) and Rockwell's long-unused streamlined body in the core at the exit (p. 74) would decide it.

**Leakage, stand-off-shock motion or vortex–wall impact.** The infinite-lip LES puts the main source at the wall 0.8–1.1d off axis and leakage-like waves weaker, but by eye (Karami 2020, p. 9–12). The stand-off shock oscillates at the tone and splits in some cycles (Powis 2016, p. 6–7), yet an ideally expanded jet resonates without one (Krothapalli 1999, p. 177). Cases with the stand-off shock well clear of the wall, ray tracing with a measured sound-speed field, amplitude-preserving reconstructions (r̄ ± 2σ rescaling, Edgington-Mitchell 2021, p. 28–30; phase averages conditioned on pair amplitude, Weightman 2018 note), long records of the shock's motion alongside the outgoing fronts, or LES with surface-source attribution would separate them, with free-jet leakage (Edgington-Mitchell 2021, p. 13–19) as the reference signature.

**What triggers leakage.** A sufficiently positive sonic-line angle "rather than the saddle point" (Weightman 2019, p. 163), or the saddle point (Edgington-Mitchell 2021, p. 26–27)? The thesis takes the sonic-line tilt at the saddle (p. 114–115), so these may be one geometry; neither gives a general criterion combining incidence angle and local vorticity (Edgington-Mitchell 2021, p. 26–28).

**The phase lag.** Values from +0.4 to −0.49 use different loop definitions and reference events, as Rockwell and Naudascher found for edge tones (p. 75–76). Krothapalli's −0.4 arises with no stand-off shock, is 2–6 times the shocklet's delay when read as one, and trades off against a 14% convection-speed error and against N (Krothapalli 1999 note). The shocklet's 0.8% agreement is largely built in, leaving a 0.24-cycle deficit that a 9% lower u_c would also explain (Weightman 2017 note), and convection speeds for the same NPR 3.4 case differ by 30–40% between LES x–t diagrams and PIV (Karami 2020, p. 13; Weightman et al. 2019, p. 432–433). A common loop definition and reference event (vortex core, saddle point or shocklet inception), u_c measured along the whole path including the turn into the wall jet, and cycle-resolved Δt would let p separate leakage from the shocklet; comparing Bogey and Gojon's standing-wave cell count with Krothapalli's N for the same tones is a cheap first check (Krothapalli 1999 note). Since df/f ≈ −0.2% per µs of Δt, ±10 µs of jitter should appear as about ±2% instantaneous-frequency wander, testable with a Hilbert analysis of long microphone records (Weightman 2017 note).

**Where the shocklet matters.** It closes the loop consistently at NPR ≥ 4.5, z/D ≤ 4.0 (Edgington-Mitchell 2025, p. 15–17); the direct 40 µs delay is at NPR 5.0, but the quantitative delay test is at NPR 3.6 (Weightman 2017, p. 5, 7–10). High-frame-rate imaging on a hemisphere at NPR 3.4–3.6 would show whether it also closes the loop there or the agreement is partly coincidental.

**What silences a loop.** A plug nozzle gives no tones for z/D < 3.2 (Powis 2016, p. 5), a concave surface none for NPR 2.9–3.9 (Mason-Smith 2015, p. 614), Henderson's zones of silence follow the plate's place in the shock train (Henderson 2002, p. 4), and intermediate plates cut tonal activity (Henderson 1993, p. 314–317). The authors blame the annular near field, lower receptivity and the stand-off-shock state respectively, but none tested the cause, and the concave and Henderson silent cases both show a steady stand-off structure (Mason-Smith 2015 note). Measuring the lip shear-layer thickness and the stand-off shock's motion in silent and resonant states, a minimum-integrated-amplification check (Rockwell 1979, p. 85–87), a dispersive phase model for an annular jet (Powis 2016 note), and a global or resolvent analysis with and without the recirculated wall jet (Mason-Smith 2015 note) would show which leg of the loop fails.

**Does the lip transient matter?** Whether the λ ≈ 0.05d bands seed the vortex or only mask its linear growth is open (Mitchell 2012, p. 339). Burst sequences conditioned on the arriving front's strength would give an empirical receptivity transfer function, and impulsive forcing of a non-parallel linear model on a measured near-lip mean flow would show whether the loop-frequency wavepacket is present from the first diameter (Mitchell 2012 note).

**What fixes the recurring St ≈ 0.25 and 0.39.** A near-lip most-unstable frequency is unlikely: it falls from about 4 to 0.5 within 1d, identically for two spacings whose tones differ twofold (Karami 2020, p. 21–23). Similar families appear in an ideally expanded, thin-lip LES at L/D 5–6 (helical 0.255–0.29, axisymmetric 0.375–0.44; Bogey 2017, p. 573, 576) and in Krothapalli et al.'s lift-plate jet (helical 0.25–0.30, axisymmetric 0.35–0.38; note's conversion). Global stability or resolvent analysis of the PIV mean flows, as done for free jets in Edgington-Mitchell 2020, and free and impinging spectra at the same NPR and lip would test the "most unstable frequency" assumption and the link to C-mode screech.

**Which upstream wave closes jet–edge loops, and with what phase.** Jordan 2018 found that k_d⁻ loops "did not match the data" and needed φ = 0 for some cases and π for others (p. 9). Stavropoulos 2026, with the same facility, nozzle and overlapping geometry (L/D = 2, R/D = 0.5) but a thicker chamfered plate, closes higher tones at M_j 0.82–0.89 with k⁻_d (p = 4–9) and uses φ = 0 almost everywhere (p. 13–18). With integer p up to 9 and φ free, the criterion can fit almost any tone, and the m = 1 tones are unexplained. Jaunet 2019 excludes the duct-like waves from its impinging model (p. 6–7), which may cost it tones in 0.82 < M_j < 1 (Jaunet 2019 note). An azimuthal array and a non-parallel model with nozzle and edge, giving R₁ and R₂, would settle these points.

**Coexistence or switching of tones.** Coexistence is established for rectangular impinging jets, where strobe-locked schlieren shows both loops at once and the spectrum fills with their combination tones (Tam 1992, p. 305–308). For round jets, switching between neighbouring stages has been seen on short spectral windows, without dwell times (Ho 1981, p. 134–135; Henderson 1993, p. 318), the four coexisting LES tones were never tested (Bogey 2017, p. 573), and shock-containing LES shows intermittent tone strength (Edgington-Mitchell 2019, p. 147). The thin-lip case is one helical mode with varying amplitude (Weightman 2018, p. 6, 9), but for the thick lips PIV POD and averaged spectra cannot tell toroidal–helical coexistence from switching (Weightman 2019, p. 33, 70, 119), though the loud difference tone argues for partial coexistence (Weightman et al. 2019 note). Band-pass Hilbert envelopes and bicoherence of the existing 2.5 s, 200 kHz microphone records (Weightman et al. 2019, p. 426), bicoherence of the convex-cylinder records for the 0.278 = 0.56/2 relation (Mason-Smith 2015 note), and long-record time-resolved schlieren with Hilbert POD and BMD would locate the interaction.

**Why full-scale pads show no tones.** Masking is a conjecture. The laboratory work suggests more specific candidates: inclined deflectors have no normal stand-off shock, now supported by a broadband 45° plate LES at a Reynolds number and exit layer where normal-plate LES resonates, though without a normal-plate control (Nonomura 2011, p. 403, 418; note); inclined plates spread the feedback path lengths (Edgington-Mitchell 2019, p. 147); flame trenches that turn the exhaust back towards the nozzle resemble the concave surface that silenced a laboratory jet (Mason-Smith 2015 note); nozzle heights of 8 D_e or more; the shocklet's need for a transonic wall jet; the acoustic environment near the nozzle (vehicle base, launch mount), since a reflector or foam can kill a tone (Henderson 1993, p. 318–319; Weightman et al. 2019, p. 434–441); and the dependence of the guided-mode band on M_j and temperature, which has not been computed for hot plumes (Jaunet 2019 note). Heat alone is an unlikely answer, since impingement tones are reported to survive in heated laboratory jets (Kumar et al. 2008 and Gustavsson et al. 2010, as cited in Powis 2016, p. 2). Hot, overexpanded subscale jets on normal, inclined and trench-like surfaces at 2–10 D_e, with time-resolved schlieren and bicoherence on near-pad microphones, would discriminate.

## Methods and data
- **Point acoustics (time-resolved):** long records (20–30 s at 200 kHz) over fine Mach sweeps, analysed with PSD maps, thresholded bicoherence and bump-wavelet scalograms (μ = 5, σ = 0.6), are cheap and decisive for mapping regimes and extinction curves, but one microphone cannot resolve azimuthal mode (Jordan 2018, p. 3; Stavropoulos 2026, p. 3–4; Jaunet 2019, p. 2). Pre-whitening (notching the tone before correlating) separates the two legs, and the side peaks of one sensor's autocorrelation give the round-trip time (Ho 1981, p. 123–131). Block-averaged spectra cannot tell coexistence from switching (Henderson 1993, p. 318; Rockwell 1979, p. 81), and restarting the jet at each set point hides hysteresis (Powis 2016, p. 4).
- **Non-time-resolved PIV and schlieren:** phase from a microphone reference (Henderson 2002, p. 2, 5) or from a POD pair's phase portrait (Weightman et al. 2019, p. 430–432). Phase-binned averages of total velocity then time emission, convection and arrival at the lip to within the bin (5° sectors; Weightman 2017, p. 8–9) and keep shocks and fronts, which phase reconstructions lose (Weightman 2019, p. 102–105). About 500 snapshots converge the leading pair in resonant flows and about 2500 are needed when no mode dominates (p. 56–57). The per-snapshot pair amplitude correlates (0.74) with an unpaired "shear thickness mode", so amplitude-conditioned statistics also come from independent snapshots; the impinging jet's turning wall flow inflates that mode's transverse energy (Weightman 2018, p. 7, 12). Vortex convection speeds can be read from single snapshots, independently of POD (Krothapalli 1999, p. 166–167), and single snapshots give front strength via Rankine–Hugoniot, smeared by particle lag (Edgington-Mitchell 2025, p. 10). Two orthogonal schlieren views with two-point correlation identify a flapping plane (Mason-Smith 2015, p. 616–617), and strobe imaging locked to one filtered tone isolates one loop even when two coexist (Tam 1992, p. 305).
- **Time-resolved schlieren:** 102-frame bursts at 250–500 kfps serve visualisation (Mitchell 2012, p. 335–339) and, averaged over 20 bursts, edge-detection tracking of the stand-off shock (Powis 2016, p. 4, 6); the thesis used them for a streamwise–temporal Fourier split but not spectral decomposition (Weightman 2019, p. 24–26, 109–110), and the 2025 paper tracked waves frame by frame at 50–200 kfps with correlation-based phase averages whose templates are not stated (p. 7, 18, 20). **No paper in this thread uses SPOD, BMD or HPOD**; the group's review mentions only DMD (Edgington-Mitchell 2019, p. 156).
- **LES:** three impinging-jet LES are now in the library. Bogey and Gojon (relaxation-filter LES of an ideally expanded jet) use single-frequency DFT maps for standing-wave cells and azimuthal phase, and axis and lip-line k–ω spectra (p. 573–585). Karami et al. (the thesis infinite-lip case at Re 5 × 10⁴) use x–t diagrams, St–k_x spectra and zero-lag correlation maps (p. 13–21). Both reproduce measured tones with laminar exit layers. Nonomura et al.'s implicit LES of an inclined plate uses only OASPL maps and single-record spectra (p. 412–419). Other impinging LES (Gojon, Varé, Uzun) appear only as citations.
- **Models:** Powell's equation (under-determined), the vortex-sheet phase criterion with a duct nozzle-reflection model, complex-mode resonance (fragile), the round and planar vortex-sheet bands of Tam and Ahuja and Tam and Norum (reproduced in their notes; the planar lower limits have a closed form), geometrical-acoustics ray tracing (qualitative) and the scattering theory of Nogueira 2024. The only stability analysis of an impinging mean flow is Karami's local, parallel, m = 0 analysis for x ≤ 0.9d, which gets the trend of the most unstable St but not its values (p. 21–25).

## Relevance to our work
- **Thesis data:** a streamwise-wavenumber split of the thin- and infinite-lip POD pairs tests for guided-mode content, and zero-lag correlation maps of the same PIV would give the shear-layer wavelength to cross-check u_c (Karami 2020 note). Phase averages conditioned on pair amplitude would show how the thin-lip source moves between strong and weak cycles (Weightman 2018 note). Band-passed Hilbert envelopes and bicoherence of the 2.5 s microphone records test coexistence (Weightman et al. 2019 note). Appendix A's eight-condition matrix, built for an LES comparison that never appeared, is a ready validation set with non-resonant cases (Weightman 2019, p. 145).
- **New time-resolved schlieren:** long records of thin- and infinite-lip jets on cylinder and hemisphere would allow SPOD at the tone with the k_x sign separating upstream waves, phase continuity across the shear layer, BMD of the difference tone and the shock-like fronts' harmonics, and the SPOD → reconstruction → HPOD pipeline for cycle-to-cycle Δt and mode or wave-form switching. A long stand-off-shock position record, as tracked by Powis et al., would give a scalar for Hilbert analysis of shock motion and splitting (Powis 2016 note). Fine NPR and z/D microphone sweeps with bicoherence should come first. Ho's M 0.9 stage table, Krothapalli's tone map and Jaunet's maps are ready validation sets for a dispersive phase model (notes).
- **PyFR LES:** nozzle exterior geometry is part of the boundary condition and must be modelled in any LES, free or impinging (Weightman 2019, p. 89). Reduced-Re, laminar-exit LES reproduce measured impinging tones (Bogey 2017, p. 572–577; Karami 2020, p. 18), indirect support for the PyFR set-up; Karami et al. needed fine resolution at the lip edge to capture receptivity (p. 6), and the λ ≈ 0.05d lip transient sets the scale to resolve (Mitchell 2012 note). Axis k–ω spectra as in Bogey 2017 would show the (0, 2) guided mode in the A1/A2 runs (Bogey 2017 note).
- **Rocket noise:** impinging-jet waves are shocks at birth, linking to crackle; the open question is why the loop does not survive in hot, overexpanded plumes on inclined deflectors and in flame trenches, for which the guided-mode bands can now be computed with the Tam 1990 machinery.
