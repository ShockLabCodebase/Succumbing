## jordan-2013-wave-packets-turbulent-jet-noise (queued 2026-10-22)

[Jordan & Colonius 2013](../Notes/jordan-2013-wave-packets-turbulent-jet-noise.md) is the founding review of the wavepacket view of mixing noise, and the page currently cites only its successors ([Cavalieri et al. 2013](../Notes/cavalieri-2013-wavepackets-velocity-field-turbulent-jets.md), [Cavalieri et al. 2019](../Notes/cavalieri-2019-wave-packet-models-jet-dynamics-sound-radiation.md)). It fixes the cold-jet far-field facts the wavepacket model must explain: peak at 20°–40° from the downstream axis at St ≈ 0.2 with steep decay, broadening to St ≈ 0.4 at 90°, OASPL ∝ U_j^n with n = 8 at 90° and n > 8 at shallow angles, almost all St < 1 sound in m = 0–2, and bursts at St ≈ 0.2 (p. 176). The m ≤ 2 result comes from the Michalke & Fuchs radial-compactness Bessel factor (p. 176), the same argument Cavalieri 2013 uses for why velocity energy at m ≈ 4–11 is silent. The mechanism is acoustic matching (Fig. 5, p. 177): a homogeneous subsonic wave is silent, envelope modulation leaks sound to φ = arccos(αa∞/ω), superdirective for m = 0, and supersonic phase speed gives Mach waves; Cavalieri 2019 later gave the closed form. Energetically the coherent part is small (≈ 15% of low-frequency fluctuation energy in unforced jets against ≈ 50% when forced, p. 180) but efficient through coherence and intermittency (p. 181, 189), and loud far-field events coincide with wavepacket breakdown near the end of the potential core (Hileman et al. 2005, M_j 1.28; p. 186). It adds to the open question **"Two sources or one jittering wavepacket?"**: it explicitly rejects the two-source reading of [Tam et al. 1996](../Notes/tam-1996-two-components-turbulent-mixing-noise-supersonic-jets.md), because spectral shape evolves smoothly with angle and aft and sideline peaks span similar bands (p. 181), while conceding the wavepacket share of sideline noise "remains to be quantified" (p. 189) — so it states the one-source side but does not settle it. Control evidence (p. 188–189) fits [Samimy et al. 2006](../Notes/samimy-2006-plasma-actuators-high-speed-jet-flow-noise-control.md): higher-m, higher-St forcing reduces noise; forcing at LSA-stable frequencies thickens the mean flow and lowers m = 0 gain, at unstable ones adds coherent Reynolds stress and noise (Koenig 2011). Scope caveat for the page: cold/low-supersonic round jets only, no BBSAN, screech or rocket conditions; for supersonic phase speeds it says calibrated PSE gives the low-frequency aft directivity (p. 185), supporting the rocket Mach-wave point.

## wong-2019-coherence-decay-wavepacket-bbsan (queued 2026-10-22)

- This is the group's first wavepacket BBSAN paper. It extends Cavalieri & Agarwal's (2014) jittering line source to a Gaussian wavepacket × Prandtl–Pack shock-cell source at NPR 3.4 (M_j 1.45), with Gaussian coherence decay of length L_c = 1.0D (p. 973–977).
- Unlike in subsonic jets, coherence decay lowers the BBSAN peak by about 2–5 dB and broadens the directivity (Fig. 2, p. 978).
- The double wavenumber transform shows why. The difference wave k_h − k_s (A⁻) is the only acoustically matched lobe and already lies inside the radiation square, so jitter smears energy out of it. The sum wave and the mixed (B) terms are silent (p. 981–983). This confirms Tam & Tanna's k − λ₁ ([tam-1982](../Notes/tam-1982-shock-associated-noise-cd-nozzles.md)).
- With ten Pack modes, the coherent model gives narrow secondary peaks and dips at high St and upstream angles. Coherence decay pulls the n = 2 lobe into the radiation square and fills the "missing sound" of Ray & Lele (2007) and Suzuki (2016) (p. 984–987). The authors argue the deficit comes from missing higher shock modes, not from a failure of linear theory (p. 980).
- It is the model-problem precursor of [wong-2021](../Notes/wong-2021-wavepacket-modelling-bbsan.md). Both agree that coherence is not needed for peak level (2021: coherent PSE within ±2 dB/St) and that jitter fills the gaps between shock-mode lobes (2021: up to 15 dB/St).
- They differ on the secondary peaks. Here they are called "artificial" (p. 984, 987). In 2021, and in [tam-1986](../Notes/tam-1986-bbsan-screech-relationship.md), the n = 2 peak is real. The note reads "artificial" as too narrow and too deep. The Norum & Seiner comparison data (p. 979) are of unstated spectral resolution and screech state, and the model peaks were matched to them, so only spectral shape was tested.
- A unifying reading from the note, derived and to be flagged as ours: Cavalieri et al. 2019's closed form applied to the k_h − k_s wave gives a peak drop of 10 log₁₀(L_m/L), i.e. −3.9, −2.9 and −1.8 dB at St 0.3, 0.4 and 0.6, which matches Fig. 2. Jitter then attenuates acoustically matched difference waves and amplifies subsonic ones (n = 1 below St ≈ 0.22, n = 2 below St ≈ 0.51). This turns the page's existing bullet "k − k_s components can be subsonic even in a supersonic jet" into a demonstrated mechanism.
- It also supports the page's reading that the long streamwise coherence needed by far-field-fitted statistical models belongs to the wavepacket: the paper argues that only low-order wavepacket coherence, not bulk turbulence statistics, matters (p. 972, 987). This bears on the open question "Statistical or wavepacket sources?".
- It adds to the open question "What fills the BBSAN dips?": jitter can fill them without shock unsteadiness, at least in the model.

## wong-2020-azimuthal-decomposition-radiated-noise-shock-containing-jets (queued 2026-10-22)

These are the first experimental, azimuthally decomposed BBSAN data in the library. The jets are unheated, screeching and from a convergent nozzle, at M_j 1.14, 1.38 and 1.50 (β 0.55–1.12, beyond the β < 0.8 of earlier azimuthal studies; p. 2016). They were measured in the group's SJAF with a two-microphone ring at r = 11D (p. 2018).
- **Modal ranking.** The axisymmetric mode is never dominant. The ±1 pair leads below St ≈ 0.6–0.7, with m = 0 on average 3–4 dB/St lower, and above St ≈ 0.6–0.7 the ±1, ±2 and ±3 pairs are about equal (p. 2019). As θ and M_j fall the lobe moves up in St, so BBSAN becomes more multimodal (p. 2020).
- **Reconstruction.** 2–3 modes (m = 0, ±1, ±2) rebuild the primary lobe within 1 dB/St at NPR 3.10–3.67. NPR 2.25, low θ (five modes at θ = 70°, NPR 3.10) and the n = 2 lobe need 4–5 (p. 2020–2021).
- **A third cause of the "missing sound".** The paper adds azimuthal truncation (Tam 1987 axisymmetric; Ray & Lele 2007 m ≤ 2; p. 2021) to the high-frequency "missing sound", alongside the missing higher shock-cell modes and coherence decay of wong-2019-coherence-decay-wavepacket-bbsan and the jitter of wong-2021-wavepacket-modelling-bbsan. The three are compatible, since the secondary lobe needs n = 2 shocks and is also the most multimodal in azimuth.
- **Link to wong-2021.** This is the dataset wong-2021 validates against (NPR 3.67). Its 4–5-mode result is why wong-2021's m = 0–2 total under-predicts the n = 2 lobe (wong-2021, p. 22). The page's bullet on the Wong 2021 model could say so.
- **Far field.** OASPL follows β⁴ for β⁴ > 0.5 (p. 2019). Eq. 1 with u_c = 0.7u_j and Pack spacing fits NPR 3.10–3.67 but under-predicts at NPR 2.25 (p. 2019–2020).
- **Convective Mach number in the plotted curves.** The note's pixel check of Fig. 3 finds that the plotted curves use M_c = 0.7M_j, i.e. u_c over the jet sound speed. That is neither the eddy Mach number u_c/a_∞ nor Papamoschou–Roshko, which bears on the page's "Convective Mach number" convention paragraph. The curves also use harmonic spacings L_n = L₁/n, not Tam et al. 1986's non-harmonic shock Fourier wavenumbers.
- **Caveat (note's reading).** m = 0 is compared with the summed ±1 pair, so about 3 dB of the 3–4 dB gap is built in. Per signed mode, m = 0 and m = ±1 are comparable.
- **Screech.** Screech is not suppressed. Agreement with the non-screeching LES of Arroyo & Moreau (2019) is the authors' only evidence that screech leaves the azimuthal frequency dependence unchanged (p. 2020). This does not contradict andre-2013-bbsan-screeching-non-screeching-underexpanded-jets, which concerns levels and peak frequency, but it is not a controlled test.
- **Methods and data.** The page currently cites Wong et al. 2021 (p. 8) for the two-microphone azimuthal method. This paper is the primary source (App. C, p. 2024–2026). It needs stationarity over the traverse, not a simultaneous array, and gives block-averaged spectra only.

## jaunet-2017-two-point-coherence-wave-packets-turbulent-jets (queued 2026-10-23)

- **Bulk statistics are not source statistics.** Classic source models built on two-point statistics use mostly acoustically irrelevant information. The m = 0, ±1, ±2 modes carry less than 10% of the bulk two-point coherence at x/D = 3, which is set by the energetic m ≈ 2–8 eddies (Figs 7–8, p. 9–10).
  - My derivation: at zero separation the cumulative coherence Γ²₂ ≈ 0.035–0.04 is a squared energy fraction, so these modes hold ≈ 20% of the u_x spectrum. That is consistent with the ≈ 15% that [Jordan & Colonius 2013](../Notes/jordan-2013-wave-packets-turbulent-jet-noise.md) quote from Michalke & Fuchs.
  - My reading of Fig. 7: the low-order modes make up a growing share of the coherence at long separations (Δx ≳ 1.5–3D).
- **Measured inputs for jittering line-source models.** This is the measured input that the jittering line-source models on the page lack. L_c varies tenfold with x and St (≈ 0.2–1.9D for m = 0). The coherence is exponential, not Gaussian, and asymmetric. [Wong 2019](../Notes/wong-2019-coherence-decay-wavepacket-bbsan.md) assumed a constant 1.0D and [Cavalieri 2019](../Notes/cavalieri-2019-wave-packet-models-jet-dynamics-sound-radiation.md) assumed L_c ≈ L, citing these data.
  - My mapping: the measured γ² e-folding length L corresponds to the model's Gaussian L_c ≈ √2 L. That gives L_c ≈ 2.1–2.7D at the peak, consistent with the L_c = 2D the authors use in Fig. 21.
- **Phase speed matters as much as coherence.** The m = 0 wavepacket travels faster than the eddies for St ≲ 0.3, at up to ≈ U_j near the nozzle against ≈ 0.6U_j (Fig. 22). In the Cavalieri–Agarwal source (eqs. 7–10), a change from 0.5 to 0.6U_j at St 0.4 raises the low-angle PSD by ≈ 24 dB (my calculation, with the paper's L_w = 5D and L_c = 2D).
  - This questions the fixed M_c ≈ 0.6M used for wavepacket directivity in the page's "Mach wave radiation" section for subsonic jets. The supersonic, rocket-relevant conclusion that jitter matters little is unaffected.
- **Bearing on the "Two sources or one jittering wavepacket?" question.** The paper gives the coherence statistics a jitter model must reproduce, but does not itself compute sideline noise.

## tam-1984-sound-instability-waves-supersonic-axisymmetric-jets (queued 2026-10-24)

- **Where it fits.** Tam & Burton 1984 (Part 2) is the founding quantitative test of the claim that linear instability waves on the mean flow radiate the Mach waves of a supersonic jet.
  - A matched-asymptotic solution joins a non-parallel instability wave to a Fourier/Hankel outer field. It reproduces Troutt & McLaughlin's measured near-field SPL contours of a cold M 2.1 jet excited at St 0.4 and 0.2. At St 0.4 the lobe direction and contour spacing are "nearly perfect" with one level matched at one point; St 0.2 needs a downstream shift of the whole field (p. 291–293).
  - Single modes radiate at about 40° from the downstream axis (n = 0) and within a 35° cone (n = 1) (p. 290–292).
- **Acoustic matching.** The far-field rule D(χ) = 2|g(η = (u_j/a_∞)ω cos χ)|² (eq. 5.8, p. 283), with only η below the sonic branch point radiating (Figs 9–10, p. 289), is the acoustic-matching statement of current-understanding item 4. It comes 35 years before the line-source form in cavalieri-2019-wave-packet-models-jet-dynamics-sound-radiation (eqs. 40–43).
- **Convective Mach number** (my reading of Figs 9–10). The radiating spectral peaks have phase speeds of 0.80–0.91u_j, so U/a_∞ ≈ 1.2–1.4. By contrast Papamoschou–Roshko's M_c for this jet is only 0.89 (papamoschou-1988-compressible-turbulent-shear-layer), and the Tam & Hu supersonic family does not exist below M_j ≈ 2.37 at this temperature (tam-1989-three-families-instability-waves-high-speed-jets). The Mach waves therefore come from KH waves faster than a_∞. This supports the page's convention note that the eddy Mach number, not M_c, sets the direction.
- **Links to other notes.** This is the solution step inside Tam's stochastic model in tam-1995-supersonic-jet-noise (that note, p. 24–27), and the source of the "Tam & Burton (1984)" citations in tam-1995, tam-1996, tam-1989 and tam-1986.
- **Effect on the page.** It does not change the current understanding. It adds the historical anchor and the first near-field validation, and it should go in "Mach wave radiation and the convective Mach number", near the Michalke near-field paragraph: it gives the "uniformly valid analysis" Michalke points to.
- **Caveats for the page.** The jet was forced, cold and at moderate Re. The amplitude is fitted, there are no far-field data (p. 274), and only coherent forced waves are treated. It says nothing about the two-source/jitter open question.

## tam-1994-turbulent-mixing-noise-supersonic-jets (queued 2026-10-24)

- **Primary source.** Tam & Chen 1994 is the primary source of the stochastic instability-wave mixing-noise model that the page now cites only through Tam 1995 ("Mixing noise: two sources" section).
- **Model.** The large structures are a superposition of instability waves of every frequency and azimuthal order, excited by white noise at the lip (delta-correlated pressure at the half-velocity point of the exit; eq. 9, p. 1776). Different (n, ω) are therefore uncorrelated, and one constant D̃ sets the level. The far-field spectrum at polar angle θ is each wavepacket's axial wavenumber spectrum at (ω/a_∞) cos θ (eq. 19, p. 1777), the acoustic-matching result of Cavalieri et al. 2019 in its 1994 form.
- **Fit to data.** With D̃ = 4.35 × 10⁻¹⁰ (T_0 500 K, isothermal) and 9.19 × 10⁻¹⁰ (1114 K), computed directivities of Seiner et al.'s (1992) M_j 2 jet agree within about 2.5 dB (p. 1778–1779). The exceptions are St 0.067 at 500 K (about 9 dB low by my reading of Fig. 5; the authors call the cause unknown) and St 0.1 at small exhaust angles at 1114 K. The constant differs by a factor of 2.1 between the two jets, so it is calibrated per jet, which the paper does not discuss.
- **Two-source split.** The paper prints that large-structure noise dominates for χ > 130° and fine-scale noise for χ < 110° (p. 1774). [Tam et al. 1996](../Notes/tam-1996-two-components-turbulent-mixing-noise-supersonic-jets.md) quote 125° "following Tam & Chen".
- **Azimuthal content.** Heating changes it: only n = ±1 radiates at 500 K, St 0.12, while n = 0 to ±5 contribute at 1114 K, St 0.4, which broadens the directivity (Figs 7–8, p. 1779). This is consistent with the radial-compactness argument of [Cavalieri et al. 2013](../Notes/cavalieri-2013-wavepackets-velocity-field-turbulent-jets.md).
- **Peak direction.** In the measured data (Figs 5–6, my readings), the peak moves from θ ≈ 25–30° at St ≈ 0.06 to ≈ 40–45° (500 K) and ≈ 50–60° (1114 K) at St 0.2–0.4. A single convective Mach number therefore does not set the directivity, which bears on the open question "Which convective Mach number sets the rocket peak angle?".
- **Wave family.** By Tam & Hu's criterion the 1114 K jet supports supersonic instability waves (u_j ≈ 1000 against a_j + a_∞ ≈ 840 m s⁻¹, my estimate), but the paper appears to compute only the KH family.
- **Effect on the page.** No change to the current understanding. It gives items 2 and 4 their primary citation. Papers-list line: "origin of the stochastic instability-wave (wavepacket) mixing-noise model; white-noise lip forcing; one constant; heated jets radiate through many azimuthal modes".

## tam-2008-sources-of-jet-noise-experimental-evidence (queued 2026-10-24)

Tam, Viswanathan, Ahuja & Panda (2008) give the experimental case for the two-source model behind the F/G spectra of [Tam et al. 1996](../Notes/tam-1996-two-components-turbulent-mixing-noise-supersonic-jets.md). They use evidence beyond spectral shape: OASPL, St_peak and velocity-exponent trends with angle (n ≈ 8 sideline cold, rising to 9.9 at 150° inlet; 6.4→9.1 at T_r/T_a 2.7; p. 262–266); the polar coherence of the aft field (max cross-correlation up to 0.59 for 10° spacing within θ > 130°, ≤ ≈ 0.1 sideline; p. 274–278); and flow–acoustic correlations from point Rayleigh scattering (≈ 22 % at 150°, noise floor below 120°; p. 279–280, correcting Panda et al. 2005's figs 12a,b). Elliptic-mirror and correlation source maps show that sideline sources peak at one x just past the end of the potential core for all sideline angles, while the aft-source peak moves upstream as the inlet angle grows (Figs. 29a, 32–35, p. 283–289). The change-over angles (120–140° inlet) agree with the 1996 boundaries (χ > 125° / < 110°), and the velocity exponents agree with the 1996 eqs. 8 and 10 within about 0.4. The note flags one tension, though: here large-structure St_peak collapses on f D_j/u_j independent of temperature (≈ 0.17 at 150°, Fig. 7b), whereas 1996 reports f D_j/a∞ ≈ 0.19 temperature-independent at 160°. For the open question "Two sources or one jittering wavepacket?", the paper settles that the aft field is coherent and the sideline field incoherent. But a single-point correlation cannot distinguish fine-scale sources from short-coherence (jittering) wavepackets, as [Jordan & Colonius 2013](../Notes/jordan-2013-wave-packets-turbulent-jet-noise.md) (p. 181, 186) and [Cavalieri et al. 2019](../Notes/cavalieri-2019-wave-packet-models-jet-dynamics-sound-radiation.md) (p. 14–16) would argue, so the question stays open. The fixed sideline source location and the abrupt change in n near 120° are the results hardest for a one-source view. The note also shows that eqs. 1–3 (Gaussian-envelope instability wave, p. 259) are the coherent Gaussian line source of Cavalieri 2019 (eq. 37), and that the autocorrelation-based "radial correlation length" (2.4 D_j, p. 272) is fixed by the F spectrum shape and peak frequency (≈ 0.68 acoustic wavelengths at f_p; my check), so it is not independent evidence. The paper suggests a Papers-list line: "Tam et al. 2008: four independent experimental tests (spectra/power laws, 2-mic correlation, Rayleigh flow–acoustic correlation, mirror) of the two-source model; aft field coherent, sideline incoherent". It supports Current understanding item 2 without changing it, and adds evidence to the two-sources open question.

## bres-2019-modelling-jet-noise-les-perspective (queued 2026-10-24)

This is a secondary but useful source for three threads.
- **Crackle.** The page says no generation study of crackle is indexed. This review summarises the Nichols et al. 2013 LES (validated at NTR 3.65, extended to 4.5): crackle leaves as weak shocklets traced back to shear-layer eddies; near-nozzle shocklets fit Mach-wave emission at Oertel's M_co; and the intense events are faster than M_co and infrequent (p. 10, Fig. 6). Tam et al. 2018 is cited as adding that source nonlinearity does not exclude propagation steepening (p. 10). This informs the open question "Is crackle radiated as shocks or formed in propagation?" in favour of "both", but only second-hand. The primary Nichols papers are not in the library.
- **BBSAN models.** Suzuki's 2016 wavepacket form of the Harper-Bourne & Fisher model gives far-field spectra unlike measurements, while the Morris & Miller 2010 RANS statistical model fits the shape better (p. 11). That ranking predates wong-2021, whose wavepacket model with LES jitter matches within ±2 dB/St. So the page's "Statistical or wavepacket sources?" question should note that the review's verdict is about Suzuki's model, not Wong's.
- **Mixing noise and wavepackets.** It restates schmidt-2018: m = 0 dominates at peak angles, the first three m carry most acoustic energy, and low-rank KH behaviour over a range of St gives way to non-low-rank Orr packets elsewhere, which is why early wavepacket models failed at low St (p. 12–13). This agrees with cavalieri-2013, cavalieri-2019 and jordan-2013.
- **Heating.** It adds a heated military-nozzle LES (NTR 3 and 7): an afterburner annulus around a military-power core lowers peak OASPL, sound power and spectra against full afterburner at the same thrust (p. 8–9). My reading of Fig. 5 is about 2 dB at the peak.

No change to the current understanding. The crackle paragraph ("The library holds no laboratory study…") can now cite this review for the LES picture, flagged as secondary.

## nonomura-2011-supersonic-jet-impinging-inclined-plate-acoustic-waves (queued 2026-10-24)

For the "Mach wave radiation and the convective Mach number" section: at fixed M_j = 2.0, raising TR_c from 1 to 5.4 (u_J/a_∞ 1.49 → 3.46) turns the wall-jet Mach waves from about 30° to 40–50° from the plate at r = 20D, and to about 38° → 60° at 100D. The impingement-region waves turn from 75° to 95° from the plate (p. 416, 421; Fig. 11 readings in the note). The authors attribute this to the much higher supersonic convection speed of hot jets (p. 421), which agrees with the convective-Mach-number view ([Papamoschou & Roshko 1988](../Notes/papamoschou-1988-compressible-turbulent-shear-layer.md); [Tam 1995](../Notes/tam-1995-supersonic-jet-noise.md)).

The note's parallax estimate implies wall-jet convection speeds of about 1.3a_∞ (cold) and 2.1a_∞ (hot), i.e. U_c/u_J ≈ 0.9 and 0.6. The hot value falls with heating, as in the trend of [Gojon 2019](../Notes/gojon-2019-antisymmetric-oscillation-modes-rectangular-screeching-jets.md) (p. 16).

The paper's claim that heating lowers the spectral peak St "significantly" because the shear layer is thicker (p. 421) is mostly the normalisation. In fD/a_∞ the hot peaks are at the same or higher frequency (the note's reading). This is a caution for St comparisons across temperature ratio.

Level: ≈+7 dB at the Mach-wave peak against +3.7 dB from ρ_Ju_J³ at constant η (the note's arithmetic), i.e. higher efficiency with velocity. The paper adds one new mechanism to the page's catalogue: Mach waves from a supersonic wall jet after impingement, with a source a few diameters down the plate.
